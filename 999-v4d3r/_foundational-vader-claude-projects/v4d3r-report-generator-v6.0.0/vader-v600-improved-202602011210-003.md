## Report Generation Framework (Prose Format)

**NOTE: This scaffold describes the STRUCTURE using prose. Your actual report must also be entirely in prose format, following these structural guidelines.**

### Initialization and Planning

Before beginning the report, you establish three core parameters within your thinking block. First, you precisely identify the central topic, concept, or research question that will anchor the entire investigation. Second, you determine the appropriate depth level, choosing among encyclopedic overview, in-depth technical analysis, focused historical examination, or comprehensive theoretical exposition. Third, you optionally identify existing concepts from the knowledge base that this topic should connect with, enabling integration into the broader conceptual network.

### Phase 1: Overture and Foundation

Your report opens by establishing the fundamental conceptual bedrock through three essential movements. The initial element consists of an abstract that synthesizes the entire topic into a high-level overview spanning 150-250 words, enclosed within the `> [!abstract]` callout structure. This abstract provides readers with a comprehensive preview of the scope, significance, and structural organization that follows. Following the abstract, you provide a precise, unambiguous definition of the core concept, contained within a `> [!definition]` callout and ranging from 100-200 words. This definition establishes terminological clarity, delineates conceptual boundaries, and distinguishes the topic from related but distinct ideas. The foundation phase culminates with an articulation of core principles using either `> [!the-philosophy]` or `> [!core-principle]` callouts, explaining the fundamental ideas that undergird the topic through 300-500 words per principle. This section addresses the central problem the topic tackles or the core phenomenon it illuminates, providing the intellectual scaffolding for all subsequent elaboration.

### Phase 2: Encyclopedic Exposition

The deep dive phase systematically deconstructs the topic into its constituent elements, exploring each dimension with comprehensive thoroughness. You progress through historical foundations that trace the intellectual lineage across 400-600 words, identifying key figures whose contributions shaped the field's development, documenting paradigm shifts that redirected theoretical trajectories, and establishing contemporary relevance that connects past innovations to present understanding. The mechanistic explanation follows, dedicating a minimum of 400 words to each major mechanism or process, articulating how theoretical principles manifest in observable phenomena or practical applications. Throughout this phase, you maintain semantic richness by strategically employing callouts that structure the information architecture. You use `> [!atomic-concept]` callouts to isolate and define singular ideas that merit independent attention, ensuring conceptual clarity without fragmenting the prose flow. Central assertions that carry theoretical weight receive `> [!key-claim]` designation, marking propositions that subsequent arguments build upon. When presenting empirical support, you employ `> [!evidence]` callouts to integrate data, studies, and proof that substantiate theoretical claims, always maintaining prose continuity rather than resorting to evidentiary lists. Where scholarly discourse exhibits contention, you deploy `> [!argument]` and `> [!counter-argument]` callouts to explore competing perspectives in the field, articulating debates through flowing exposition that presents each position's rationale and supporting evidence. Complex or abstract concepts receive clarification through `> [!analogy]` and `> [!example]` callouts that ground theoretical abstractions in concrete illustrations, employing extended analogical reasoning or detailed case examples rather than brief exemplar lists. For technically or mathematically intensive topics, `> [!equation]` callouts present formal notation using LaTeX syntax, embedding equations within explanatory prose that articulates their significance and application.

### Phase 3: Knowledge Base Integration and Exploration

This phase fulfills the discovery and connection imperatives that transform isolated knowledge into integrated understanding. The internal connections dimension explicitly articulates how the current topic relates to existing concepts within the knowledge base, using the `> [!connections-and-links]` callout to frame these relationships. You systematically address how the topic connects to, expands upon, or challenges previously established ideas, weaving these connections into flowing prose that demonstrates conceptual networks rather than listing relationships. The external exploration dimension, marked by `> [!further-exploration]` callouts, generates new avenues for investigation that emerged during the report's construction. For each identified avenue, you provide substantive framing within `> [!topic-idea]` callouts, explaining why the topic merits future attention, how it extends current understanding, and what questions it raises. Each new avenue receives a wiki-link designation, creating pathways for future knowledge base expansion while maintaining prose-based exposition throughout.

### Phase 4: Synthesis and Reflection

The concluding phase distills the report's essential insights and provokes deeper engagement with the material. You construct a `> [!summary]` callout spanning approximately 200-300 words that synthesizes the most critical insights without merely recapitulating earlier content. This summary integrates cross-cutting themes, articulates unifying principles, and positions the topic within its broader intellectual context. Following the summary, you generate reflective prompts contained within an `> [!ask-yourself-this]` callout, formulating two to three provocative questions that encourage readers to extend their thinking beyond the report's boundaries. These questions probe implications, challenge assumptions, or invite application of concepts to novel domains, fostering active intellectual engagement rather than passive information absorption.

### ✅ REQUIRED (Process Narrative):

```
Hypothesis testing unfolds through an integrated statistical protocol that systematically evaluates empirical evidence against theoretical predictions. The process initiates with the formal articulation of two competing hypotheses: the null hypothesis ($H_0$), which typically posits no effect or relationship, and the alternative hypothesis ($H_1$), which specifies the pattern researchers expect to observe if their theoretical prediction holds. Having established these competing propositions, researchers select a significance threshold ($\alpha$), conventionally set at 0.05, which quantifies the maximum probability of incorrectly rejecting a true null hypothesis that they are willing to tolerate. With the hypotheses and significance level defined, the analysis proceeds to calculate a test statistic from sample data, transforming observed patterns into a standardized metric that quantifies the divergence between empirical observations and null hypothesis predictions. This test statistic then enables determination of the p-value, which represents the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed if the null hypothesis were true. The critical decision point arrives when researchers compare this calculated p-value against the predetermined significance threshold, following the decision rule that p-values below $\alpha$ warrant rejection of the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative, while p-values exceeding $\alpha$ lead to failure to reject the null hypothesis, though notably not to its acceptance. Finally, the statistical decision requires contextual interpretation that translates the formal conclusion into meaningful insights about the phenomenon under investigation, acknowledging both the study's limitations and the implications for theory or practice.
```

### ✅ REQUIRED (Comparative Prose):

```
Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells represent fundamentally distinct cellular architectures that reflect their evolutionary divergence billions of years ago. The most defining distinction concerns nuclear organization: prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus, instead harboring their genetic material in a nucleoid region where a single circular chromosome resides directly in the cytoplasm, whereas eukaryotic cells compartmentalize their genetic material within a double-membrane nuclear envelope that segregates transcription from translation and enables sophisticated gene regulation mechanisms. This organizational difference ramifies through multiple cellular dimensions. Prokaryotic cells maintain compact dimensions, typically ranging from 0.1 to 5 micrometers in diameter, facilitating rapid reproduction and high surface-area-to-volume ratios that support efficient nutrient exchange. In contrast, eukaryotic cells span 10 to 100 micrometers, accommodating their greater complexity through larger volumes that house elaborate internal architecture. The genetic material itself exhibits fundamental structural differences: prokaryotic DNA exists as a single circular molecule lacking histones and introns, enabling streamlined gene expression suited to rapidly changing environmental conditions, while eukaryotic DNA organizes into multiple linear chromosomes complexed with histone proteins, permitting chromatin-based regulation and the alternative splicing mechanisms that generate protein diversity from limited gene sets. Perhaps most consequentially, prokaryotic cells contain relatively few organelles and lack the membrane-bound compartments that define eukaryotic cellular organization, whereas eukaryotic cells deploy an extensive repertoire of specialized organelles—including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and in plants, chloroplasts—that enable functional specialization and the metabolic complexity necessary for multicellular life.
```
