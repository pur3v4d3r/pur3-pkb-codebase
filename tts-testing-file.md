Achievement Goal Theory is one of the most consequential frameworks in modern motivation science, and its consequence rests not on the obvious observation that learners pursue different aims but on the far more interesting claim that the *purpose* a learner adopts in an achievement situation organizes nearly everything that follows — the strategies they deploy, the emotions they generate, the attributions they make, the difficulties they seek or avoid, and the trajectory of their competence development across months and years. This report traces the theory from its originating insight in the developmental work of John Nicholls and the social-cognitive research of Carol Dweck, through the bifurcation that established mastery and performance goals as qualitatively distinct orientations toward competence, through Andrew Elliot's approach-avoidance refinement that doubled the conceptual space, through the 2×2 framework that has become the dominant operationalization, and into the climates and contexts that shape which goals learners are likely to adopt in the first place. The argument has three load-bearing threads. The first is that achievement goals are not preferences for outcomes but conceptions of what competence *is* — a claim that distinguishes the framework from goal-setting traditions that take the meaning of success as given. The second is that the mastery-performance distinction has proven analytically robust across three decades because it tracks a genuine architectural difference in how the cognitive system handles errors, effort, and other people's performance. The third is that the framework's most important contribution to educational practice is not its taxonomy of goals but its account of the perceived classroom environments — the [[mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate]] distinction — that systematically channel learners toward one orientation or the other. The report closes by examining the framework's tensions with adjacent theories, the productive disagreements at its current frontier, and the cross-domain transfer of its central insight to expertise development, organizational learning, and the design of personal knowledge environments.
