#### 2. [DEFINITION] Writing to Learn (Educational Psychology — Emig, Klein, Applebee) *(Line 163)*

> [!definition] Writing to Learn (Educational Psychology — Emig, Klein, Applebee)
> A pedagogical and research tradition, originating in Emig's 1977 landmark paper "Writing as a Mode of Learning," that positions writing not merely as a communication tool but as a uniquely powerful instrument for developing, clarifying, and consolidating understanding. The core claim — supported by substantial empirical evidence accumulated across the following five decades — is that the cognitive demands of writing (selecting information, organizing it into coherent language, generating transitions and explanations) force the kind of deep processing that passive reading or listening cannot. Boundary condition: not all writing is equally effective for learning. Copying or transcribing does not engage the mechanisms that make writing powerful. Only generative, meaning-making writing — writing that requires the writer to do cognitive work — activates the learning mechanisms the tradition identifies.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Generative Learning Theory (Cognitive Psychology — Wittrock, 1974, 1990) *(Line 166)*

> [!definition] Generative Learning Theory (Cognitive Psychology — Wittrock, 1974, 1990)
> Merlin Wittrock's model positing that learning occurs through the active generation of relationships between new information and prior knowledge, and between different elements of new information. The learner's task is not to receive information but to generate connections — analogies, summaries, questions, inferences — that integrate new material into existing cognitive structures. Wittrock distinguished between reproductive processing (repeating or copying information) and generative processing (actively relating new information to prior knowledge through self-generated elaborations). The empirical prediction — repeatedly confirmed — is that generative processing produces substantially superior retention and transfer compared to reproductive processing, even when both involve the same information and similar time investment. Boundary condition: generative processing requires sufficient prior knowledge to enable meaningful connection-making; in total novice conditions, highly directed support may be needed before truly generative engagement is possible.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Self-Explanation Effect (Cognitive Psychology — Chi, VanLehn, de Leeuw, 1989–1994) *(Line 169)*

> [!definition] Self-Explanation Effect (Cognitive Psychology — Chi, VanLehn, de Leeuw, 1989–1994)
> The robust empirical finding that learners who spontaneously explain material to themselves while studying — articulating not just what is stated but why it follows and how it connects — achieve substantially better learning outcomes than learners who passively read the same material. Michelene Chi and colleagues identified self-explanation as a specific cognitive operation: monitoring the developing mental model for coherence gaps, generating inferences to fill those gaps, and revising the mental model accordingly. The effect is large (often Cohen's d > 0.5 in controlled studies), generalizes across domains, and predicts not only recall but transfer to novel problems. Boundary condition: the quality of self-explanation varies substantially. Effective self-explanation generates genuine inferences; ineffective "self-explanation" merely paraphrases stated information without adding new understanding.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Elaborative Interrogation (Cognitive Psychology — Pressley, Woloshyn, McDaniel, 1992–2000) *(Line 172)*

> [!definition] Elaborative Interrogation (Cognitive Psychology — Pressley, Woloshyn, McDaniel, 1992–2000)
> A learning strategy in which the learner generates WHY explanations for facts or concepts being studied — asking and answering the question "why is this true?" or "why does this make sense?" — rather than passively reading or rereading. The research consistently shows that elaborative interrogation improves both recall and comprehension relative to passive study, with particularly strong effects for material that the learner has some prior knowledge to connect it to. The cognitive mechanism is activation of causal reasoning and connection to existing knowledge networks. Boundary condition: elaborative interrogation is most effective when learners have sufficient prior knowledge to generate plausible causal explanations; with very unfamiliar material, the effect is weaker because the learner cannot generate meaningful causal connections.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] ICAP Framework (Educational Psychology — Chi & Wylie, 2014) *(Line 175)*

> [!definition] ICAP Framework (Educational Psychology — Chi & Wylie, 2014)
> Chi and Wylie's taxonomy of learning engagement modes, organized in descending order of learning effectiveness: **Interactive** (learners engage in dialogue or discussion that generates new ideas neither party had independently), **Constructive** (learners produce outputs that go beyond the presented information — explanations, inferences, summaries in own words), **Active** (learners manipulate material physically or procedurally without generating new ideas — highlighting, re-reading), and **Passive** (learners receive information without external activity — listening, watching). The ICAP hypothesis holds that Constructive > Active > Passive in learning effectiveness, with Interactive providing additional benefits through social knowledge co-construction. Crucially, the distinction between Active and Constructive is not about effort or time — it is about whether the learner generates new mental content or merely processes existing content.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Schema Construction (Cognitive Psychology — Bartlett, Rumelhart, Anderson, Norman) *(Line 178)*

> [!definition] Schema Construction (Cognitive Psychology — Bartlett, Rumelhart, Anderson, Norman)
> The process by which new information is integrated into existing cognitive structures ([[schemas]]) through two complementary operations: assimilation (fitting new information into existing schemas) and accommodation (revising existing schemas to incorporate information that does not fit). Schema construction is not merely additive — it reorganizes existing knowledge as it integrates new knowledge. This is why understanding is qualitatively different from information possession: genuine understanding means that the new knowledge has been structurally integrated into existing schemas, enabling inference, application, and transfer. Established in [[Report 03: Constructing Understanding]], schema construction is the cognitive architecture that Writing to Learn, Self-Explanation, and Elaborative Interrogation all, in different ways, facilitate.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Constructivism (Educational Philosophy — Piaget, Vygotsky, Dewey) *(Line 181)*

> [!definition] Constructivism (Educational Philosophy — Piaget, Vygotsky, Dewey)
> The family of epistemological and educational theories holding that knowledge is not transmitted from teacher (or text) to learner but actively constructed by the learner through engagement with experience, material, and other minds. Piaget's [[Constructivism]] emphasizes individual cognitive construction through the assimilation-accommodation cycle. Vygotsky's [[Social-Constructivism|Social Constructivism]] emphasizes the role of social interaction and language in guiding cognitive construction. Dewey's [[pragmatism]] emphasizes that knowledge is constructed through purposeful engagement with genuine problems. All three traditions share the foundational claim: the learner cannot be a passive recipient of knowledge. Knowledge, by its nature, must be made — not received.

#### 9. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Wittrock Meets Vygotsky: Language as the Medium of Construction *(Line 188)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Wittrock Meets Vygotsky: Language as the Medium of Construction
> Wittrock's [[Generative-Learning-Theory|Generative Learning Theory]] and Vygotsky's [[Social-Constructivism|Social Constructivism]] approach the role of language in learning from very different angles — one through laboratory cognitive psychology, the other through developmental philosophy — and arrive at a structurally identical conclusion. For Wittrock, generating verbal elaborations (summaries, analogies, questions) is the cognitive operation through which new information becomes connected to existing schemas. For Vygotsky, language is the primary tool of thought itself — internalized social speech becomes the medium through which higher-order thinking occurs. The convergence: writing is powerful as a learning tool because it exteriorizes the internal language through which thought happens, forcing that language to become explicit, coherent, and inspectable. A note is not a record of thought that occurred before writing; it is, in large measure, the medium through which that thought occurs. This has profound implications for PKB design: the act of creating a note *is* a cognitive act, not a documentation act.

#### 13. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Mueller and Oppenheimer Findings Misread *(Line 219)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Mueller and Oppenheimer Findings Misread
> The popular narrative around Mueller and Oppenheimer's research is that handwriting is better than typing for learning. The actual finding is more subtle and more important: verbatim transcription is worse than selective, reformulated note-making — and laptops enabled verbatim transcription while the physical constraint of handwriting forced reformulation. In their own experiments, when laptop users were explicitly instructed *not* to transcribe verbatim, the performance gap narrowed substantially. The variable that mattered was not the instrument but the processing mode. This distinction is critical for PKB design: the danger is not typing. The danger is passivity. PKB tools that enable one-click web clipping, bulk article imports, or automated transcript capture are, in cognitive terms, equivalent to the laptop condition that produced the worst learning outcomes. They enable the accumulation of information without the construction of knowledge. The lesson is not "type less" but "generate more."

#### 14. [EVIDENCE] Chi et al. Self-Explanation Effect — Core Findings *(Line 231)*

> [!evidence] Chi et al. Self-Explanation Effect — Core Findings
> Across multiple studies from the 1989 original through work extending into the 2000s, Chi and colleagues found: (1) Self-explanation frequency predicts learning outcomes even after controlling for time on task, suggesting the effect is not simply about spending more time with material. (2) The quality of self-explanations — specifically their inferential density — matters more than their frequency. (3) Students who self-explain exhibit better transfer to novel problems, not just better recall of studied examples. (4) The effect generalizes from physics to biology, mathematics, and medical diagnosis. (5) When students are taught to self-explain (rather than doing it spontaneously), they show significant learning gains over uninstructed controls. The evidence strength is high: multiple replications, active research group, domain generalization, effect on both recall and transfer.

#### 15. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Elaborative Interrogation as a WHY-Engine for the PKB *(Line 241)*

> [!analytical-insight] Elaborative Interrogation as a WHY-Engine for the PKB
> The elaborative interrogation research has a subtler implication for PKB design than its standard pedagogical application suggests. Pressley's paradigm was designed for fact-learning contexts — relatively isolated propositions. But the WHY-questioning mechanism generalizes importantly: it is the cognitive operation through which facts become embedded in explanatory structures. A fact that has been WHY-questioned is no longer a free-floating proposition in memory; it has been integrated into a causal network. This is exactly the difference between a PKB note that reads "The spacing effect improves retention" and one that reads "The spacing effect improves retention because retrieval from memory is itself a reconstructive act — when we retrieve, we re-encode, and retrieval after forgetting requires more effortful reconstruction, which produces stronger re-encoding." The second note records a WHY-explanation. It is more effortful to write. It is substantially more valuable as a knowledge object.

#### 16. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] The Generation Effect Meets Constructivism: Convergence from Laboratory and Philosophy *(Line 251)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] The Generation Effect Meets Constructivism: Convergence from Laboratory and Philosophy
> The [[Generation-Effect|Generation Effect]] research tradition is laboratory cognitive psychology at its most controlled: carefully isolated variables, measured recall performance, rigorous replication. [[Constructivism]] is educational philosophy at its most theoretical: epistemological arguments about the nature of knowledge, grounded in developmental observation rather than laboratory experiment. These traditions have almost no direct scholarly dialogue. Yet they converge on the identical structural claim. The generation effect operationalizes, in measurable laboratory terms, what constructivism has argued philosophically: meaning constructed through active generation is qualitatively different from — and more durably held than — meaning received through passive transmission. The convergence is not coincidental. Both traditions are tracking the same underlying cognitive reality: that the constructive act of generation is the mechanism of learning, not merely its byproduct.

#### 17. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Heterogeneity in Writing-to-Learn Research Points to the ICAP Gradient *(Line 259)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Heterogeneity in Writing-to-Learn Research Points to the ICAP Gradient
> The most important finding in Klein's meta-analysis is not the average effect size — it is the heterogeneity. Writing-to-learn interventions vary enormously in their effectiveness, and the variance is theoretically explicable: it tracks precisely the ICAP framework's Constructive/Active distinction. Writing that requires the learner to generate content (inferences, explanations, connections, questions) shows large effects. Writing that allows the learner to reproduce content (copying, summarizing stated information, describing without analyzing) shows small or no effects. This suggests that the pedagogical recommendation "write more" is inadequate and potentially misleading. The recommendation that the evidence supports is: "generate more — write in ways that require you to produce mental content that was not explicitly present in the source." For PKB design, this means that a template prompt asking "What does this connect to?" produces more learning value than a template that records "Summary of source."

#### 21. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Cycle Has a Critical Dependency: Prior Knowledge as the Raw Material of Inference *(Line 305)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Cycle Has a Critical Dependency: Prior Knowledge as the Raw Material of Inference
> The Articulatory Construction Cycle depends on a resource that is often invisible in discussions of note-making technique: prior knowledge. Inference generation — Stage 3 — requires the learner to have something to connect new information *to*. This is why both the Elaborative Interrogation research and the Self-Explanation research show weaker effects for learners with very little prior knowledge in the domain: they cannot generate meaningful inferences because they have insufficient existing schema structure to connect to. This has a critical implication for PKB design that is typically ignored. The same note-making process will produce very different cognitive effects depending on the learner's prior knowledge. In domains where prior knowledge is thin, generative note-making needs scaffolding — specific prompts that direct the learner toward productive inference directions they might not find independently. In domains of expertise, the scaffolding should fade and the learner should be left to generate connections freely. This is the note-making analog of the [[Expertise-Reversal-Effect-—-Cognitive-Psychology-Kalyuga,-Chandler,-Tuovinen-&-S|Expertise Reversal Effect]] established in [[Report 10: Scaffolding and Fading]].

#### 22. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Elaborative Interrogation Meets the SECI Model: Making Tacit Knowledge Explicit *(Line 323)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Elaborative Interrogation Meets the SECI Model: Making Tacit Knowledge Explicit
> Nonaka's [[SECI-Model|SECI Model]] from [[Knowledge-Management|Knowledge Management]] describes four modes of knowledge creation, including "Externalization" — the conversion of tacit knowledge (embodied, intuitive, difficult to articulate) into explicit knowledge (documented, communicable, inspectable). Elaborative interrogation, as a cognitive operation, is an externalization mechanism: when you ask "why is this true?" and generate a causal explanation, you are externalizing the implicit causal understanding that underlies your pattern recognition in the domain. Expert practitioners often cannot directly state why they make the decisions they make — their expertise is tacit. The WHY-questioning process forces them to externalize that tacit causal knowledge. For PKB design, this means that elaborative interrogation is not only a learning strategy for novices acquiring new information — it is also a knowledge capture strategy for experts making their existing expertise explicit. The domain expert who asks themselves WHY they hold each belief in their domain is performing genuine knowledge externalization, not just learning reinforcement.

#### 23. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Accommodation Notes vs. Assimilation Notes — A Distinction the PKM Literature Has Not Articulated *(Line 333)*

> [!analytical-insight] Accommodation Notes vs. Assimilation Notes — A Distinction the PKM Literature Has Not Articulated
> Standard PKM advice treats all note-making as if it were the same cognitive act. But the distinction between assimilation (new info fits existing schema) and accommodation (schema must reorganize) suggests that genuinely different note-making practices are appropriate for genuinely different cognitive situations. An assimilation note should record the connection explicitly: "This new finding from X confirms/extends/applies my existing understanding of Y in the following way..." An accommodation note should record the reorganization: "I previously believed X. This conflicts with X because Y. Revising my understanding: the more accurate picture is Z, which incorporates both the old understanding and the new finding in the following structure..." Accommodation notes are cognitively more demanding to write, require greater depth of engagement with prior knowledge, and produce proportionally greater learning gain. PKB templates should create space — and provide prompts — for both types, and practitioners should learn to recognize when they are in an accommodation rather than an assimilation situation. The felt sense of surprise, confusion, or dissonance when encountering new information is a signal that accommodation is being demanded.

#### 24. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Cognitive Event Model of Note-Making *(Line 345)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Cognitive Event Model of Note-Making
> Integrating the Articulatory Construction Cycle, Chi's self-explanation mechanism, the elaborative interrogation's causal embedding function, and Vygotsky's account of writing as a cognitive tool, I propose the **Cognitive Event Model** as a unified framework for understanding what a note actually is.
> 
> In this model, a note is not primarily a storage artifact — it is a record of a cognitive event. The cognitive event is the moment of construction: the point at which comprehension monitoring revealed a gap, inference generation filled it, and schema reorganization occurred. The note that records this event has two components: the *content* (what was understood after the construction event) and the *trace* (what the construction process involved — what was confusing, what connection was made, what changed). Conventional note-making records only the content. Genuine knowledge-constructive note-making records both.
> 
> This model has a strong prediction: notes that record traces of cognitive events will be qualitatively more useful for future learning and application than notes that record only content — because the trace encodes not just the output of understanding but the process that produced it, enabling the reader to re-engage with the construction process rather than merely reviewing its product. PKB design should create structural space for trace-recording as well as content-recording. This is a novel design principle not articulated in any of the contributing disciplines but derivable from their integration.

#### 26. [BEST-PRACTICE] The Minimum Constructive Response Rule *(Line 375)*

> [!best-practice] The Minimum Constructive Response Rule
> Design every capture workflow in your PKB to require a minimum constructive response — a brief note in your own words that either (a) explains why the captured material is relevant, (b) connects it to something already in your vault, or (c) poses a question it raises for your existing understanding. Even two sentences meeting this requirement will produce substantially greater encoding than a full-text capture with no processing. The value is not in the two sentences themselves but in the cognitive work required to generate them.

#### 27. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] Progressive Summarization: Valuable but Incomplete *(Line 407)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] Progressive Summarization: Valuable but Incomplete
> Progressive summarization addresses a real problem: the raw capture of source material produces notes that are too dense and unsynthesized to be useful. By progressively condensing material to its most salient elements, progressive summarization forces some degree of selection and judgment. This constitutes Active processing in ICAP terms — the learner is doing something with the material, not merely possessing it. However, standard progressive summarization — even through its final summary layer — stops short of Constructive processing. Highlighting what is already stated, bolding what is already stated, and summarizing what is already stated are all reproductive operations. They produce more compressed copies of the source, not learner-generated connections and inferences. Progressive summarization should be supplemented with a generative layer: after completing the summary, the PKB practitioner should write, in entirely their own words and without reference to their progressive summarization, the key insight and why it matters, the most important connection to existing knowledge, and the most important question it leaves open. This generative layer is where the cognitive value of the note-making process is created. The progressive summarization provides the raw material; the generative layer is the construction event.

#### 28. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] Atomicity vs. Construction Depth: A PKM Design Tension *(Line 417)*

> [!tension-identified] Atomicity vs. Construction Depth: A PKM Design Tension
> The [[Atomic-Notes|Atomic Notes]] principle optimizes for organizational clarity and retrieval efficiency: a well-bounded note with one idea is easier to link, tag, and resurface than a sprawling multi-idea note. The cognitive science of generative processing optimizes for construction quality: a note that records the full process of schema reorganization — including what changed, why, and how it connects to existing understanding — produces substantially greater learning value but is necessarily multi-element and relational. The tension is real and has no perfect resolution. A practical heuristic: separate the construction event (the generative note-making moment, which should be as expansive as the cognitive event demands) from the archiving event (the subsequent processing into atomic notes for the knowledge graph). Do not let the organizational requirement of atomicity constrain the constructive requirement of elaboration at the moment of initial engagement. Make the rich construction note first; extract and link the atomic components afterward.

#### 31. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Articulatory Construction Principle *(Line 449)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Articulatory Construction Principle
> Integrating the mechanisms from Phase IV and the evidence from Phase III, I propose the **Articulatory Construction Principle** as this report's central synthetic contribution: *Knowledge becomes durably encoded in long-term memory, and available for transfer to new contexts, only when it has been actively articulated in a form that requires the learner to make their implicit understanding explicit.*
> 
> The principle has three components. First, *articulatory*: the mechanism requires external language — writing or speaking — not merely internal thought. Internal thought is too fluid and too susceptible to the fluency illusion to force the gap-detection and inference generation that construction requires. External articulation creates a fixed record that can be inspected, evaluated, and found wanting. Second, *constructive*: the articulation must require the learner to generate content not explicitly present in the source. Paraphrase is not sufficient; inference and connection are required. Third, *explicit*: the articulation must make the implicit explicit — it must draw out the tacit understanding, the felt sense of connection, the intuitive recognition, and force it into communicable form.
> 
> This principle has a corollary with direct PKB implications: **any note-making practice that does not require the note-maker to make their implicit understanding explicit is, from a learning standpoint, essentially valueless beyond the archival function.** This is a strong claim. It is what the integrated evidence supports.

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Articulatory Construction Principle (Cognitive Psychology / Educational Psychology — Original Synthesis) *(Line 509)*

> [!definition] Articulatory Construction Principle (Cognitive Psychology / Educational Psychology — Original Synthesis)
> The principle that knowledge becomes durably encoded and available for transfer only when actively articulated in a form requiring the learner to make their implicit understanding explicit — through inference generation, causal embedding, schema connection, or gap repair. Distinguishes genuinely constructive note-making from reproductive note-taking.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Event Model (Knowledge Management / Cognitive Psychology — Original Synthesis) *(Line 512)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Event Model (Knowledge Management / Cognitive Psychology — Original Synthesis)
> A framework holding that a note is primarily a record of a cognitive event — the moment of schema construction — rather than a storage artifact. Notes have two components: content (what was understood) and trace (the process through which that understanding was constructed, including what was confusing, what inference filled the gap, and what changed). Trace-recording notes are qualitatively more valuable as learning artifacts than content-only notes.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Constructive Processing (Educational Psychology — Chi & Wylie, 2014) *(Line 515)*

> [!definition] Constructive Processing (Educational Psychology — Chi & Wylie, 2014)
> The ICAP framework's second-highest mode of learning engagement, in which the learner produces outputs that go *beyond* the presented information — inferences, explanations in own words, connections, summaries that require selection and integration. Contrasted with Active processing (manipulating material without generating new content) and Passive processing (receiving information without activity). Constructive > Active > Passive in learning effectiveness.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Elaborative Interrogation (Cognitive Psychology — Pressley, Woloshyn, McDaniel, 1992–2000) *(Line 518)*

> [!definition] Elaborative Interrogation (Cognitive Psychology — Pressley, Woloshyn, McDaniel, 1992–2000)
> A learning strategy involving the generation of WHY explanations for studied facts or concepts — asking and answering "why is this true?" to embed information in causal structures connected to prior knowledge. Produces better recall and comprehension than passive reading, with strongest effects for learners with moderate-to-high prior knowledge in the domain.

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Generative Learning Theory (Cognitive Psychology — Wittrock, 1974, 1990) *(Line 521)*

> [!definition] Generative Learning Theory (Cognitive Psychology — Wittrock, 1974, 1990)
> Merlin Wittrock's model that learning occurs through the active generation of connections between new information and prior knowledge. Distinguishes reproductive processing (repeating stated information) from generative processing (generating new connections through summaries, analogies, inferences, questions). Generative processing produces substantially better retention and transfer.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] ICAP Framework (Educational Psychology — Chi & Wylie, 2014) *(Line 524)*

> [!definition] ICAP Framework (Educational Psychology — Chi & Wylie, 2014)
> A taxonomy of learning engagement modes ordered by learning effectiveness: Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive. The framework provides a classification system for evaluating whether a given learning activity (including note-making) is operating at a cognitively productive level.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Note-Making vs. Note-Taking (Knowledge Management — Original Distinction) *(Line 527)*

> [!definition] Note-Making vs. Note-Taking (Knowledge Management — Original Distinction)
> A distinction that the research in this report motivates but that the literature has not consistently articulated. Note-taking is primarily reproductive: copying, transcribing, or paraphrasing source content into a note. Note-making is primarily constructive: generating new content — inferences, connections, causal explanations, questions — that was not explicitly present in the source. The cognitive science strongly supports the superiority of note-making for learning purposes.

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Progressive Summarization (Knowledge Management — Forte, 2017) *(Line 530)*

> [!definition] Progressive Summarization (Knowledge Management — Forte, 2017)
> A multi-pass note processing technique involving progressive highlighting, bolding, and summarization of captured material to identify its most salient elements. Valuable as an organizational practice but stops short of Constructive processing in ICAP terms unless supplemented with a generative layer requiring the learner to produce content beyond what is stated in the source.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Schema Accommodation (Cognitive Psychology — Piaget, Rumelhart, Anderson) *(Line 533)*

> [!definition] Schema Accommodation (Cognitive Psychology — Piaget, Rumelhart, Anderson)
> The process of reorganizing existing cognitive schemas to incorporate new information that does not fit current schema structures. Contrasted with assimilation (fitting new information into existing schemas without reorganization). Accommodation produces qualitative restructuring of understanding rather than mere addition of new information; accommodation notes should record this reorganization explicitly.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Self-Explanation Effect (Cognitive Psychology — Chi, VanLehn, de Leeuw, 1989–1994) *(Line 536)*

> [!definition] Self-Explanation Effect (Cognitive Psychology — Chi, VanLehn, de Leeuw, 1989–1994)
> The robust finding that learners who generate explanations to themselves about why stated content follows, how it connects to prior knowledge, and what it implies achieve substantially better learning outcomes than learners who passively read the same material. The effect is large, generalizes across domains, and predicts transfer as well as recall.

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Writing to Learn (Educational Psychology — Emig, 1977; Klein, 1999) *(Line 539)*

> [!definition] Writing to Learn (Educational Psychology — Emig, 1977; Klein, 1999)
> The tradition and research program holding that writing is a powerful mode of learning — not merely communication — because the cognitive demands of generating coherent prose (selection, organization, elaboration) force deep processing that passive reading cannot. Effective writing-to-learn requires generative rather than reproductive writing.
