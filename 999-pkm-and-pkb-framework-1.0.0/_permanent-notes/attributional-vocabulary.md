# Attributional Vocabulary

> [!definition] **Attributional Vocabulary** *(from [[inner-speech-vocabulary-motivation-self-efficacy-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-25]])*
> **Definition:** The vocabulary used in assigning causes to academic outcomes, structured along three dimensions: *locus* (internal vs. external — "I failed" vs. "the text failed me"), *stability* (stable vs. unstable — "I always struggle" vs. "I struggled today"), and *controllability* (controllable vs. uncontrollable — "I didn't try hard enough" vs. "I can't do this"). Crucially, these dimensions are not purely cognitive constructs — they are encoded in the grammatical and lexical structure of the attributional statement itself.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the central empirical domain for the dialectical challenge. The claim under examination is whether these attributional vocabulary choices merely reflect pre-existing beliefs or actively construct the motivational consequences that follow.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> Across dozens of studies, Weiner and colleagues established that the stability dimension of attribution is most strongly predictive of expectancy change (whether the learner expects to do better or worse next time), while the locus dimension most strongly predicts emotional outcomes (pride/shame for internal attributions, gratitude/anger for external). Controllability predicts whether effort mobilization or helplessness follows. The vocabulary that encodes each dimension differs systematically: ability vocabulary (stable-internal-uncontrollable) reliably produces the most damaging motivational sequence — shame without expectancy change without effort mobilization.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> The convergent evidence across attribution research suggests that ability-referencing vocabulary ("I'm not good at this," "I don't have the mind for this kind of material," "I'm just not a strong reader") is not merely inaccurate — it activates the most motivationally destructive attributional configuration simultaneously: internal, stable, and uncontrollable. Of all vocabulary choices available to a struggling reader, ability vocabulary is uniquely catastrophic because it forecloses all three motivational pathways: it assigns shame (internal), eliminates expectancy change (stable), and removes the basis for effort mobilization (uncontrollable). No other single vocabulary pattern does all three simultaneously.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> Research on source credibility in persuasion establishes that perceived expertise and objectivity are the primary determinants of persuasive impact. Inner speech carries a credibility premium in the domain of self-assessment because we experience it as unmediated access to our own cognitive state, not as an attempt to influence us. This means that negative inner speech — "I'm struggling because I'm not intelligent enough for this material" — may have *higher* persuasive impact on self-efficacy than the same message delivered externally, because it is perceived as an objective self-report rather than a motivated evaluation. The implication is significant: correcting inner speech vocabulary matters more, not less, than correcting externally delivered feedback.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> The evidence from AGT research, taken together with the metacognitive monitoring literature, suggests a specific mechanism: mastery-oriented goal vocabulary in the [[forethought-phase]] sensitizes the monitoring system to *understanding cues* (noticing when you do not understand something, noticing when an inference does not follow), while performance-oriented goal vocabulary sensitizes the monitoring system to *completion cues* (noticing when you have finished the page, noticed that it took a long time). Monitoring sensitivity to understanding cues is, by definition, what [[metacognitive-monitoring]] is supposed to deliver — and the vocabulary choice in goal-setting appears to determine which cues the monitoring system is tuned to track.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> The cross-phase cascade analysis suggests that the self-reflection phase — where attributional vocabulary determines the causal explanation that seeds the next forethought cycle — is the highest-leverage point for vocabulary intervention. > [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> The specific lexical choices within motivational inner speech — not just the general emotional valence — are functionally significant. "I can't do this" and "I don't have the right strategy yet" may produce similar surface affect but generate categorically different attributional inferences, self-efficacy predictions, and goal constructions. Vocabulary is not packaging for pre-formed meaning; it is partially constitutive of the motivational state itself.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> A genuine tension exists in the SDT evidence base here. SDT's internalization research suggests that the movement from controlled to autonomous regulation cannot be faked — it requires actual value alignment, not vocabulary substitution. Yet the regulatory language intervention research suggests that vocabulary shifts precede and partly cause the underlying motivational state change. The resolution may lie in [[internalization]] theory itself: the mechanism of internalization involves initially adopting the language of a regulatory style before that style becomes genuinely one's own — exactly as children adopt the language of social norms before those norms are fully internalized. This suggests a bootstrapping possibility: deliberate vocabulary practice with autonomous regulatory language can initiate the internalization process rather than merely describing it. But the tension remains active: purely performative vocabulary without engaged reflection may produce a kind of motivated reasoning about one's own motivation rather than genuine regulatory shift.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> The most consequential property of the Attributional Vocabulary Loop is not that it produces bad outcomes in a single cycle — it is that it becomes increasingly self-sealing over repeated cycles. Each turn of the loop narrows the vocabulary available to the reader by providing "evidence" for the attributed cause: the reader who uses ability vocabulary and subsequently reads less effectively experiences this as confirmation that they indeed lack ability. Over time, the vocabulary repertoire for self-explanation contracts toward the attributed cause, because alternatives are systematically disconfirmed by experience. This means early vocabulary intervention is dramatically more efficient than late intervention — the loop compounds in the destructive direction just as surely as a growth mindset compounds in the constructive direction.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> One of the less obvious implications of the self-efficacy calibration mechanism is that positive ability-referencing inner speech ("I'm naturally good at this") is motivationally problematic in a different way than negative ability-referencing speech, but in the same structural family. Both encode efficacy as stable and uncontrollable. Positive ability vocabulary produces two specific failure modes: it reduces the probability of strategic effort allocation (why invest in strategy if you're naturally gifted?), and it creates fragility — when difficulty eventually arrives, as it always does in academic learning, there is no strategy-and-effort vocabulary available to interpret the experience except as evidence that the ability attribution was wrong. Dweck's research on [[growth-mindset]] demonstrates exactly this dynamic: children praised for ability (positive ability vocabulary) subsequently chose easier tasks, showed more fragility under challenge, and performed worse than children praised for effort (strategy-process vocabulary). The vocabulary of praise — delivered externally in Dweck's studies but self-delivered in real-world reading — determines whether efficacy is flexible or brittle.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> Vocabulary deficits at the superordinate level of goal construction cascade downward through the hierarchy. A reader who can only articulate superordinate goals in controlled regulatory vocabulary ("I should understand this") lacks the motivational architecture to construct specific and challenging intermediate goals, because the energy driving goal construction is controlled rather than autonomous. Autonomous regulatory vocabulary at the superordinate level — "I'm genuinely trying to understand how this argument connects to what I already know about this domain" — generates a motivational pressure toward more specific and more challenging intermediate goals. The cascade implication is that vocabulary development at the superordinate level (the regulatory register) has multiplicative effects on goal quality throughout the hierarchy.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> There is a specific and underappreciated way that the reports in this knowledge base function as Motivational Lexicon development tools. When you read a report on [[attribution-theory]] that gives you precise vocabulary for the three dimensions — locus, stability, controllability — and the vocabulary to distinguish "effort" attributions from "strategy" attributions from "ability" attributions, you are not only acquiring declarative knowledge of attribution theory. You are acquiring new vocabulary items for your Motivational Lexicon. The next time you finish a difficult reading session, "I didn't have the right strategy for that kind of argument" is now available where previously only "I didn't understand it" was. The reports are building the very inner speech vocabulary that enables better engagement with subsequent reports. This creates a genuine positive feedback loop between PKB development and SRL capability — one that compounds with each new conceptual node added to the knowledge base.

> | "I have to read this" | "I'll feel guilty if I don't" | "I'm reading this because it matters for my goal" | "I'm reading this because I genuinely want to understand this domain" |
> | "I should focus" | "I'd be embarrassed to not understand this" | "Focusing here serves my real objectives" | "Deep engagement with this is exactly what I'm committed to" |
> | "I must finish this section" | "I'll be disappointed in myself if I don't" | "Finishing this section advances what I'm actually trying to accomplish" | "Working through this fully is consistent with who I am as a learner" |
> 
> Practice instruction: Begin by identifying your natural regulatory register for different reading contexts. The target is not to jump immediately to integrated regulation — progression through identified regulation is a more realistic and sustainable pathway. The vocabulary shift from introjected to identified ("because I'll feel guilty" → "because it serves my goal") is often the most consequential single step.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> A structured inner speech template for use at the beginning, middle, and end of any significant reading session, designed to engage all three motivational registers.
> 
> **Before reading (Forethought Phase):**
> 1. *Goal vocabulary*: "What specifically do I want to understand from this reading?" (Mastery orientation; specific and challenging)
> 2. *Regulatory vocabulary*: "Why does this matter to me personally?" (Autonomous regulation activation)
> 
> **During reading (Performance Phase):**
> 3. *Monitoring vocabulary*: "Am I following the argument / understanding the concept / or do I need to re-engage?" (Comprehension-cue monitoring, not completion-cue monitoring)
> 
> **After reading (Self-Reflection Phase):**
> 4. *Attributional vocabulary*: "What explains my understanding or confusion — specifically, what approach, strategy, or background knowledge was relevant here?" (Strategy/approach attribution; avoid ability vocabulary)
> 5. *Forward vocabulary*: "What specific next step follows from this reading?" (Seeds the next forethought phase with strategy vocabulary)

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> The following template structures goal vocabulary at three hierarchical levels, integrating SDT regulatory language at the superordinate level and AGT mastery orientation at the intermediate level.
> 
> **Superordinate Goal (Why am I engaging with this domain?):**
> "I am developing understanding of [domain] because [personally meaningful reason connected to genuine values or interests]."
> *Vocabulary principle: autonomous regulation language; first-person ownership; values-connection*
> 
> **Intermediate Goal (What do I want from this reading session?):**
> "By the end of this session, I want to be able to [specific generative capability: explain, apply, critique, connect, evaluate] [specific content]."
> *Vocabulary principle: mastery orientation; specific and generative (not "understand" — "explain," "apply," "critique")*
> 
> **Subordinate Goal (How will I engage with this text?):**
> "For [specific section/argument/concept], I will [specific strategy: map the argument structure, identify the key claims and evidence, locate the tension with what I already know, construct a counterargument]."
> *Vocabulary principle: strategy vocabulary; process-focused; specific enough to generate monitoring checkpoints*
> 
> Note: The vocabulary "understand" is specifically flagged as insufficient for intermediate goal setting, because it fails to generate a monitoring checkpoint — there is no moment when you know whether you "understand" something, whereas there is a clear moment when you can or cannot "explain it in your own words" or "identify the key claims."

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> Mechanical vocabulary substitution without genuine engagement produces exactly what the SDT authenticity tension warned against: a kind of motivated self-deception that temporarily elevates mood without changing underlying motivational architecture. The tools above work when used as invitations to genuine reflection, not as scripts to perform. The criterion for genuine use: the vocabulary should surface something true that you did not previously have words for — not dress up a state you're aware of in better clothes.

## Conceptual Tensions

> [!tension] **The Appraisal-Language Tension: Which Comes First?** *(from [[inner-speech-vocabulary-motivation-self-efficacy-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-25]])*
> On one side: emotion researchers in the appraisal tradition argue that cognitive evaluations precede and generate emotional responses — suggesting that vocabulary articulates an already-completed appraisal. On the other side: the linguistic relativity literature (Lupyan, Roberson) and the work of Lisa Feldman Barrett on conceptual acts in emotion construction suggest that the verbal category applied to an ambiguous bodily and situational signal is itself what *creates* the emotional experience. The tension is productive: it suggests that the expressive/constitutive distinction is not binary…

> **Position A:** Growth-oriented attributional vocabulary ("not yet," "effort + strategy") represents an objectively superior motivational architecture that is universally applicable.
> 
> **Position B:** What counts as constructive inner speech vocabulary is culturally and contextually indexed. The specific formulations of growth mindset and attribution retraining research carry cultural presuppositions that may not translate across contexts.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The intervention evidence base is predominantly WEIRD-sample. Cross-cultural studies show some variation in the effectiveness…

> [!tension] **The Authenticity Problem in Regulatory Vocabulary** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> A genuine tension exists in the SDT evidence base here. SDT's internalization research suggests that the movement from controlled to autonomous regulation cannot be faked — it requires actual value alignment, not vocabulary substitution. Yet the regulatory language intervention research suggests that vocabulary shifts precede and partly cause the underlying motivational state change. The resolution may lie in [[internalization]] theory itself: the mechanism of internalization involves initially adopting the language of a regulatory style before that style becomes genuinely one's own — exactly as children adopt the language of social norms before those norms are fully internalized. This suggests a bootstrapping possibility: deliberate vocabulary practice with autonomous regulatory language can initiate the internalization process rather than merely describing it. But the tension remains active: purely performative vocabulary without engaged reflection may produce a kind of motivated reasoning about one's own motivation rather than genuine regulatory shift.

> [!tension] **Vocabulary Authenticity vs. Vocabulary Practice: Can You Bootstrap Autonomous Motivation?** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> **Position A (SDT orthodoxy):** Authentic autonomous motivation cannot be manufactured through vocabulary substitution. The move from introjected to identified regulation requires genuine value alignment — not performative language change. Changing vocabulary without changing underlying values produces self-deception, not internalization.
> 
> **Position B (Internalization process view):** The internalization process itself involves initially adopting the regulatory language of a more autonomous style before that style is genuinely one's own — exactly as children adopt the language of social norms before full internalization. Vocabulary practice can initiate the internalization process rather than merely describing it.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The evidence supports a conditional version of Position B: vocabulary practice produces real motivational change when accompanied by genuine reflection on personal values connection, but not when performed mechanically. The threshold between genuine and performative vocabulary practice is not clearly operationalized in the research.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** The practical tools in Phase V are designed on Position B assumptions. If Position A's stronger claim is correct, they will produce a kind of motivated self-deception rather than genuine motivational development.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** The analysis accepts the conditional Position B — vocabulary practice as an initiator of internalization rather than a substitute for it — while flagging the authenticity tension as a genuine practical risk requiring reflective engagement, not mechanical vocabulary substitution.



## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **What is the Granularity Limit?** *(from [[inner-speech-vocabulary-motivation-self-efficacy-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-25]])*
> The vocabulary granularity principle suggests that finer-grained comprehension monitoring vocabulary enables finer regulatory access. But is there a ceiling? Does very fine-grained inner speech vocabulary create cognitive load that undermines the very comprehension it is trying to monitor? This is an important practical question for designing vocabulary development programs for academic reading.

> [!open-question] **The Automaticity Threshold: When Does Vocabulary Become Infrastructure?** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> At what point does deliberate vocabulary practice become sufficiently internalized that it operates without conscious intention during reading — functioning as genuine motivational infrastructure rather than a metacognitive tax on working memory? The evidence suggests the threshold exists, but neither the timeline nor the conditions for reaching it are well-characterized. This is the most practically consequential open question for implementation of the Motivational Lexicon framework.

> [!protocol] **Protocol 1: The Forethought Vocabulary Primer (Pre-Reading)** *(from [[inner-speech-vocabulary-motivation-self-efficacy-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-25]])*
> **Context:** Use at the beginning of any academic reading session where SRL engagement matters. Based on the reconstructed account that forethought-phase vocabulary constitutes the motivational architecture of the session. Reflects reconstructed (not thesis) understanding.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Goal formulation** (transformation framing): State the session's goal using a transformation template: "By the end of this reading, I will understand [specific argument/concept/connection] well enough to [apply it / explain it / connect it to / interrogate it with]." Note: "well enough to" encodes a success criterion that is about comprehension quality, not task completion.
> 
> 2. **Difficulty anticipation** (developmental framing): Note what you expect to find difficult using temporal markers: "I haven't…

> [!protocol] **Protocol 2: The Mid-Reading Comprehension Monitoring Vocabulary System** *(from [[inner-speech-vocabulary-motivation-self-efficacy-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-25]])*
> **Context:** Use during reading of dense academic texts when comprehension monitoring is needed. Based on the vocabulary granularity principle — finer-grained vocabulary enables finer regulatory response.
> 
> **The Vocabulary Tier System:**
> 
> *Tier 1 — Full comprehension* (no regulatory response needed):
> - "I understand the argument structure and the evidence chain."
> - "This connects to [prior knowledge] in [specific way]."
> 
> *Tier 2 — Partial comprehension* (continue reading, mark for re-engagement):
> - "I understand the claim but haven't yet traced the evidence."
> - "I understand the local argument but not yet how it fits the larger structure."
> - "I've grasped the main idea but the nuance is unclear."
> 
> *Tier 3 — Comprehension gap* (pause and re-read / note / seek clarification):
> - "I haven't…

> [!protocol] **Protocol 3: The Post-Reading Attributional Reflection Template** *(from [[inner-speech-vocabulary-motivation-self-efficacy-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-25]])*
> **Context:** Use in the self-reflection phase after a reading session. Based on Weiner's attributional dimensions and the reconstructed account that attributional vocabulary in the self-reflection phase constitutes the efficacy-building or efficacy-depleting event that will carry forward to the next reading session.
> 
> **Template:**
> 
> *For comprehension successes:*
> "I understood [specific content] because [controllable internal cause: strategy deployed / effort applied / background knowledge activated]. This shows that [transferable efficacy belief: when I use this approach with similar material, I can build understanding]."
> 
> *For comprehension gaps:*
> "I didn't yet understand [specific content]. This is because [controllable-unstable cause: I haven't yet built / I need more exposure to / I…

> [!protocol] **The Motivational Vocabulary Session Protocol** *(from [[inner-speech-attribution-self-efficacy-focused-analysis-2026-03-25]])*
> **Context:** Use at the beginning and end of each significant reading session to build Motivational Lexicon through deliberate vocabulary practice.
> 
> **Before Reading (5 minutes):**
> 1. *Superordinate Goal*: Write one sentence connecting this reading to a genuine personal value or interest ("I'm reading this because..."). Avoid obligation language.
> 2. *Intermediate Goal*: Write what specific capability you want to have after reading ("By the end, I want to be able to..."). Use a generative verb (explain, apply, critique, connect — not "understand").
> 3. *Subordinate Goal*: Identify the one most challenging section and write what specific strategy you will apply to it.
> 
> **After Reading (5 minutes):**
> 4. *Monitoring review*: What did you notice about your comprehension during reading? Describe in terms of specific passages and specific gaps, not overall impressions.
> 5. *Attribution vocabulary*: Complete this sentence: "Where I struggled, the most accurate explanation is..." — use strategy, approach, or background vocabulary; consciously avoid ability vocabulary.
> 6. *Forward vocabulary*: Write one specific next step that follows from today's reading.
> 
> **Success Criteria:** The protocol is working when the before-reading goal vocabulary becomes increasingly specific and challenging, and the after-reading attribution vocabulary becomes increasingly nuanced and strategy-focused.
> 
> **Common Pitfall:** The most common failure mode is mechanical completion — writing something in each slot without genuine reflection. The criterion for genuine completion: each response should surface something true that you did not previously have words for.
