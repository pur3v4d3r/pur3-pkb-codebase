# Adaptive Inference

> [!definition] **Adaptive Inference** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> **Definition:** A post-performance self-regulatory decision, produced during the self-reflection phase's self-reaction sub-process, to modify learning approaches for future attempts based on accurate, controllable causal attributions. Distinguished from defensive inference by its willingness to implicate modifiable factors (effort, strategy choice) rather than uncontrollable factors (ability, task difficulty).
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The adaptive/defensive inference fork is identified as the cycle's primary regulatory valve — the point at which the cycle either compounds self-regulatory capability or stagnates.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Adaptive inference is present when a learner, after a suboptimal performance, identifies a specific strategy they used, evaluates why it was insufficient, and commits to a specific alternative strategy for the next attempt. Defensive inference is present when the explanation is in terms of external or stable factors without strategy modification commitment.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** Zimmerman derived the adaptive/defensive distinction from [[Attribution-Retraining]] research (Dweck, 1975; Försterling, 1985) and [[Bandura]]'s treatment of protective self-efficacy mechanisms.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Self-Reflection-Phase]], [[Attribution-Retraining]], [[Self-Efficacy]]

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> The research does not merely suggest that goal-setting helps — it identifies the specific features of effective goals: *specificity, moderate difficulty, and proximal framing*. For PKB-type reports, "understand this report" fails all three criteria. "Be able to explain the four mechanisms of schema construction and give one example of each" succeeds on all three. > [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> When a learner finds a complex academic text genuinely difficult to comprehend — as PKB-type reports frequently are — the attribution they generate in the Self-Reflection Phase is the most consequential variable in the subsequent cycle. If the difficulty is attributed to "this topic is above my level" (stable, internal, uncontrollable), the next Forethought Phase will feature reduced goal ambition, less strategic planning effort, and depressed self-efficacy. If it is attributed to "I approached this text without enough prior knowledge activated and without a specific enough comprehension goal" (unstable, controllable), the next Forethought Phase incorporates specific revisions. The exact same difficulty experience produces divergent trajectories. The evidence suggests that the *default* attribution for intellectually sophisticated adult learners who have not trained their SRL is ability-based, not strategy-based — which means attribution retraining is a non-trivial component of SRL development.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> The distinction between Task Analysis and Motivational Beliefs is not merely taxonomic — it has direct consequences for how tools and prompts should be designed. A prompt that addresses only Task Analysis ("What is your goal for this reading session?") activates the cognitive sub-system but leaves the motivational sub-system unaddressed. Conversely, a prompt that addresses only Motivational Beliefs ("How confident are you?") may surface anxiety without providing the strategic scaffolding that would resolve it. Effective Forethought tools must activate both sub-systems in sequence: first establish what the task requires, then calibrate the motivational stance toward it. The research on [[Self-Efficacy]] specifically shows that confidence beliefs track task demands — so accurate task analysis is itself a prerequisite for calibrated self-efficacy.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> A genuine intellectual tension in the strategic planning literature is the following: the strategies most beneficial for deep comprehension (elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, connecting to prior schema) require significant prior knowledge to execute. A reader with weak background knowledge in a domain cannot generate effective self-explanations because they lack the conceptual structure to detect what is missing or surprising. This means the effectiveness of Forethought-Phase strategic planning is partially bounded by the learner's current expertise level — the very thing they are trying to develop. This tension is not fully resolved in the literature, but its practical implication is that early-stage learners in a domain need more scaffolded strategies (worked examples, partial explanations, structured annotation guides) while advanced learners can deploy more generative strategies. A PKB learning system operating across multiple domains will encounter both scenarios.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> The popular self-help literature on growth mindset often misreads the self-efficacy research as prescribing *high confidence* as the target. The actual research identifies *calibrated confidence* as the functional variable. A PKB learner who regularly overestimates their comprehension of reports — the [[Fluency-Illusion|fluency illusion]] is the dominant mechanism here — will fail to engage in the effortful post-reading Self-Reflection activities that actually consolidate learning. Accurate self-efficacy, including accurate recognition of what is not yet understood, is the functional prerequisite. The design implication is that Forethought tools should prompt for *calibration reasoning* ("What specifically would tell me I have achieved this goal?") rather than confidence elevation ("I believe I can do this").

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> When learners set goals for reading, they almost universally omit the comprehension criterion — the specification of *what level of understanding* they are targeting. "Understand section 3" is not a comprehension criterion; it is a content anchor without a standard. "Be able to explain the three-tier monitoring architecture, including why it is organized hierarchically rather than in parallel, without looking at the text" is a comprehension criterion. The omission of this element means that during Self-Reflection, learners have no specific standard against which to evaluate — so they default to *phenomenological fluency* ("did the reading feel smooth?") rather than *epistemic assessment* ("can I actually reproduce the core argument?"). This is the exact mechanism through which the [[Fluency-Illusion|fluency illusion]] captures otherwise sophisticated readers.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> Because self-efficacy is built primarily from mastery experiences, the earliest cycles in a deliberate SRL practice are disproportionately important. A learner who designs overly ambitious comprehension goals in their first Forethought sessions, fails to achieve them, and then attributes failure to ability rather than to goal miscalibration, will rapidly deplete self-efficacy for the challenging reading task — potentially before the practice is established enough to generate corrective cycles. This means early Forethought Phase design should deliberately err toward *achievable specificity* rather than maximum ambition: goals that are specific enough to be evaluated but calibrated to the learner's current actual capability. The first 5-10 SRL cycles are a foundation-laying phase, not a peak performance phase, and tool design should reflect that.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> A non-obvious tension in the goal orientation research concerns intellectually sophisticated adult learners: they often have a strong implicit performance orientation even when explicitly committed to mastery learning. Having succeeded academically through demonstrated ability, they have internalized a performance framework — they have learned that being seen as smart (by themselves as much as others) is rewarding. When engaging with genuinely difficult material in a private learning context, this can manifest as avoidance of the most challenging sections (protecting self-image from the encounter with not-knowing), superficial processing that produces the *feeling* of understanding without the substance, and resistance to the explicit self-evaluation practices of the Self-Reflection Phase (which make deficits visible and thus threatening). This is the expertise trap: advanced intellectual skills combined with implicit performance orientation produces a specific failure mode. The solution is not to lecture oneself about mastery orientation but to design Forethought tools whose language structurally activates mastery framing — a point addressed in detail in Phase V.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> A genuine tension in this literature is the asymmetry between how quickly maladaptive attributional patterns develop and how slowly they reverse. Intervention studies by Dweck and Goetz (1978) and by Perry and Penner (1990) suggest that changing an established attributional pattern from ability-focused to effort/strategy-focused typically requires multiple targeted interventions across several weeks, while the maladaptive pattern can establish itself from a single high-stakes failure attributed to ability. This asymmetry implies that the Self-Reflection Phase is not symmetrically sensitive to good and bad inputs — it appears to weight negative outcomes more heavily. The question of why this asymmetry exists and whether it is adaptive or maladaptive in different contexts has not been fully resolved in the literature.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> The most analytically significant feature of the Attributional Bridge is that it is *constructed*, not *read from* the performance data. Two learners facing identical performance discrepancies are not reading the same causal signal — they are constructing different interpretations of the same data. This means the bridge is not a neutral conduit between the Performance Phase and the Forethought Phase but an active interpretive process that can systematically amplify or dampen a learner's capability. The practical implication is counterintuitive: improving performance without improving the bridge may not improve the learner's SRL trajectory, because the performance data will be fed through the same maladaptive interpretive architecture.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> The self-defeating cycle illuminates a behavioral pattern that confounds many educators: the student who "doesn't try" despite clear intelligence. The conventional interpretation is motivational deficiency — a student who doesn't care. The SRL cyclical analysis suggests a different mechanism: a student who cares deeply but who has learned that high effort followed by failure produces more threatening evidence for low ability than low effort followed by failure. By not trying, the student preserves a residual self-efficacy that full investment would put at risk. This is a rational protective strategy within a self-defeating cycle — and it explains why direct motivational encouragement ("just try harder") tends to fail with these students. The problem is not motivational deficiency but an attributional architecture that has made full investment psychologically dangerous.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> A genuine intellectual tension in the literature is whether defensive inference is always maladaptive. Defensive responses are typically framed as failures of self-regulation, but some research (Crocker & Park, 2004) suggests they may represent rational responses to environments that use performance as a signal of global self-worth rather than domain-specific competence. If a student is in an evaluative environment where poor performance genuinely threatens social standing, relationship quality, or future opportunities, defensive self-protection may be the psychologically optimal response. The tension between the individual-focused adaptive inference model and the environmental determinants of self-protection has not been adequately integrated in the SRL literature.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> The SRL cycle is not only as strong as its weakest phase — it is as *precise* as its least precise monitoring. A learner who monitors only pass/fail on learning tasks has only pass/fail data available for attribution. This means they can only attribute to coarse causes (knew the material / didn't know it) and can only draw coarse adaptive inferences (study more / study less). Learners who monitor at the strategy and sub-strategy level have access to causal data that enables strategy-specific attribution and strategy-specific adaptive inference. This implies that metacognitive monitoring training — a component of the Performance Phase — has direct consequences for Self-Reflection Phase quality, and therefore for the entire cycle's adaptivity. The investment in monitoring precision is a cycle-level investment, not merely a performance-phase one.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> The role of conditional metacognitive knowledge in enabling adaptive attribution reveals a function of strategy education that is typically undervalued: it is not merely performance-enhancing, it is *motivationally protective*. A learner who knows multiple strategies and understands their conditions of application has a rich set of controllable causal attributions available when performance fails. This causal vocabulary insulates self-efficacy from ability-attribution damage. The practical implication is that strategy education in early learning is not only a performance investment — it is an investment in the resilience of the Self-Reflection Phase across all subsequent cycles. The learner who knows only one strategy has only one dimension of attributional latitude.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> The structural analogues across athletic coaching, organizational learning, and clinical psychology reveal a universal pattern: wherever performance cycles through a human interpretation layer before feeding back into the beliefs that govern the next cycle, the same structural vulnerabilities apply. All these systems have an Attributional Bridge. All are subject to the asymmetric ratchet. All are more effectively intervened at the interpretive layer than at the execution layer. This is not a coincidence — it follows from the shared structure of cyclic systems in which beliefs are both inputs to and outputs of performance. The specific content of these systems differs; the structural architecture is the same.










































## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> An SRL intervention that begins by teaching self-reflection quality — specifically, training learners to make specific, strategy-focused causal attributions after performance outcomes — creates the conditions under which newly acquired strategy knowledge can be incorporated adaptively. Contrast this with the standard sequence (teach strategies → practice → debrief). The standard sequence teaches content for the forethought phase but trusts that learners will naturally engage in adaptive…

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> Feedback designed to close the SRL cycle adaptively should include three components: (1) *performance information* — what the learner actually did relative to the reference standard (supporting self-evaluation); (2) *attribution information* — a specific causal account of the performance outcome in terms of controllable strategy use (supporting accurate self-judgment); and (3) *adaptive inference scaffolding* — a specific recommendation for how to modify the approach for the next attempt…

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> Zimmerman's model was developed and validated primarily with discrete, bounded tasks (writing, mathematics, dart throwing, music practice). Its application to extended, complex, open-ended learning (learning a programming language, developing professional expertise) involves temporal assumptions that have not been adequately tested. The self-reflection phase's adaptive inference output may have different properties at different timescales: immediate inferences after a 30-minute study session…

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> The most powerful structural feature of an effective SRL loop is a direct, friction-free handoff mechanism from Self-Reflection to the next Forethought Phase. In practice, this means the Self-Reflection template includes a field specifically titled "Adjustments for Next Forethought" that captures the strategic inference in concrete, actionable language ("Next session: activate prior schema before reading by writing a brief recall of what I know about [topic] first," not "be more prepared"). This field becomes the input to a corresponding field in the next session's Forethought template. The loop is physically closed in the document architecture.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> Before finalizing any Forethought or Self-Reflection prompt, apply this checklist:
> - Is the goal framed as understanding to be developed (mastery) rather than performance to be demonstrated?
> - Does the difficulty prompt invite curiosity ("What is making this difficult?") rather than judgment ("What went wrong?")?
> - Does the attribution prompt require a specific strategy-level explanation before allowing global conclusions?
> - Does the self-evaluation standard reference the learner's own prior criterion (mastery reference) rather than an ideal or external standard?
> - Does the adaptive inference capture specific, controllable adjustments rather than global intentions?
> - Is the overall tone that of a knowledgeable collaborator (your own best future self) rather than an examiner?

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> To support adaptive Self-Reflection Phase processing: (1) Provide feedback at the strategy level, not only the outcome level. (2) Make personal progress visible as a primary metric, with normative comparison either absent or secondary. (3) Ensure learners have a strategy vocabulary (conditional metacognitive knowledge) that provides controllable causal attributions when performance fails. (4) Frame challenges explicitly as strategy problems, not ability tests — the framing of the task affects the attributional architecture that learners bring to it. (5) Build in reflection prompts that ask *why* performance happened, not merely *what* happened — directing learners toward causal attribution rather than outcome description.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> When reviewing previous notes, the attributional quality of the review determines its value. Noting that a concept is now confusing ("I don't understand this anymore") is outcome-level data. Asking *why* it is confusing — was the note too shallow to encode the causal structure? Was I in an encoding context that prioritized coverage over depth? Was the concept genuinely not well understood when I wrote it? — is strategy-level attribution. The PKB review that asks "what does this gap reveal about my learning strategy?" rather than "how well did I understand this?" will generate adaptive inferences for future note-taking and review practice.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> High test anxiety and performance anxiety can systematically impair the Self-Reflection Phase by biasing attribution toward ability causes and reducing the cognitive flexibility required for strategy-level reattribution. This means that interventions targeting the Attributional Bridge — teaching more adaptive attributions, building conditional metacognitive knowledge — may be less effective in high-anxiety learners unless the anxiety is addressed as a co-occurring intervention target. The implication for PKB practice is that high-stakes review conditions (studying before an exam) may produce less adaptive self-reflection than lower-stakes review conditions, which argues for building reflective practice into lower-stakes contexts where the Attributional Bridge can process without anxiety interference.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[srl-forethought-selfreflection-applied-2026-03-25]])*
> Research consistently finds that learners systematically overestimate comprehension when their evaluative evidence is familiarity-based. This is not a character flaw — it is a natural consequence of how recognition memory works and how fluent processing feels subjectively. The structural fix is replacing familiarity-based evidence with retrieval-based evidence at every self-evaluation step. The Self-Reflection Protocol template in Part V operationalises this.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[srl-forethought-selfreflection-applied-2026-03-25]])*
> The most common failure in scaffold development is fading the scaffold too quickly, before the regulatory processes have been genuinely internalised. A reliable signal that Stage 3 readiness has been reached: the learner can accurately predict in advance which comprehension goals will be met and which will not, which strategies will be effective for a given text type, and which sections will require the most intensive processing. This level of prospective regulatory accuracy is the hallmark of internalised self-regulatory competence.

## Conceptual Tensions

> [!tension] **The Calibration vs. Motivation Tension** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> There is a genuine tension in the SRL literature about whether accurate self-efficacy calibration or high (even somewhat overcalibrated) self-efficacy better predicts SRL development. Bandura consistently argued that slightly overestimated self-efficacy is motivationally adaptive — it sustains effort in the face of setbacks. Zimmerman's model implies that *accurate* self-efficacy updating is what makes the cycle self-correcting. These positions are not incompatible, but they pull in different directions when designing feedback for struggling learners: should feedback aim to recalibrate…

> [!tension] **Accurate Calibration vs. Motivationally Optimal Self-Efficacy** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> **Position A (Accurate Calibration):** The cyclical model's adaptive functioning requires that self-efficacy beliefs be updated *accurately* based on attributed performance outcomes. Calibration accuracy — the alignment between self-efficacy and actual capability — is the hallmark of a well-functioning calibration engine.
> 
> **Position B (Motivationally Optimal Overestimation):** Bandura consistently argued that slightly optimistic self-efficacy — somewhat higher than objective performance data would warrant — is motivationally adaptive. It sustains effort in the face of setbacks without…

> [!tension] **The Knowledge Prerequisite Tension in Strategic Planning** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> A genuine intellectual tension in the strategic planning literature is the following: the strategies most beneficial for deep comprehension (elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, connecting to prior schema) require significant prior knowledge to execute. A reader with weak background knowledge in a domain cannot generate effective self-explanations because they lack the conceptual structure to detect what is missing or surprising. This means the effectiveness of Forethought-Phase strategic planning is partially bounded by the learner's current expertise level — the very thing they are trying to develop. This tension is not fully resolved in the literature, but its practical implication is that early-stage learners in a domain need more scaffolded strategies (worked examples, partial explanations, structured annotation guides) while advanced learners can deploy more generative strategies. A PKB learning system operating across multiple domains will encounter both scenarios.

> [!tension] **The Expertise Trap — Performance Orientation in Advanced Learners** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> A non-obvious tension in the goal orientation research concerns intellectually sophisticated adult learners: they often have a strong implicit performance orientation even when explicitly committed to mastery learning. Having succeeded academically through demonstrated ability, they have internalized a performance framework — they have learned that being seen as smart (by themselves as much as others) is rewarding. When engaging with genuinely difficult material in a private learning context, this can manifest as avoidance of the most challenging sections (protecting self-image from the encounter with not-knowing), superficial processing that produces the *feeling* of understanding without the substance, and resistance to the explicit self-evaluation practices of the Self-Reflection Phase (which make deficits visible and thus threatening). This is the expertise trap: advanced intellectual skills combined with implicit performance orientation produces a specific failure mode. The solution is not to lecture oneself about mastery orientation but to design Forethought tools whose language structurally activates mastery framing — a point addressed in detail in Phase V.

> [!tension] **The Scaffolding Dependence vs. Internalization Tension** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Position A (Scaffolding Advocates):** External scaffolds — detailed prompts, structured templates, comprehensive protocols — are necessary during the early stages of SRL development because the cognitive and motivational demands of the Forethought and Reflection Phases exceed what a novice self-regulator can manage without support. Without scaffolding, learners revert to surface-level practices. The scaffolds should be removed gradually as the practices become habituated.
> **Position B (Autonomy Advocates):** Highly prescriptive external scaffolds risk producing scaffold dependence — the ability to execute the protocol with the tool but not internalize the underlying regulatory processes. SDT research suggests that controlling structures (even self-imposed ones) can undermine the development of autonomous self-regulation. Simpler, more open-ended prompts may produce slower initial development but better long-term internalization.
> **Current Evidence State:** Both positions have empirical support. The scaffolding literature (Vygotsky, Wood, Palincsar & Brown) supports structured support during learning; the SDT internalization literature (Deci, Ryan, Reeve) warns against controlling structures. The resolution probably involves graduated fading: detailed initial scaffolds that are progressively simplified as practice develops.
> **Why This Matters:** The tools in the appendix of this report err toward comprehensive scaffolding — appropriate for early-stage deliberate practice. The exit strategy from scaffolding must be planned.
> **This Report's Stance:** Scaffolding is appropriate and necessary for the first 20-40 SRL cycles; thereafter, progressive simplification of tools while retaining the core structural elements (goal + criterion + strategy + attribution + handoff) is the recommended trajectory.

> [!tension] **The Attribution Asymmetry Problem** *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*
> A genuine tension in this literature is the asymmetry between how quickly maladaptive attributional patterns develop and how slowly they reverse. Intervention studies by Dweck and Goetz (1978) and by Perry and Penner (1990) suggest that changing an established attributional pattern from ability-focused to effort/strategy-focused typically requires multiple targeted interventions across several weeks, while the maladaptive pattern can establish itself from a single high-stakes failure attributed to ability. The question of why this asymmetry exists and whether it is adaptive or maladaptive in different contexts has not been fully resolved in the literature.

> [!tension] **The Protective Value of Defensive Inference** *(from [[zimmerman-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-22]])*

Defensive responses are typically framed as failures of self-regulation, but some research (Crocker & Park, 2004) suggests they may represent rational responses to environments that use performance as a signal of global self-worth rather than domain-specific competence. ## Protocols & Methods

> [!protocol] **The Adaptive Cycle Closure Protocol: A Structured Self-Reflection Sequence** *(from [[zimmerman-cyclical-srl-focused-analysis-2026-03-23]])*
> **Context:** Use after any significant learning episode (a study session, a test, a performance, a meeting) where you want to ensure the cycle closes adaptively rather than degenerately or defensively.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Performance Reconstruction** (5 minutes): Without evaluation, reconstruct what you actually did during the learning episode. What strategies did you use? How much effort did you invest? What was your attention quality? (Builds self-observation data for attribution.)
> 2. **Outcome Assessment** (2 minutes): Compare your actual outcome to your goal. What was the gap, if any? Be specific: not "I didn't do well" but "I understood the main argument but couldn't recall specific evidence."
> 3. **Causal Attribution** (5 minutes): Explain the gap (or its absence) in terms of specific,…

> [!protocol] **Pre-Reading Forethought Protocol v1.0** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **STEP 1: MICRO-SURVEY (2 min)**
> Read the title, abstract/introduction summary, major headings, and conclusion callouts of the target text.
> 
> *Prompt:* "Based on my micro-survey, this text's central analytical claim or question appears to be: _______________"
> 
> *Prompt:* "The sections/phases most important for my current learning priorities are: _______________"
> 
> ---
> 
> **STEP 2: PRIOR KNOWLEDGE ACTIVATION (1 min)**
> Without looking at the text, write what you already know about the central topic.
> 
> *Prompt:* "What I already understand about [topic]: _______________"
> 
> *Prompt:* "What I'm uncertain about or what questions I have going into this: _______________"
> 
> ---
> 
> **STEP 3: GOAL SETTING — THREE-PART STRUCTURE (2 min)**
> Set your learning goal using the three-part structure:
> 
> *Content Anchor:* "The specific concept, mechanism, or argument I want to understand from this reading: _______________"
> 
> *Comprehension Criterion:* "I will know I've achieved this when I can (without looking at the text): _______________"
> [Examples: explain the mechanism in my own words / give 2 original examples / identify the implications for X / connect this to [existing PKB note] by articulating how they relate]
> 
> *Transfer Target:* "I want this understanding because: _______________"
> [Examples: it connects to / extends / resolves a question in [note] / it informs how I approach [practice] / it answers the question I had after [prior session]]
> 
> ---
> 
> **STEP 4: STRATEGIC PLANNING (1–2 min)**
> Select your reading strategy based on your schema level:
> 
> *Schema Level Check:* Is this topic schema-rich (I have significant prior knowledge) or schema-sparse (genuinely new territory)?
> 
> *If schema-rich:* Focus strategies: Elaborative interrogation during reading ("Why does this work this way?"), connection-noting to existing PKB nodes, identify tensions with prior understanding.
> 
> *If schema-sparse:* Focus strategies: Structural reading first (central claim + main support), tolerance for ambiguity in details, flag-for-follow-up rather than pressure for immediate comprehension, modest comprehension criterion.
> 
> *My strategy for this session:* _______________
> 
> ---
> 
> **STEP 5: SELF-EFFICACY CALIBRATION (1 min)**
> *Prompt:* "My confidence that I can meet the comprehension criterion I set, given the strategy I've chosen, is: ___/10"
> 
> *Calibration check:* "What specifically would need to be true for me to achieve this comprehension criterion? [Check if your confidence rating reflects the actual task demands]"
> 
> *If confidence is below 5:* Consider whether the comprehension criterion needs to be adjusted for this session (is it too ambitious for the current schema level?), or whether a preliminary reading/schema-building activity is needed first.
>

>

> [!protocol] **Attribution Retraining — First Principles Protocol** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> When you notice a global attribution ("I'm not good at this," "this material is too hard"), stop and systematically run through the following five controllable-attribution candidates before accepting the global one:
> 
> 1. **Schema gap:** "Was there prior knowledge that this text assumed I had, that I didn't have? [Y/N] If yes, what specific prerequisite reading would address this?"
> 
> 2. **Goal calibration:** "Was my comprehension criterion appropriately matched to my current schema level in this domain? [Y/N] If too ambitious, what more proximal criterion would have been achievable?"
> 
> 3. **Strategy mismatch:** "Did I use the most effective reading strategy for this text type and my schema level? [Y/N] If no, which of these would have been better: advance organizer activation / elaborative interrogation / self-explanation at section breaks / structural reading before detail?"
> 
> 4. **Attention management:** "Was my attention available for the cognitive demands this text required? [Y/N] If no, what environmental or scheduling adjustment would address this?"
> 
> 5. **Time pressure:** "Did I attempt too much in this session relative to the text's density? [Y/N] If yes, what is a more realistic scope for one session?"
> 
> **If after running all five you still believe the most accurate attribution is a global one:** Make a note to return to this text after completing a lighter-touch introduction to the domain (schema-building session). The attribution may be accurate — this material genuinely exceeds current schema — but the corrective action is still specific and controllable: build the schema first.

## Concrete Examples

> [!example] **Strategic Planning for Two Report Types** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> *Report Type A (Schema-Rich):* You are reading a focused analysis of Organismic Integration Theory, having already extensively studied [[Self-Determination-Theory]] foundational reports.
> - **Pre-reading:** Brief recall of SDT sub-theories, formulate 2-3 questions OIT specifically addresses.
> - **During-reading:** Focus elaborative interrogation on how OIT extends rather than duplicates SDT. Self-explain transitions in argument. Connect new mechanisms to existing schema nodes.
> - **Note-making:** Expand existing SDT schema node, add OIT-specific mechanisms, identify tensions with prior understanding.
> 
> *Report Type B (Schema-Sparse):* You are reading a focused analysis of the Predictive Processing framework, with limited prior exposure.
> - **Pre-reading:** Survey structure, identify key terms before reading. Consider reviewing a brief introductory resource on Bayesian brain models first. Set modest comprehension criteria (orientation-level, not mechanism-level).
> - **During-reading:** Focus on structural understanding over detail. Read for the central claim and its main evidential support. Tolerate ambiguity in details; flag for follow-up.
> - **Note-making:** Create a new schema node with minimal content. Prioritize the conceptual frame over the mechanisms. Record questions for future exploration.

> [!example] **Applied Task Analysis — Example Report Header** *(from [[srl-forethought-selfreflection-applied-2026-03-25]])*
> Imagine you're about to read a report titled "Zimmerman's Cyclical SRL — A Dialectical Re-Examination." The task analysis would identify: *document type* = dialectical critique (assumes prior framework knowledge); *prerequisite check* = Zimmerman's original three-phase model, self-efficacy theory, metacognitive monitoring — all present in your PKB; *structural demand* = the text will argue for qualifications, boundary conditions, or failures of the original model — reading is evaluative and connective, not just receptive; *difficulty hotspot* = sections challenging the sufficiency of the monitoring-control loop, which is theoretically complex; *strategy implication* = slower pace on the critical sections, deliberate generation of counter-arguments during reading.
