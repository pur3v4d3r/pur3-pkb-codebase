# Initial Mass Function

> [!definition] **Initial Mass Function** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> A statistical description of the distribution of stellar masses at the time of birth within a population of newly formed stars. The IMF describes the relative number of stars born in each mass range — it does not describe the mass of any individual star but rather the probability distribution from which individual stellar masses are drawn.
> 
> **Boundary:** The IMF is a population-level description, not a prediction for individual star-forming events. It is also a birth mass function — it does not account for mass lost during stellar evolution.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The IMF represents one of the deepest regularities in star formation, encoding the combined effects of fragmentation, accretion, feedback, and environmental conditions. Understanding its origin is tantamount to understanding why the stellar population looks the way it does.
> 
> **See also:** [[stellar-evolution]], [[main-sequence-star]], [[Hertzsprung-Russell-Diagram]]

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** What is the interstellar medium?
> **A:** **Q:** What is the interstellar medium (ISM), and what are its principal phases?
**A:** The ISM is the matter and radiation occupying the space between stars within a galaxy. Its principal phases include the hot ionised medium (~10⁶ K), warm neutral/ionised medium (~8,000 K), cold neutral medium (~100 K), and cold molecular gas (~10-20 K). Stars form exclusively in the coldest, densest molecular phase.
**Source:** Section 1
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** #ISM, #star-formation, #astrophysics

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** What is the Jeans mass?
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Jeans mass, and how does it relate to star formation?
**A:** The Jeans mass is the minimum mass at which a region of gas becomes gravitationally unstable — where self-gravity overwhelms thermal pressure and collapse begins. It depends on gas temperature (lower T = lower Jeans mass) and density (higher ρ = lower Jeans mass). Molecular cloud cores have Jeans masses of a few solar masses, matching typical stellar masses.
**Source:** Section 3
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #Jeans-instability, #gravitational-collapse, #threshold

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** Why does protostellar collapse occur in two stages?
> **A:** **Q:** Why does the collapse of a protostar proceed through two distinct hydrostatic stages?
**A:** The first hydrostatic core forms when the collapsing gas becomes optically thick (trapping heat). This core is stable until ~2,000 K, when molecular hydrogen dissociates — absorbing energy, removing pressure support, and triggering a second, more rapid collapse. The second collapse produces the true protostar.
**Source:** Section 4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #protostar, #collapse, #hydrostatic-equilibrium

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** What is the angular momentum problem?
> **A:** **Q:** What is the angular momentum problem in star formation, and how is it resolved?
**A:** Conservation of angular momentum would cause collapsing gas to spin up so rapidly that centrifugal forces halt contraction before stellar densities are reached. The problem is resolved by angular momentum redistribution mechanisms: magnetic braking (transmitting angular momentum outward via field lines), formation of accretion disks, and bipolar outflows that carry angular momentum away.
**Source:** Section 3
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #angular-momentum, #accretion-disk, #outflows

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** What defines a main-sequence star?
> **A:** **Q:** What physical condition defines a star on the main sequence?
**A:** A main-sequence star is in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium: the energy generated by hydrogen fusion in the core exactly balances energy radiated from the surface, and outward pressure exactly balances inward gravity. This thermonuclear thermostat is self-regulating and sustains the star for billions of years.
**Source:** Section 5
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** #main-sequence, #nuclear-fusion, #equilibrium

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** Brown dwarf vs. star distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What distinguishes a brown dwarf from a true star?
**A:** A brown dwarf has insufficient mass (below ~0.08 solar masses) to achieve core temperatures (~10⁷ K) needed for sustained hydrogen fusion. It may burn deuterium temporarily but cannot establish the self-sustaining thermonuclear equilibrium that defines a main-sequence star. It slowly cools and fades.
**Source:** Section 5
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** #brown-dwarf, #hydrogen-burning-limit, #threshold

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** What is the Initial Mass Function?
> **A:** **Q:** What is the stellar initial mass function (IMF), and why is it significant?
**A:** The IMF is the statistical distribution of stellar masses at birth. It peaks at low masses (~0.2–0.3 M☉) and declines steeply toward higher masses. It is remarkably consistent across environments. Its origin — whether from turbulent fragmentation, competitive accretion, or feedback — is one of the deepest unsolved problems in astrophysics.
**Source:** Section 6
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #IMF, #stellar-population, #star-formation

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** Threshold Cascade vs. single ignition
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Threshold Cascade Model of stellar ignition, and how does it differ from a single-threshold view?
**A:** The Threshold Cascade Model identifies stellar ignition as a sequence of energy transitions: multiple "soft" thresholds (opacity limit, H₂ dissociation, deuterium burning) that delay but don't halt evolution, followed by one "hard" threshold (hydrogen fusion) that establishes permanent equilibrium. This contrasts with a simplified single-threshold view.
**Source:** Section 5 (original synthesis)
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #threshold-cascade, #original-synthesis, #self-organisation

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** Star formation efficiency
> **A:** **Q:** What is the typical star formation efficiency in a molecular cloud, and why is it so low?
**A:** Only a few percent of a molecular cloud's gas mass is converted into stars. The low efficiency results from feedback: protostellar outflows, ionising radiation from massive stars, stellar winds, and supernovae disrupt the cloud before most gas can collapse. This paradoxically enables continued star formation over cosmic timescales.
**Source:** Section 6
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #star-formation-efficiency, #feedback, #molecular-cloud

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[how-stars-are-created-in-the-universe-foundational-report-2026-04-17]])*
> **Q:** Role of ALMA in star formation
> **A:** **Q:** How has the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) transformed the study of star formation?
**A:** ALMA provides unprecedented angular resolution and sensitivity at sub-millimetre wavelengths, enabling astronomers to resolve individual protostellar disks (revealing planet-forming gaps), detect complex organic molecules in protostellar envelopes, and measure core mass functions in diverse environments — directly testing theories of the IMF origin.
**Source:** Section 7
**Difficulty:** Intermediate

## References

- **Shu, F. H. (1977). "Self-Similar Collapse of Isothermal Spheres and Star Formation." *The Astrophysical Journal*, 214, 488–497.**: The foundational analytical treatment of protostellar collapse, modelling the inside-out collapse of a singular isothermal sphere. This paper defined the standard framework for understanding the early stages of protostellar evolution and remains one of the most cited papers in star formation research.
- **Larson, R. B. (1981). "Turbulence and Star Formation in Molecular Clouds." *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, 194(4), 809–826.**: Established the empirical scaling relations (Larson's Laws) between size, velocity dispersion, and density in molecular clouds. These relations remain fundamental observational benchmarks for molecular cloud structure and dynamics.
- **Salpeter, E. E. (1955). "The Luminosity Function and Stellar Evolution." *The Astrophysical Journal*, 121, 161–167.**: The first empirical determination of the stellar initial mass function, demonstrating the power-law decline in the number of stars with increasing mass. The Salpeter IMF remains a foundational reference for all subsequent work on stellar mass distributions.
- **McKee, C. F., & Ostriker, E. C. (2007). "Theory of Star Formation." *Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics*, 45, 565–687.**: A comprehensive review of star formation theory, covering the interstellar medium, molecular cloud structure, gravitational collapse, protostellar evolution, the initial mass function, and feedback. An essential reference for graduate-level study of the field. Recommended sections: §2 (ISM phases), §5 (gravitational instability), §9 (IMF).
- **André, P., et al. (2014). "From Filamentary Networks to Dense Cores in Molecular Clouds: Toward a New Paradigm for Star Formation." *Protostars and Planets VI*, 27–51.**: Reviews the Herschel Space Observatory's discovery that molecular clouds are pervaded by filamentary networks, and proposes that dense cores form primarily at the intersections and along the crests of these filaments. This paper shaped the current understanding of cloud structure and core formation.
- **Kroupa, P. (2001). "On the Variation of the Initial Mass Function." *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, 322(2), 231–246.**: A critical reassessment of the stellar IMF, proposing a multi-part power-law parameterisation that has become the standard reference for the low-mass end of the mass function. Important for understanding the full shape of the IMF from brown dwarfs to massive stars.
- **Bonnell, I. A., Bate, M. R., Clarke, C. J., & Pringle, J. E. (2001). "Competitive Accretion in Embedded Stellar Clusters." *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, 323(3), 785–794.**: Introduced the competitive accretion model for massive star formation, proposing that protostars in dense clusters grow by accreting from a shared gas reservoir, with the most massive objects acquiring disproportionate shares. A foundational paper for the competitive accretion paradigm.
- **Hartmann, L. (2009). *Accretion Processes in Star Formation* (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.**: A graduate-level textbook covering the physics of accretion in the context of star formation, including disk structure, angular momentum transport, outflows, and the connection to planet formation. Recommended for readers seeking a deeper treatment of the protostellar phase.
- **Stahler, S. W., & Palla, F. (2004). *The Formation of Stars*. Wiley-VCH.**: The standard graduate textbook on star formation, covering the interstellar medium, molecular clouds, gravitational collapse, protostellar evolution, nuclear ignition, and the initial mass function. Recommended for comprehensive reference. Particularly valuable: Chapters 5–10 on protostellar physics.
- **ALMA Partnership, et al. (2015). "The 2014 ALMA Long Baseline Campaign: First Results from High Angular Resolution Observations toward the HL Tau Region." *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*, 808(1), L3.**: The landmark ALMA observation of the HL Tauri protoplanetary disk, revealing concentric rings and gaps interpreted as evidence for planet formation beginning within the first million years of star formation. A transformative image for the field.
