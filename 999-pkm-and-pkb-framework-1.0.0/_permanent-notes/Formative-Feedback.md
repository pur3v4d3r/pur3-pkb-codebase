# Formative Feedback

> [!definition] **Formative Feedback** *(from [[feedback-design-autonomy-mastery-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> Feedback provided *during* a learning episode, while the learner still has time and opportunity to act on it and close the gap between current and desired performance. Contrasted with summative feedback, which reports performance after the fact. The defining feature is not format or content but *functional timing*: it must arrive when it can still influence the course of learning.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[feedback-design-autonomy-mastery-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> Feedback is only formative insofar as the learner has time, capacity, and opportunity to act on it. The same information delivered a week after a task closes compared with a day before a revision opportunity is structurally different in its learning function, regardless of its content.

Most common in practice; highly effective when the task itself is the learning goal but ineffective when learners generalise it to their personal worth.

> Feedback that is experienced as *informational* — providing relevant performance data without implying evaluative pressure — tends to support intrinsic motivation. > Demonstrates that a brief online mindset intervention can be delivered at scale across diverse high schools and produces significant improvements in GPA for at-risk students. Important bridge between laboratory experiments and scalable educational deployment, and one of the first demonstrations of online mindset intervention effectiveness.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> Comprehensive statement of Self-Determination Theory, providing the theoretical complement to mindset theory in explaining motivated learning. Articulates the three basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) and their role in sustaining intrinsic motivation. Essential context for understanding how mindset beliefs operate within environmental conditions that support or undermine basic needs.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> The core theoretical claim is directional and causal: implicit theories of intelligence (entity vs. incremental) generate different goal orientations (performance vs. mastery), which generate different responses to challenge and failure, which produce different long-term achievement trajectories. Mindset is not merely correlated with achievement patterns — the framework proposes it is a generative cause.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> A growth mindset belief may be necessary but not sufficient for sustained motivated learning. SDT suggests that even learners who believe in malleability will disengage if environments undermine their autonomy (through excessive control), competence (through overwhelming difficulty without scaffolding), or relatedness (through social isolation or threat). Mindset beliefs operate most powerfully in environments that also meet basic psychological needs.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> Growth mindset curricula often include instruction about neuroplasticity precisely because understanding that the brain physically changes through learning provides a mechanistic, credible basis for the belief that intelligence is malleable. This is not mere inspirational framing — it is a scientific claim that can, in principle, shift a child's implicit theory by providing them with a biological model of development. Research by [[Lisa-Blackwell]], [[Kali-Trzesniewski]], and Dweck (2007) suggests that teaching the neuroscience of brain plasticity as part of mindset interventions strengthens their effects.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[feedback-design-autonomy-mastery-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[feedback-design-autonomy-mastery-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> **Misconception 1**: "Positive feedback is always motivating." False. Person-directed positive feedback is systematically demotivating in the long run, even while producing short-term positive affect. The motivational value of positive feedback depends entirely on what it is attributed to and what level it targets.
> 
> **Misconception 2**: "More feedback is better." False. Feedback density above a threshold reduces learner agency and self-monitoring development. Strategic selectivity in feedback…

> [!example] **Application** *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> Effective process praise is specific, accurate, and focused on controllable variables. Examples: "I noticed how you tried three different approaches before finding one that worked — that's the kind of strategic thinking that builds real skill." / "You asked a really good question when you were confused instead of giving up — that's a growth mindset in action." / "This draft is much stronger than your first one. What changed in your thinking between the two?" Each of these examples attributes performance to controllable processes (strategy, help-seeking, revision) rather than to fixed attributes (intelligence, talent, natural ability).

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> The single most damaging misapplication of growth mindset theory in educational practice is reducing it to a simple "effort is always good" message. Dweck and her colleagues have repeatedly clarified: growth mindset is not a celebration of effort per se. It is the belief that effective effort, combined with good strategy and willingness to learn from feedback, develops ability. Praising a student who is struggling and using poor strategies by saying "you're trying so hard, that's what matters!" may feel kind but communicates a fixed-mindset-adjacent message: the outcome (poor performance) is somehow separate from the process (effort), and effort itself is intrinsically virtuous regardless of its effectiveness.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> The gap between authentic growth mindset cultivation and "growth mindset theater" — superficial adoption of growth mindset language without structural or cultural change — is one of the most significant barriers to realizing the benefits the research suggests are possible. Growth mindset theater may, paradoxically, inoculate students against genuine mindset development by associating the concepts with perceived insincerity or institutional lip service.

> [!example] **Mechanism in Action: Course Selection** *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> A concrete illustration of this mechanism is the course selection behavior documented in Dweck's research. When students with different mindset orientations were given the choice between reviewing material they had already mastered (safe, no risk of failure) versus learning new, challenging material (risky, possibility of confusion), fixed-mindset students disproportionately chose the safe option and growth-mindset students chose the challenging option — even when both groups were told their choice was private and would not affect their grade. The behavior is driven by what difficulty means within each implicit theory, not by external incentives.

> [!methodology-and-sources] **Research Grounding: Intervention Evidence** *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> The evidence for mindset interventions is best characterized as *real but context-dependent*. The most rigorous recent evidence — large-scale RCTs with pre-registered designs — finds consistent but modest average effects, with meaningfully larger effects for students at risk and in schools with supportive cultures. This is not a story of "mindset is a magic bullet" nor of "mindset is a myth." It is a story of a real psychological mechanism operating in a context-dependent way, whose expression depends substantially on the environmental conditions in which learners are embedded.

> [!methodology-and-sources] **Research Grounding for This Report** *(from growth-mindset-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-05.md)*
> This report draws primarily on the empirical literature in educational psychology and motivation science, anchored in the research program of Carol S. Dweck and colleagues at Stanford University. The theoretical claims about implicit theories of intelligence, goal orientation, and behavioral patterns are well-supported by controlled laboratory experiments, longitudinal field studies, and — more recently — large-scale randomized controlled trials. The limitations section engages honestly with the most significant challenges to growth mindset theory, including the Sisk et al. (2018) meta-analysis and replication concerns. Claims about neuroplasticity draw on well-established neuroscience rather than contested fringe findings. The discussion of structural conditions and equity concerns reflects a genuine scientific debate within the field, not merely political considerations. Where claims go beyond what individual studies establish — particularly in the synthesis and integration sections — this report's original contribution is analytical and integrative rather than empirical, and is clearly framed as such.
