# Default Value Assignment

> [!definition] **Default Value Assignment** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> The automatic process by which unfilled schema slots are populated with typical, expected values in the absence of specific input information. Default value assignment is the source of schema-driven inference generation during comprehension and is a primary mechanism of reconstructive memory errors (when default inferences are misremembered as explicitly stated input).

## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Frederic Charles Bartlett (1886–1969)** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> British experimental psychologist who provided the founding empirical demonstration of schema-mediated memory in his landmark 1932 work *Remembering*. His "War of the Ghosts" experiments revealed systematic schema-assimilation distortions in memory for culturally unfamiliar material. Bartlett coined "effort after meaning" and "schema" (in the functional cognitive sense). His framework anticipated by decades the constructivist theories that would dominate cognitive psychology from the 1970s onward. See also: [[bartlett]], [[frederic-bartlett]].

> [!person] **Jean Piaget (1896–1980)** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> Swiss developmental psychologist whose structural-developmental theory provided the foundational account of schemas in developmental psychology. Piaget's concepts of *assimilation* (incorporating new experience into existing schemas), *accommodation* (modifying schemas to fit new experience), and *equilibration* (the self-regulatory process driving developmental change) map directly onto the accretion-restructuring distinction in cognitive schema theory. See also: [[jean-piaget]], [[Piaget]], [[assimilation]], [[accommodation]].

> [!person] **David Rumelhart (1942–2011)** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> American cognitive psychologist at UC San Diego who provided the first formal, computationally explicit account of schemas in cognitive science (1975, 1980). Rumelhart formalized the slot-default architecture, the goodness-of-fit account of schema activation, and the interactive activation model of processing. With Donald Norman, he developed the accretion-tuning-restructuring account of schema modification. Later contributed to the PDP/connectionist revolution, creating a productive tension with his own earlier symbolic schema theory. See also: [[David-Rumelhart]], [[rumelhart]].

> [!person] **Richard C. Anderson (b. 1934)** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> American educational psychologist at the University of Illinois who, more than any other researcher, established the empirical program of schema-theoretic research in reading comprehension and educational contexts. His studies on how readers' background knowledge and cultural schemas shape text comprehension provided the empirical foundation for schema theory's educational applications. His work with colleagues at the Center for the Study of Reading (1976–1990s) generated hundreds of studies directly addressing schema effects on learning. See also: [[richard-anderson]].

> [!person] **Roger Schank (b. 1946) and Robert Abelson (1928–2005)** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> American AI researcher (Schank) and cognitive psychologist (Abelson) who together developed the *script* concept — a temporally ordered schema for routine, stereotyped event sequences (restaurant script, doctor visit script, etc.). Their 1977 work *Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding* was enormously influential in both AI natural language processing and cognitive psychology, providing the most detailed formal treatment of procedural/event schemas. See also: [[schank-and-abelson]], [[roger-schank]].

## Debates

> [!debate] **Schema Specificity vs. Generality** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> How abstract should schemas be? The specificity-generality tension runs through schema theory: schemas that are highly specific predict accurately for narrow instances but do not generalize; schemas that are highly general generalize broadly but provide insufficient constraint to make any specific prediction. The question of how people acquire appropriately general schemas (abstract enough to apply to novel instances but specific enough to generate real predictions) from specific experience remains a central theoretical puzzle — relevant to debates about [[transfer-of-learning]], [[Generalization]], and the design of training conditions for transfer.

## Spaced Repetition Seeds

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is a schema?
> **A:** An organized mental framework that represents knowledge about a concept, domain, situation, event, role, or procedure. Schemas have *structure* (slots with constraints on filler values), *generativity* (they generate predictions and inferences beyond explicit input), *selectivity* (they direct attention and filter information), and *hierarchy* (they are embedded in more abstract schemas). The fundamental unit of organized knowledge in Schema Theory.

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is the difference between accretion, tuning, and restructuring?
> **A:** Three qualitatively different modes of schema modification: **Accretion** = adding new facts to existing schema slots without changing schema structure (cognitively cheap). **Tuning** = adjusting schema parameters — constraints on slot values, strengths of defaults — based on accumulated feedback (the primary mechanism of deliberate practice). **Restructuring** = creating new schemas or radically reorganizing existing schemas at the structural level (cognitively expensive, often resisted, the mechanism of conceptual change).

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: Why is human memory reconstructive rather than reproductive?
> **A:** Because what is stored is a *partial trace* organized by whatever schemas were active at encoding, not a complete recording. At retrieval, this partial trace scaffolds reconstruction using: the reactivated encoding schema, default value assignment to fill gaps, contextual plausibility inferences. The result is a plausible reconstruction influenced by knowledge, expectations, and post-encoding experience — not a playback of a stored record. This is why memories are systematically distorted toward schema-consistent default values over time.

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is the worked example effect, and what is its schema-theoretic explanation?
> **A:** The finding that novice learners consistently learn more from studying solved problems (worked examples) than from solving equivalent problems on their own. Schema-theoretic explanation: schema construction is the learning goal; worked examples provide explicit organized structure from which schemas can be induced. Unaided problem-solving for novices who lack relevant schemas produces cognitive overload (no schema to guide search) with minimal schema construction opportunity.

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What does cognitive conflict trigger in Piaget's account, and how does it map onto schema theory?
> **A:** Cognitive conflict = the state of disequilibrium produced when new experience cannot be assimilated into existing schemas. It triggers *accommodation* (Piaget) = *restructuring* (Rumelhart/Norman) — modification of the schema's structural organization to fit the new, schema-resistant experience. Schema theory's implication for education: learners with incorrect schemas (misconceptions) will not restructure from mere informational correction; instruction must create vivid, inescapable demonstrations of the existing schema's failure before scaffolding construction of the correct framework.

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is default value assignment, and why does it cause memory errors?
> **A:** Default value assignment is the automatic filling of empty schema slots with typical expected values during comprehension. It causes memory errors because: the filled default values are represented in the situational model produced during comprehension; over time, these default inferences become indistinguishable from explicitly stated information; at recall, default-value inferences are frequently misremembered as having been explicitly present in the original material (false recognition), and schema-consistent items NOT present are frequently falsely remembered as having been seen (false alarms).

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: How do Schema Theory and Cognitive Load Theory interact to explain the expertise advantage?
> **A:** CLT: expert performance is more efficient because it requires less intrinsic cognitive load. Schema Theory: expertise is constituted by well-organized, highly automated domain schemas. Integration: well-established schemas enable *chunking* — compressing many individual elements into a single schema-organized unit in working memory — dramatically increasing functional WM capacity. Schema automation reduces the cognitive load of deploying the schemas themselves. Together: expertise is high-schema richness + high-schema automation = low cognitive load for domain-typical processing.

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is the encoding specificity principle and how does Schema Theory explain it?
> **A:** Encoding specificity (Tulving & Thompson, 1973): memory retrieval is most effective when retrieval conditions reinstate the contextual conditions active at encoding. Schema-theoretic explanation: retrieval involves reinstating the schemas that organized encoded material; the organized schemas act as a retrieval scaffold for the partial memory trace. When retrieval context reinstates the encoding schemas (same physical environment, same emotional state, same conceptual context), the relevant organizational frameworks are pre-activated and provide retrieval support. Context-dependence and state-dependence of memory follow directly.

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: Why is prior knowledge the strongest predictor of new domain learning?
> **A:** Because prior domain knowledge constitutes domain *schemas*, and schemas are the cognitive prerequisite for new learning — not merely a helpful resource. Schemas: (1) provide organizational frameworks within which new information receives immediate structural homes; (2) enable efficient chunking that extends effective WM capacity; (3) generate productive inferences that fill in unstated implications; (4) direct attention toward schema-relevant (i.e., important) information; (5) create rich associative networks that provide multiple memory retrieval routes. Without relevant schemas, new information cannot be organized, meaningfully connected, or reliably retained.

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is the generativity problem for schema theory?
> **A:** The tension between schemas being simultaneously abstract enough to generalize to novel instances and specific enough to generate substantive predictions about any particular instance. Schemas that are too specific don't transfer; schemas that are too general don't constrain. The question of how people acquire appropriately calibrated schemas (right level of abstraction) from specific experience, and how the cognitive system determines which schema applies in which context, remains a key theoretical open question in Schema Theory.

> [!flashcard] **Card 11** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is the schema-activation account of reading comprehension failure?
> **A:** Reading comprehension requires that relevant schemas be activated at the time of processing (not after); without relevant schema activation, text elements cannot be organized into a coherent situation model. This explains: (1) Bransford & Johnson's finding — relevant topic schema must be given before reading, not after, to support comprehension; (2) why comprehension is domain-specific — fluent general readers fail to comprehend texts in unfamiliar domains because they lack domain schemas; (3) why background knowledge interventions (schema pre-activation) dramatically improve comprehension for learners with weak domain schemas.

> [!flashcard] **Card 12** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: How does Schema Theory explain the expert-novice difference in problem categorization?
> **A:** Experts organize domain problems by *deep structural features* (underlying principles that determine solution procedure); novices organize by *surface features* (perceptual or contextual characteristics visible without domain analysis). This difference is a schema organization difference: expert schemas are indexed by the structural principles that actually govern domain problems; novice schemas are indexed by surface-level features that are irrelevant to problem solution. Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser (1981) physics study: experts categorized "conservation of energy" problems together regardless of surface differences; novices grouped "inclined plane" problems together regardless of structural principles.

> [!flashcard] **Card 13** *(from [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03]])*
> **Q:** Q: What is the situated or dynamic schema view, and how does it challenge classical schema theory?
> **A:** The view (Barsalou; situated/embodied cognition programs) that schemas are not fixed, stored structures retrieved in their entirety but are dynamically constructed in each situation from stable underlying representational components. This challenges the classical view that schemas are stable long-term memory structures that are either activated or not. The practical implication: schema-based effects (default value assignment, schema-guided attention, etc.) are probabilistic tendencies whose strength is modulated by situational factors — goals, emotional state, contextual constraints — not fixed laws that operate identically across contexts.
