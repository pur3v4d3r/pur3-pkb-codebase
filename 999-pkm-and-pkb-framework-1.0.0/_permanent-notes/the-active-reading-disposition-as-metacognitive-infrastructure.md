# The Active Reading Disposition as Metacognitive Infrastructure

> [!definition] **The Active Reading Disposition as Metacognitive Infrastructure** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> The reconstructed understanding points to an insight that neither the reading-primary hypothesis nor its alternatives fully articulate: the most important variable in reading-based vocabulary acquisition may not be the *amount* of reading but the *stance* toward unfamiliar words during reading. A reader who maintains a metacognitive disposition of active word-noticing — who treats unfamiliar words as data rather than obstacles, who pauses at semantic uncertainty rather than tolerating it, who uses the [[encoding-specificity-principle]] to encode new words in the contexts where they were first encountered — will acquire substantially more vocabulary per hour of reading than a fluent but attention-inattentive reader who covers three times as many pages. This disposition is neither natural nor automatic; it is a learnable skill that requires metacognitive scaffolding, explicit instruction, and deliberate practice. The implication is that vocabulary-building reading programmes should spend as much time developing this metacognitive stance as they spend recommending text difficulty or reading volume.

> **A:** **Q:** What is the approximate acquisition rate for incidental vocabulary learning from a single contextual encounter in reading, according to the research literature?
**A:** Approximately 5–15% probability of acquiring a usable meaning from a single encounter (Nagy, Herman & Anderson, 1985; Swanborn & de Glopper, 1999). This rate is higher than zero but much lower than the received view implies, especially for low-frequency words where encounters are rare.
**Source:** Phase III Empirical Challenge

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 04
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Incremental Knowledge Model of word knowledge, and what does it imply about reading-based vocabulary acquisition?
**A:** The model holds that word knowledge is not binary (known/unknown) but multi-dimensional: form, meaning, collocational patterns, register, morphology, and productive availability each constitute separate, partially independent knowledge dimensions. Reading efficiently builds receptive meaning knowledge but systematically under-builds productive depth, collocational precision, and register sensitivity — dimensions requiring active use and generative processing that reading rarely demands.
**Source:** Phase II, Incremental Knowledge Model; Phase III Philosophical Challenge
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** #word-knowledge #receptive #productive #vocabulary

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 05
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Threshold-Dependency Principle, and what is the vocabulary-specific example that instantiates it?
**A:** The Threshold-Dependency Principle holds that some acquisition mechanisms are conditionalised on prior acquisition they cannot themselves efficiently provide — the mechanism works above-threshold but is inefficient below it. In vocabulary: incidental acquisition from reading works only when the reader already knows 95–98% of the running words in a text. Below this threshold, the text density of unknown words prevents the contextual inference on which reading-based acquisition depends.
**Source:** Phase IV Assumption Challenge; Phase V Original Synthesis
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Process
**Tags:** #threshold #vocabulary #acquisition #principle

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 06
> **A:** **Q:** What does the Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model prescribe, and how does it differ from the received view in the direction of the dependencies between reading and instruction?
**A:** The model assigns Channel A (deliberate instruction using frequency-ranked lists + spaced retrieval practice) to building the vocabulary threshold, and Channel B (extensive reading with active word-noticing) to consolidation and enrichment above threshold. The key difference from the received view: the model makes Channel A a *prerequisite* for Channel B's efficiency — instruction unlocks reading, rather than reading being primary and instruction supplementary.
**Source:** Phase V, Reconstructive Synthesis
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** #dual-channel-model #vocabulary #instruction #reading

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 07
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Passive Exposure Fallacy, and what is the vocabulary-reading example that illustrates it?
**A:** The Passive Exposure Fallacy is treating exposure volume as equivalent to acquisition — recommending more input without specifying the active processing requirements that make input educationally productive. In vocabulary: recommending "read more" without specifying that active word-noticing (pausing at unfamiliar words, attempting inference, following up in deliberate study) is required for reading to produce vocabulary gains. Passive reading produces comprehension experience; active-noticing reading produces vocabulary acquisition.
**Source:** Phase VI, FAR Transfer
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Application
**Tags:** #passive-exposure #vocabulary #reading #metacognition

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 08
> **A:** **Q:** What did Beck, McKeown and McCaslin (1983) find about context quality in naturally occurring text, and why does this matter for the reading-vocabulary hypothesis?
**A:** They found that a substantial proportion of natural text contexts are *misdirective* (pointing to a wrong meaning), *non-directive* (insufficient for inference), or *incomplete* (partial meaning only). Only a minority are *directive* — richly informative about the target word's meaning. This undermines the assumed contextual inference mechanism: the reading-primary hypothesis depends on context being reliably sufficient for word meaning inference, but this assumption does not hold across naturally occurring text.
**Source:** Phase III Practical Challenge
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Evidence
**Tags:** #context-quality #vocabulary #inference #reading

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 09
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between receptive and productive vocabulary knowledge, and why does reading primarily build the former?
**A:** Receptive vocabulary comprises words a reader can recognise and approximately understand in context; productive vocabulary comprises words a speaker/writer can accurately deploy in their own output. Productive vocabulary is typically 50–70% of receptive vocabulary in size and substantially shallower in depth. Reading builds receptive knowledge because the cognitive task is comprehension (processing incoming words for meaning), not production (generating words from memory). Productive knowledge requires active use, generation, and retrieval practice — cognitive operations that reading rarely demands.
**Source:** Phase II; Phase III Philosophical Challenge
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** #receptive #productive #vocabulary #word-knowledge

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[vocabulary-building-reading-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-28]])*
> **Q:** Seed 10
> **A:** **Q:** How does the active word-noticing disposition differ from standard fluent reading, and why is this distinction critical for the reconstructed model?
**A:** Fluent reading optimises for comprehension: it tolerates local ambiguity, skips processing unfamiliar words when the passage meaning is sufficient, and prioritises speed and global coherence. The active word-noticing disposition deliberately slows at unfamiliar words, attempts contextual inference, and flags items for follow-up — sacrificing some reading speed for vocabulary acquisition. The distinction is critical because the same reading volume produces dramatically different vocabulary outcomes depending on which stance the reader maintains: the reconstructed model holds that active noticing, not reading volume per se, is the key mediator of reading's vocabulary acquisition efficiency.
**Source:** Phase V Reconstructive Synthesis; Original Synthesis
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Application
**Tags:** #active-noticing #metacognition #reading #vocabulary
