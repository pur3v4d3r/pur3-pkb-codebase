# Constructive Threshold

> [!definition] **Constructive Threshold** *(from [[silent-reading-critical-analysis-focused-analysis-2026-03-21]])*
> The point in a reading episode at which inference generation shifts from elaborative (enriching) to evaluative (assessing) operations. Proposed in this analysis as a key gatekeeper for critical analysis development. Phonological load is identified as a primary factor determining whether a reader crosses this threshold.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> The moderating effect of prior knowledge on EI effectiveness is one of the most robust findings in the strategy literature. Studies consistently show:
> - Participants with domain-relevant knowledge benefit substantially more from EI than those without (Woloshyn et al., 1992; Martin & Pressley, 1991)
> - When learners are asked to elaborate on entirely novel facts (e.g., facts about a fictional African tribe, or made-up biological properties), EI effects are markedly reduced (Wood et al., 1990)
> - When prior knowledge is experimentally provided before EI (e.g., by having learners read a background passage), the EI benefit is partially restored (Pressley et al., 1992)
> - The relationship between prior knowledge and EI benefit is approximately continuous — modest prior knowledge produces modest EI benefits, rich prior knowledge produces rich EI benefits

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> The prior knowledge moderator is typically discussed as a "limitation" or "boundary condition" of EI — the implication being that EI works in the normal case but fails when knowledge is absent. This framing is misleading. The more accurate reading of the evidence is that prior knowledge is not limiting an otherwise-functional process; it is a *required input* to the process. You cannot build a situation model without integration anchors, and prior knowledge provides those anchors. The evidence does not show EI becoming *less effective* when prior knowledge is absent — it shows EI becoming *impossible* in its proper sense, because the cognitive operation EI invokes (integration into a situation model) requires materials the learner doesn't have. This is a structural dependency, not a surface moderation.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> If elaborative interrogation worked simply by increasing "depth of processing" in a generic sense, we would expect: (a) relatively uniform effects across knowledge levels, (b) relatively similar effects for good and bad "why" questions, and (c) roughly comparable advantages for recall and comprehension. The observed pattern — knowledge-dependent, question-quality-sensitive, comprehension-dominant effects — is precisely what a mechanism specifically targeting situation model construction would predict. The evidence thus provides converging support for the architectural specificity argument developed in Phase IV.

> Directly demonstrates that EI effect magnitude varies with question type — a finding that presages the Dual Register Diagnostic. Questions requiring genuine causal reasoning produce larger effects than questions that can be answered by paraphrase or text retrieval.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> The received account of EI as "deep processing" is true but architecturally underspecified. The more precise claim is that EI questions, when properly constructed, impose an answer demand that exceeds the textbase's capacity to satisfy. The reader literally cannot answer the "why" question by retrieving a propositional chain from the text alone — the causal, mechanistic, or functional explanation the question demands requires integration with prior knowledge and world-model structures. This level-asymmetry between question demand and textbase capacity is the generative mechanism, not "depth" as an abstract quantity.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> The generation advantage in EI likely has two distinct components that are typically conflated in the literature. The first is the generic [[The-Generation-Effect|Generation Effect]] — self-produced content is encoded more distinctively and persistently than passively received content. The second, and more theoretically important, is what might be called the *construction premium*: the act of building the causal connection from scratch, rather than receiving it pre-built, requires the learner to traverse the conceptual path between proposition and prior knowledge actively, reinforcing both the path and the endpoint. Provided elaborations, however accurate, allow the reader to recognize without constructing — and recognition does not activate the full situation model integration process that production does.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> A well-formed elaborative interrogation question functions as a *comprehension gateway* — a cognitive threshold operator that makes textbase-level retrieval an insufficient response. The reader who answers the question by locating a sentence is not answering the question; they are answering a different, simpler question. The actual question can only be answered by constructing a causal or functional account that integrates text propositions with prior knowledge — that is, by building a situation model.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> There are two ways elaborative interrogation can fail, and they are mechanistically distinct. The first is the Dual Register failure — the question is in Register 1 and textbase retrieval suffices; deep processing never occurs. The second is the anchor absence failure — the question is genuine Register 2 but the learner lacks the prior knowledge structures to serve as integration anchors; the question invokes a process the learner cannot execute. The first failure mode produces near-zero EI benefit. The second failure mode can produce active harm — time is spent on unsuccessful integration attempts rather than productive textbase learning, and the learner may construct distorted or confabulated situation models that interfere with subsequent accurate learning. Recognizing these as *different* failure modes is important for diagnosis and remediation.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> Most accounts of EI as a desirable difficulty (following Bjork's framework) treat the difficulty as generically productive. The more precise claim is that EI's difficulty is specifically targeted at the [[Schema-Construction]] process. The cognitive load it imposes is not distributed across reading in general — it is concentrated at the point of causal integration, where new propositional content must be connected to existing schemas. This targeted load produces targeted benefits: specifically, richer situation models and stronger schema modification, with comparatively smaller benefits for textbase recall. This specificity predicts the comprehension-dominant effect profile found in the evidence.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> For a personal knowledge base user applying elaborative interrogation during reading:
> - Formulate "why" questions as you encounter propositions, then answer them before continuing
> - Apply the Dual Register Diagnostic: *"Can I answer this from the text alone?"* — if yes, the question is not doing the work EI is designed to do; reformulate at a higher level
> - When you cannot answer a "why" question, do not immediately look it up; the failure to answer is diagnostic — it signals that your situation model is underdeveloped for this topic
> - Use EI questions as PKB note prompts: rather than recording *what* the text says, record *why* the mechanism works or *why* the finding is true — this automatically structures notes at the situation model level rather than the textbase level

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> The most common practical misconception about elaborative interrogation is treating it as synonymous with "asking questions while reading" in general. Questions like "What is this?" or "What did the author mean?" or "When did this happen?" are not EI questions in the relevant sense — they prompt textbase-level retrieval, not situation model construction. The specific format — "Why is this true?" or "Why does this happen?" — is not arbitrary. It targets the mechanistic requirement that a causal or functional account be generated. Changing the question format to "what" or "how many" transforms EI into a recall prompt with none of the situation model construction benefits.



## Conceptual Tensions

> [!tension] **The Self-Explanation Paradox** *(from [[silent-reading-critical-analysis-focused-analysis-2026-03-21]])*
> Here is a genuine tension: the self-explanation effect depends on the reader verbalizing internally — using inner speech to construct explanations. But inner speech recruits the same phonological resources that vocalization occupies during oral reading. If silent reading "frees" the phonological loop, and self-explanation re-occupies it, is the "freedom" of silent reading illusory? The resolution may be that inner speech during self-explanation is qualitatively different from articulatory rehearsal during vocalization — less constrained, more controllable, capable of supporting evaluative…

> [!tension] **Does Prior Knowledge Moderate or Gate EI Effectiveness?** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Position A (Moderator View):** Prior knowledge is a moderator variable for EI — the strategy works better with more prior knowledge, worse with less, but the underlying mechanism is the same across knowledge levels. On this view, EI should be recommended across expertise levels with the expectation of varying benefit.
> 
> **Position B (Gate View, this report):** Prior knowledge is a structural gate for EI's mechanism — situation model construction is not merely *less effective* without integration anchors, it is *not executable* in its proper sense. On this view, EI should not be recommended for learners without relevant prior knowledge because the mechanism it invokes cannot operate.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The evidence supports Position B more than Position A. Studies showing near-zero or negative EI effects in the absence of prior knowledge (Woloshyn et al., 1992) are more consistent with a gate than a moderator. However, no study has directly tested the constructive process at the level of granularity needed to definitively establish gatedness versus moderation.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** The practical implication of Position B is significantly stronger: it says EI is contraindicated for novices in a domain, not merely less efficient. This is a substantially different prescriptive conclusion than the moderator view.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** The analysis argues for Position B (the gate view) on theoretical grounds — situation model construction structurally requires integration anchors — while acknowledging that the empirical evidence could be consistent with either position.

> [!tension] **Is the Generation Advantage in EI a Generic Generation Effect or a Specific Construction Premium?** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Position A (Generic Generation Effect):** The generation advantage in EI is the same phenomenon as the generic generation effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978): any self-produced content is encoded more strongly than passively received content, for reasons of processing distinctiveness and effortful encoding.
> 
> **Position B (Construction Premium, this report):** The generation advantage in EI is specifically tied to the causal-integration process — the traversal of the conceptual path from text proposition to integration anchor. Provided elaborations allow recognition of the connection; generated elaborations require constructing it, producing a richer trace.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The two positions generate different predictions: under Position A, generation advantage should be roughly equal across question types; under Position B, generation advantage should be larger for Register 2 questions (which require genuine construction) than Register 1 questions (which do not). This prediction has not been directly tested.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** The analysis proposes Position B as the more architecturally specific account, while acknowledging it as speculative-theoretical rather than empirically established.

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 1 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is elaborative interrogation, and what is the specific question format it uses?
> 
> **A:** Elaborative interrogation is a study strategy in which the learner generates answers to "why" questions about factual material being studied — specifically, "Why is it true that [fact]?" The defining feature is the causal-explanatory demand, which distinguishes EI from other question-asking strategies that use "what" or "when" formats.

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 2 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between a textbase and a situation model? Give a concrete reading example.
> 
> **A:** A textbase is the propositional representation of what the text asserts, stripped of exact wording — it supports verbatim recall and close-paraphrase recognition. A situation model is the integrated representation of the subject matter itself, constructed by fusing textbase propositions with prior knowledge schemas — it supports inference, transfer, and novel application. Example: a reader who can recall that "photosynthesis converts light into glucose" has a textbase; one who can explain why plants use glucose as an energy carrier rather than storing ATP directly has a situation model.
>

>

> **Type:** Distinction

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 3 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Comprehension Gateway Mechanism, and what are its three structural components?
> 
> **A:** The Comprehension Gateway Mechanism is the process by which EI produces comprehension benefits: a Register 2 "why" question makes textbase-level retrieval insufficient, forcing situation model construction. Its three components are: (1) Demand-Exceeds-Textbase Structure — the question cannot be answered from the text; (2) Integration Anchor Requirement — construction requires connecting text propositions to prior knowledge schemas; (3) Construction Premium — generating the connection produces richer encoding than receiving it.
> 
> **Source:** Phase VI, Synthesis

> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> 
> **Type:** Process

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 4 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** Why does prior knowledge moderate elaborative interrogation effectiveness, and is this moderation peripheral or structural?
> 
> **A:** Prior knowledge moderation is structural, not peripheral. Situation model construction — the process EI invokes — requires prior knowledge schemas as integration anchors: points of connection to which new text propositions can be attached within a causal-relational framework. A learner without relevant prior knowledge cannot execute the gateway mechanism because they lack the integration anchors that construction requires. This is why EI effects collapse (not merely diminish) when prior knowledge is absent, and why providing background reading before EI instruction partially restores EI benefits.
> 
> **Source:** Phases III-IV
> 
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> 
> **Type:** Connection

> **Tags:** #prior-knowledge, #elaborative-interrogation, #integration-anchor, #situation-model

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 5 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Dual Register Diagnostic, and how is a Register 1 question distinguished from a Register 2 question?
> 
> **A:** The Dual Register Diagnostic classifies EI questions by their answer level: Register 1 questions can be answered by someone who knows only the text (textbase-satisfiable); Register 2 questions require world knowledge not in the text (situation model-requiring). The diagnostic test is: "Could a reader with only this text, and no other knowledge, answer this question?" If yes: Register 1. If no: Register 2. Register 1 questions do not function as comprehension gateways and produce weak EI effects.
>

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 6 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the generation advantage in EI, and why does it specifically arise in elaborative interrogation rather than from the generic generation effect alone?
> 
> **A:** The generation advantage is the finding that self-produced elaborations outperform experimenter-provided elaborations in EI. While the generic generation effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) contributes, EI's generation advantage also includes a specific construction premium: generating a causal connection requires actively traversing the conceptual path from text proposition to prior knowledge schema, reinforcing both the path and the endpoint. Provided elaborations allow recognition of a connection; generated elaborations require its construction — and the construction is the learning.
>

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 7 — Application
> **A:** **Q:** How should elaborative interrogation be integrated into PKB note-building practice, and what is the key design criterion for EI-style note prompts?
> 
> **A:** In PKB practice, EI should be applied by: (1) formulating Register 2 "why" questions as you read — ones whose answers require constructing causal connections, not locating sentences; (2) generating answers before consulting other sources; (3) writing notes at the situation model level ("X causes Y because [mechanism]") rather than the textbase level ("X causes Y"); (4) tagging unanswerable questions as prior knowledge gaps requiring background reading. The key design criterion for note prompts is the Dual Register Diagnostic: a prompt should be answerable only by constructing a causal-functional account, not by paraphrasing the source.

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 8 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Demand-Architecture Isomorphism, and what does it predict about the relationship between task design and learning depth?
> 
> **A:** The Demand-Architecture Isomorphism is the principle that the level of cognitive architecture engaged in processing is isomorphic to the level required to satisfy the task's demand structure. It predicts that: the level of understanding produced by a learning activity is determined by the minimum representational level needed to answer the task, not by the learner's subjective effort. A textbase-satisfiable question produces textbase encoding regardless of effort; a situation-model-requiring question forces situation model encoding. This implies that the most important instructional design decision is not "how difficult" but "what level of architecture does answering this demand?"
>

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 9 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does elaborative interrogation connect to the desirable difficulties framework, and what specifically is "desirable" about EI's difficulty?
> 
> **A:** EI is a desirable difficulty (Bjork, 1994) because the cognitive demand it imposes is precisely the demand of situation model construction — the load that produces durable, transferable understanding. But EI's difficulty is not generically desirable; it is specifically targeted: the question format structurally blocks the textbase shortcut and requires exactly the kind of cognitive work (integration of text propositions with prior schemas) that produces rich encoding. The difficulty is desirable because it *is* the mechanism of the benefit — not an unfortunate cost of a useful technique but the productive struggle of construction itself.

> **Q:** Seed 10 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the expertise reversal effect for EI, and why does the Comprehension Gateway Mechanism predict it?
> 
> **A:** The expertise reversal effect for EI is the finding that EI benefit diminishes as learners become expert in a domain — eventually becoming redundant or counterproductive. The Comprehension Gateway Mechanism predicts this: experts already construct situation models automatically during reading because their prior knowledge schemas are rich and activated rapidly. The gateway mechanism is no longer needed — the door is already open. Requiring experts to generate elaborations for material they already have situation models for produces redundancy effects (wasted processing resources on unnecessary elaboration), not learning benefits.

## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Fergus I. M. Craik (b. 1935) and Robert S. Lockhart (b. 1939)** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> Craik and Lockhart, working together at the University of Toronto, published the foundational [[Craik-and-Lockhart's-1972-paper|Levels of Processing paper]] in 1972, proposing that memory retention is a function of the depth of encoding rather than the structural rehearsal system postulated by Atkinson and Shiffrin. Their framework established the theoretical context within which EI research emerged and is most commonly interpreted.
> 
> **Key Works:** Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 671-684.
> 
> **Lineage:** Craik and Lockhart built on the information-processing tradition (Atkinson & Shiffrin) and provided the theoretical architecture that Pressley and colleagues later applied to EI. The LOP framework was subsequently extended and critiqued extensively; van Dijk and Kintsch's three-level model provides a more architecturally specific account of what "depth" means in text comprehension.
> 
> **Relationship to Report:** The LOP framework is the standard explanation for EI that this report critiques as insufficiently specific. Craik and Lockhart's contribution is honored — deep processing is genuinely what EI produces — but the report argues that "situation model construction" specifies the mechanism more precisely than "depth of processing" does.

> [!person] **Teun van Dijk (b. 1943) and Walter Kintsch (b. 1932)** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> Van Dijk and Kintsch, working in discourse psychology through the 1970s-1990s, developed the construction-integration model of text comprehension that introduced the critical three-level distinction (surface form, textbase, situation model). Kintsch's subsequent work refined the model extensively, making it the dominant theoretical framework in reading comprehension research.
> 
> **Key Works:** van Dijk, T. A., & Kintsch, W. (1983). *Strategies of discourse comprehension.* New York: Academic Press. Kintsch, W. (1998). *Comprehension: A paradigm for cognition.* Cambridge University Press.
> 
> **Lineage:** Van Dijk built on the text grammar tradition; Kintsch brought propositional analysis and later connectionist mechanisms into the model. Their framework is the most widely used theoretical account of reading comprehension in cognitive science.
> 
> **Relationship to Report:** The three-level model is the primary analytical framework. The situation model / textbase distinction is the architectural foundation of the Comprehension Gateway Mechanism.

> [!person] **Michael Pressley (1951–2006)** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> Pressley, working primarily at the University of Maryland and later the University at Albany, was the most productive researcher in the EI tradition and contributed more than any other individual to establishing EI as a validated learning strategy. His research program systematically explored EI's boundary conditions, the prior knowledge moderator, and the generation advantage.
> 
> **Key Works:** Pressley, M., McDaniel, M. A., Turnure, J. E., Wood, E., & Ahmad, M. (1987). Generation and precision of elaboration: Effects on intentional and incidental learning. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 13(2), 291-300.
> 
> **Lineage:** Pressley built on the LOP framework and the broader cognitive strategies research tradition (Flavell on metacognition, Palincsar and Brown on reciprocal teaching). His research is the primary empirical foundation for this report.
> 
> **Relationship to Report:** The empirical evidence base is primarily the Pressley research program. The analysis develops a mechanistic account that explains why Pressley's empirical patterns — prior knowledge moderation, generation advantage — look the way they do.

> [!person] **John Dunlosky (b. 1963 est.)** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> Dunlosky, working at Kent State University, has been the most influential synthesizer of the learning strategies literature, conducting the most comprehensive meta-analytic review (Dunlosky et al., 2013) that assessed EI's utility alongside nine other major strategies.
> 
> **Key Works:** Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4-58.
> 
> **Relationship to Report:** The Dunlosky et al. meta-analysis provides the evidence synthesis and the framework for evaluating EI's utility rating and boundary conditions.

## Protocols & Methods

> [!protocol] **EI Session Protocol for PKB Reading** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Context:** Use when reading expository texts in domains where you have moderate prior knowledge (above the constructive threshold but below expert level).
> 
> **Derived From:** Phases IV-V; Comprehension Gateway Mechanism; Dual Register Diagnostic.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Pre-reading schema activation (3-5 min):** Before reading, articulate what you already know about the topic. Identify the causal structures and explanatory frameworks you bring. This activates integration anchors.
> 2. **First reading:** Read through the passage to build a working textbase. Do not stop to generate elaborations yet — establish the propositional content first.
> 3. **EI pass:** Return to each major proposition and formulate a Register 2 "why" question. Apply the Dual Register Diagnostic: "Can I answer this from the text alone?" If yes, reformulate at a higher level.
> 4. **Answer generation:** For each Register 2 question, generate your own answer before consulting any other source. Spend at least 30-60 seconds genuinely trying to construct the causal account.
> 5. **Failure tagging:** When you cannot generate an adequate answer, tag that question explicitly — "no anchor." This is diagnostic: it tells you where your situation model is underdeveloped and what background reading is needed.
> 6. **PKB note creation:** Write notes at the situation model level, not the textbase level. Instead of "X causes Y," write "X causes Y because [causal mechanism connecting to prior framework Z]."
> 
> **Success Criteria:** You can answer novel inference questions about the material — questions not present in the text and not covered in your elaborations.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** (1) Generating Register 1 questions without noticing — apply the diagnostic consciously. (2) Looking up answers immediately after failure rather than sitting with the failure — the failure state is itself diagnostic. (3) Treating EI as an alternative to building prior knowledge — in genuinely unfamiliar domains, EI should be preceded by background reading.

> [!methodology-and-sources] **Research Grounding for This Report** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Traditions Synthesized:** This analysis integrates three research traditions: (1) the elaborative interrogation research tradition (Pressley and colleagues, 1987-1998), grounded in experimental cognitive psychology; (2) the discourse comprehension tradition (van Dijk, Kintsch), grounded in psycholinguistics and text cognition; and (3) the desirable difficulties framework (Bjork), grounded in memory and training research.
