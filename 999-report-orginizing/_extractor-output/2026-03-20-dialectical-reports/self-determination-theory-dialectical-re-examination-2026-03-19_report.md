#### 2. [STEEL-MAN] The Strongest Version of Self-Determination Theory *(Line 232)*

> [!steel-man] The Strongest Version of Self-Determination Theory
> SDT's most compelling formulation runs as follows. Human beings are, by nature, growth-oriented organisms: active, curious, and inclined toward integration of experience into an increasingly coherent self. This is not a value judgment but an observation about the natural trajectory of healthy development, akin to how seedlings orient toward light. When conditions support this natural tendency — specifically, when the environment satisfies the psychological needs for [[Autonomy-Need|autonomy]] (volition and self-authorship), [[Competence-Need|competence]] (effectiveness and mastery), and [[Relatedness-Need|relatedness]] (genuine connection) — organisms flourish: they engage more deeply, persist through difficulty, experience well-being, and develop the kind of integrated, flexible functioning that enables both personal and social flourishing. When these needs are chronically frustrated, organisms do not simply fail to flourish — they actively deteriorate: they become alienated, exhibit rigid or brittle functioning, and develop the substitutes for genuine need satisfaction (contingent self-worth, extrinsically oriented goals, defensive self-presentation) that create the appearance of motivation while its substance has been hollowed out. The theory's practical power derives from this tight link between environmental conditions, need satisfaction, and psychological outcomes: redesign the environment to support rather than control, and you predictably improve motivation quality, engagement depth, and human flourishing. This is a genuinely powerful and broadly supported framework.
> 
> **Why this version matters**: If the steel-manned version is correct, SDT provides not merely a description of motivation but a design specification for environments — schools, workplaces, therapeutic relationships, families — that predictably produce the conditions for human flourishing. The implications for institutional design would be both clear and actionable, grounded in one of the best-replicated bodies of findings in psychology.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Self-Determination Theory (SDT) *(Line 247)*

> [!definition] Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
> A macro-theory of human motivation, personality, and well-being developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan. SDT comprises six integrated mini-theories: [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]] (how external events affect intrinsic motivation), [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]] (the internalization continuum), [[Causality-Orientations-Theory]] (individual differences in motivational orientation), [[Basic-Psychological-Needs|Basic Psychological Needs Theory]] (the three needs), [[Relationship-Motivation-Theory]] (needs in close relationships), and [[Goal-Content-Theory]] (intrinsic vs. extrinsic goal contents). SDT contrasts with both behavioral theories (which focus on external rewards and punishments) and purely cognitive theories (which focus on beliefs and expectations), arguing that the *quality* of motivation — autonomous vs. controlled — is more important than motivational *quantity*.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs *(Line 250)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs
> In SDT, psychological needs are organismic — meaning they are part of human nature, not learned preferences or culturally constructed values. The three needs are [[Autonomy-Need|autonomy]] (the experience of volition; acting from one's own values rather than from external pressure or internal compulsion), [[Competence-Need|competence]] (the experience of effectiveness; mastering challenges within one's zone of development), and [[Relatedness-Need|relatedness]] (the experience of genuine connection; feeling cared for and caring for others). Critically, SDT claims that all three are *universal* — present and consequential for all humans regardless of culture, age, or individual difference — and *basic* — meaning their satisfaction is necessary (not merely beneficial) for psychological health.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] The Internalization Continuum (OIT) *(Line 253)*

> [!definition] The Internalization Continuum (OIT)
> [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]] describes how externally prompted behavior can be taken in and made progressively more self-determined. The continuum runs from [[External-Regulation|external regulation]] (compliance to obtain reward or avoid punishment) through [[Introjected-Regulation|introjected regulation]] (taking in but not accepting external pressure; ego-involvement, "should") to [[Identified-Regulation|identified regulation]] (endorsing the value of an activity even if not intrinsically enjoyable) to [[Integrated-Regulation|integrated regulation]] (full congruence of the activity's value with one's identity and other values). [[Intrinsic-Motivation|Intrinsic motivation]] — engaging because the activity is inherently interesting or enjoyable — sits outside the internalization continuum as qualitatively distinct. OIT's key claim is that internalization is a natural developmental process that tends toward integration under need-supportive conditions.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Autonomous vs. Controlled Motivation *(Line 256)*

> [!definition] Autonomous vs. Controlled Motivation
> [[Autonomous-Motivation|Autonomous motivation]] encompasses intrinsic motivation and identified/integrated regulation — any form of motivation where the person experiences themselves as the origin of their action. [[Controlled-Motivation|Controlled motivation]] encompasses external and introjected regulation — any form where action is experienced as compelled by external pressure or internal compulsion. The [[Relative-Autonomy-Index]] measures the balance between autonomous and controlled motivation. SDT's core empirical claim is that autonomous motivation consistently predicts better outcomes for complex tasks — deeper learning, greater creativity, stronger persistence, higher well-being — than controlled motivation.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] The Undermining Effect (Overjustification Effect) *(Line 259)*

> [!definition] The Undermining Effect (Overjustification Effect)
> [[Undermining-Effect|The undermining effect]] (also called the [[Overjustification-Effect|overjustification effect]]) refers to the phenomenon whereby introducing external rewards for an intrinsically motivating activity reduces subsequent intrinsic motivation. It is predicted by [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]] through the mechanism of the [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]]: when a person attributes their engagement to the reward rather than to themselves, the experience of volition is diminished, reducing intrinsic motivation. This is among SDT's most policy-consequential claims.

#### 12. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Undermining Effect is Real but Substantially More Conditional Than SDT's Standard Account Acknowledges *(Line 293)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Undermining Effect is Real but Substantially More Conditional Than SDT's Standard Account Acknowledges
> The meta-analytic record does support undermining for one specific configuration: tangible, expected, contingent rewards for tasks that participants find intrinsically interesting, administered without informational feedback. But Cameron and Pierce's (1994, 1996) competing meta-analyses — which Deci et al. sharply criticized on methodological grounds — identified conditions under which rewards do not undermine and may even enhance subsequent engagement: verbal rewards, unexpected rewards, rewards contingent on high performance rather than mere engagement, and rewards administered to participants who had low initial interest in tasks. The resolution is not that one meta-analysis was right and the other wrong. It is that undermining is a moderator-dependent phenomenon requiring specific conditions to manifest. The categorical version of the claim — "external rewards undermine intrinsic motivation" — is a simplification that the nuanced evidence does not support.

#### 13. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Cross-Cultural Replication Paradox *(Line 300)*

> [!tension-identified] The Cross-Cultural Replication Paradox
> SDT replications in Japanese, Chinese, South Korean, and Turkish contexts show that need satisfaction predicts well-being as predicted — but that autonomy satisfaction manifests through choosing to conform to social expectations, through relationally-embedded decision-making, and through what researchers call "autonomous relatedness" rather than through the independence-oriented expression characteristic of North American samples. Chirkov and colleagues interpret this as confirming SDT's universality: people in collectivist cultures experience autonomy, but through culturally appropriate forms. SDT proponents accept this reading. But it creates a genuine interpretive tension: if what counts as autonomy satisfaction is itself culturally variable — if deciding to conform to one's group can be as autonomy-satisfying as deciding to deviate from it — then the empirical content of the universality claim is doing less theoretical work than it appears. The question "do all humans have an autonomy need?" starts to look importantly different from the question "do all humans need autonomy in the same way?"

#### 14. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] Hidden Assumption 1: The Undifferentiated Undermining Assumption *(Line 305)*

> [!assumption-challenge] Hidden Assumption 1: The Undifferentiated Undermining Assumption
> **The assumption**: SDT's standard account, particularly as disseminated in popular summaries and policy documents, assumes that external rewards represent a categorical threat to intrinsic motivation for engaging activities — that the undermining effect is a reliable, general phenomenon that practitioners should design around.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: The nuanced moderator-conditional version of the claim is found in careful readings of the technical literature. The categorical version is what gets transmitted through educational policy documents, management consulting materials, and graduate course summaries. The gap between the technical claim and the disseminated claim is wide, and the technical authors are not always clear that the categorical version is an overstatement.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If the undermining effect is substantially moderator-dependent, then anti-reward policies based on the categorical claim may be eliminating useful extrinsic scaffolding in contexts where it would not undermine intrinsic motivation — and may be generating unnecessary performance anxiety in contexts where informational contingent rewards would support competence without threatening autonomy.
> 
> **Assessment**: The assumption fails. The moderator-conditional version is better supported. Undermining is real for specific conditions; it is not a categorical law. The reconstruction will need to specify when rewards threaten and when they scaffold.

#### 15. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] OIT Embeds a Teleological Directionality That Is Presented as Empirical Description *(Line 318)*

> [!analytical-insight] OIT Embeds a Teleological Directionality That Is Presented as Empirical Description
> The claim that internalization "tends toward" integration is not a description of what humans naturally do when left to themselves — it is a claim about what healthy development looks like, measured against a particular conception of human flourishing. The evidence that internalization proceeds from external toward integrated under need-supportive conditions is real. But the implicit claim that integration represents the natural endpoint — that this is where development goes when nothing impedes it — borrows from a Aristotelian-inflected conception of human nature as tending toward actualization that is philosophically assumed rather than empirically demonstrated. This becomes visible when you ask: in cultures where relational embeddedness is the dominant mode of identity, does integrated self-regulation look the same as in cultures where individual self-authorship is the developmental ideal? SDT researchers have begun addressing this question, but the theoretical architecture of OIT was built before that question was fully posed.

#### 16. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] Hidden Assumption 2: The Bounded-Self Assumption *(Line 323)*

> [!assumption-challenge] Hidden Assumption 2: The Bounded-Self Assumption
> **The assumption**: SDT's autonomy need presupposes that there is a coherent, bounded self from which authentic action originates — a self whose values can be distinguished from external pressures, whose integration is both possible and developmentally desirable.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: SDT does not make this metaphysical commitment explicit. It is expressed through the language of "authentic self," "true self vs. false self compliance," and "integrated regulation" — language that sounds psychological rather than philosophical but carries substantial commitments about the nature of selfhood.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: Buddhist psychology — a tradition with sophisticated empirical claims about human psychology — holds that the self is a construction rather than a stable entity, that reifying a coherent self and acting from "its" values is itself a form of attachment that creates suffering rather than relieving it. Phenomenological traditions (Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger) challenge the Cartesian picture of a bounded self that SDT's language implies, arguing that the self is constitutively intersubjective, never simply "one's own." Post-structuralist critiques (Foucault, Butler) argue that the subject who "authors" their own action is itself a product of discursive practices rather than a pre-given source of authentic motivation. These critiques do not collectively refute SDT's pragmatic value — but they destabilize the theoretical foundation on which the autonomy need rests.
> 
> **Assessment**: The strong version of the assumption — a fully coherent, individuated self awaiting authentic expression — does not survive philosophical scrutiny. A minimal-self version — sufficient continuity of values, preferences, and relational identity to distinguish "acting from one's own values" from "acting from pure external compulsion" — survives and is sufficient to ground SDT's empirical program. The reconstruction will operate from this minimal-self version.

#### 17. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Relatedness-Autonomy Tension in Practice Reveals an Undertheorized Tradeoff *(Line 336)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Relatedness-Autonomy Tension in Practice Reveals an Undertheorized Tradeoff
> SDT theoretical accounts typically claim that autonomy support and structure are not in tension — that a teacher can simultaneously provide clear expectations and support student self-determination. This is true in principle. In practice, teachers navigating classrooms with highly variable student self-regulatory capacity, time pressure, and social-emotional complexity frequently find that supporting one student's autonomy creates structure-deprivation anxiety in another; that encouraging initiative in students with low [[Autonomy-Orientation|autonomy orientation]] produces confusion rather than engagement; and that the moment-to-moment judgment required to calibrate between structure and autonomy support exceeds what the theoretical prescription specifies. SDT provides a destination without an adequate map for how to reach it across the full range of starting points.

#### 18. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Motivational Climate Measurement Problem *(Line 341)*

> [!tension-identified] The Motivational Climate Measurement Problem
> The most common way to assess whether an environment is autonomy-supportive is self-report: asking participants whether their teacher/manager/therapist acknowledged their perspective, provided rationale, minimized pressure, and so forth. These measures are informative but systematically conflate the teacher's actual behavior with the student's interpretive frame. A student with high [[Control-Orientation|control orientation]] may experience explicit structure as pressure and interpret it as controlling even when the teacher's intention and behavior are autonomy-supportive by independent observer ratings. This measurement entanglement means that evidence for autonomy-supportive effects may be partly capturing individual differences in how participants interpret environments rather than pure environmental effects.

#### 19. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] Hidden Assumption 3: The Modifiability Assumption *(Line 348)*

> [!assumption-challenge] Hidden Assumption 3: The Modifiability Assumption
> **The assumption**: SDT's practical recommendations implicitly assume that the relevant environments — classrooms, workplaces, therapeutic relationships — can be redesigned to be more need-supportive, and that practitioners have the autonomy and structural support to do this redesigning.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: The theory is framed at the level of psychological mechanisms, not structural conditions. It describes what environments should do without accounting for why they often cannot — understaffed classrooms, incentive structures that punish autonomy-supportive behavior, institutional constraints that make rationale-provision impractical, populations whose basic psychological need satisfaction history creates responses that autonomy-supportive interventions are not designed to address.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: When practitioners apply SDT recommendations in contexts where the structural conditions for implementation are absent, they may experience both the theory's failure to produce expected outcomes and a subtle accusation of personal inadequacy (failure to be sufficiently autonomy-supportive) that SDT's framework doesn't help them process.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption does not undermine SDT's theoretical validity, but it substantially limits the scope conditions under which its practical prescriptions can be straightforwardly applied. The theory's dissemination has not been proportionally attentive to these scope conditions.

#### 20. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] SDT's Universalism Requires a More Explicit Account of Structural Preconditions *(Line 357)*

> [!analytical-insight] SDT's Universalism Requires a More Explicit Account of Structural Preconditions
> SDT is, in one sense, a theory of psychological liberation — it argues that human nature tends toward autonomous flourishing and that much of what passes for "naturally low motivation" is a predictable response to need-frustrating environments. This is a genuinely radical claim about human potential. But it arrives without an account of why environments are need-frustrating in the first place, what maintains need-frustrating structures, and what forms of action beyond individual attitude change are required to alter them. A theory that identifies the conditions for flourishing without a complementary theory of why those conditions are so frequently absent is incomplete as a guide to human development.

#### 23. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] Hidden Assumption 4: The Three-and-Only-Three Assumption *(Line 379)*

> [!assumption-challenge] Hidden Assumption 4: The Three-and-Only-Three Assumption
> **The assumption**: SDT claims that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are the complete set of basic psychological needs — that these three and no others meet the criteria for organismic necessity (universal, innate, whose frustration produces ill-being).
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: The claim is stated explicitly in SDT literature but its justification is typically circular: these are the needs that research has established are basic, and the research program was designed around these three. Alternative candidates — security, meaning/purpose, autonomy within group membership, physical security — have been proposed by researchers in adjacent traditions (terror management theory, self-transcendence research, Maslow's hierarchy) without fully persuasive demonstrations that SDT's three subsume them.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If there are additional basic needs not captured by the three-need model, then environments optimized for autonomy, competence, and relatedness satisfaction might nonetheless fail to support human flourishing along dimensions the model doesn't see. The framework would have blind spots that its internal measures cannot detect.
> 
> **Assessment**: Partially survives. The case for autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic needs is strong and well-documented. The case for their completeness — that nothing else qualifies — is substantially weaker. The evidence of their necessity is stronger than the evidence of their sufficiency.

#### 24. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Internalization Teleology → Developmental Stage Theories *(Line 394)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Internalization Teleology → Developmental Stage Theories
> OIT's assumption that development naturally tends toward integration has a close structural analogue in stage-developmental theories generally (Piaget, Kohlberg, Loevinger, Kegan). All of these theories describe qualitatively distinct developmental stages and claim that development under appropriate conditions tends toward the highest stage — with the highest stage reliably reflecting the theorist's own cultural values about what mature functioning looks like. The pattern is robust enough to warrant a general caution: any developmental theory that posits a natural endpoint should be examined for the degree to which that endpoint is descriptive (this is what tends to happen) vs. normative (this is what should happen) vs. culturally inflected (this is what looks like flourishing in the context where the theorist lives and works). SDT is not uniquely guilty here — but nor is it exempt.

#### 27. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Reconceived Universality Thesis: Needs as Vulnerability Structures Rather Than Capacity Requirements *(Line 426)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Reconceived Universality Thesis: Needs as Vulnerability Structures Rather Than Capacity Requirements
> The most productive reconceptualization that emerges from this dialectical process is to think of basic psychological needs not as capacities that require expression (the received view) but as *vulnerability structures* — dimensions along which human beings are reliably vulnerable to ill-being when conditions work against them. This reframing does several things simultaneously. First, it makes the universality claim empirically tighter: the claim is that all humans share these vulnerabilities, which is better supported than the claim that all humans share these needs for fulfillment in specific forms. Second, it reduces the teleological pressure on OIT: rather than development tending toward need satisfaction (which implies a destination), development can be understood as navigating vulnerability management (which implies ongoing process). Third, it opens space for cultural variation in how the vulnerabilities are managed: if autonomy frustration is universally harmful but autonomy satisfaction is culturally variable in form, then designing for need support requires cultural sensitivity rather than one-size-fits-all prescriptions.

#### 28. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Reconstructed SDT: A Motivational Vulnerability-and-Resilience Framework *(Line 437)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Reconstructed SDT: A Motivational Vulnerability-and-Resilience Framework
> The reconstruction that emerges from this dialectical process is a version of SDT that can be stated as follows. Human beings are reliably vulnerable to motivational deterioration — reduced engagement quality, increased rigidity and defensiveness, lower well-being — when environments frustrate their need for volitional congruence (autonomy), perceived effectiveness (competence), and genuine connection (relatedness). This vulnerability is universal in the sense that the frustration-ill-being links hold robustly across cultures, even though the forms of need satisfaction are culturally and individually variable. Environments can build motivational resilience by supporting need satisfaction in culturally appropriate forms, and the quality of self-regulation can improve progressively under sustained need support — but this progressive quality is a conditional achievement rather than a natural trajectory. External regulation can scaffold genuine engagement rather than always threatening it, but the conditions under which scaffolding vs. threatening occurs are moderator-specific and must be assessed rather than assumed. This reconstructed SDT preserves the empirically robust core while shedding the teleological, categorical, and bounded-self commitments that the dialectical process revealed as fragile.

#### 32. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Motivational Needs → Therapeutic Practice *(Line 464)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Motivational Needs → Therapeutic Practice

The SDT reconstruction suggests a framework: design the therapeutic environment to reliably prevent frustration of core vulnerabilities (autonomy, competence, relatedness), while allowing the form of need satisfaction to be individually responsive. This resolves the false dichotomy between evidence-based universalism and individualized responsiveness by grounding them in the frustration/satisfaction distinction.

#### 33. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] OIT Internalization Teleology → Organizational Change Models *(Line 473)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] OIT Internalization Teleology → Organizational Change Models
> Stage-based organizational development models (Spiral Dynamics, Laloux's Teal organizations, Kegan's orders of mind as applied to leadership) make the same structural move as OIT: they describe a sequence of developmental stages and claim that development under the right conditions naturally tends toward the highest stage — which reliably looks like autonomous, self-organizing, complexity-embracing functioning that reflects the intellectual values of the theorists and their primary audiences. The SDT reconstruction suggests a more useful frame: describe the sequence without teleology (these stages have been observed to occur in this sequence under certain conditions), specify the conditions required for progression (rather than assuming natural tendency), and resist the implication that higher stages are universally desirable rather than contextually appropriate.

#### 34. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Dissemination Gap as a Structural Problem in Applied Science *(Line 482)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Dissemination Gap as a Structural Problem in Applied Science
> The gap between the moderator-conditional technical finding and the categorical popular or policy transmission is not unique to SDT. Stereotype threat effects, growth mindset interventions, ego-depletion, power poses, and many other behavioral science findings have followed the same pattern: nuanced, moderator-conditional effects in careful research; categorical claims in popular dissemination; and eventually a "replication crisis" that is partly a crisis of the categorical version, not necessarily of the conditional version. SDT's undermining effect is an instance of a general structural problem in the science-to-application translation pipeline. The solution is not more careful research — the careful research already exists. It is a different model of dissemination that preserves and transmits the moderator structure rather than collapsing it.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 531)*

> [!definition] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> Motivation that originates from the person's own values, interests, or genuine endorsement of an activity's importance. Encompasses both intrinsic motivation (acting because an activity is inherently enjoyable or interesting) and identified/integrated regulation (acting because one genuinely values the activity even if it is not intrinsically enjoyable). Distinguished from controlled motivation by the *perceived locus of causality*: autonomous motivation is associated with an internal perceived locus of causality, meaning the person experiences themselves as the origin of their action. In the reconstructed framework, "autonomous" is better understood as *volitional congruence* — acting in ways that align with one's own values — rather than as *independence*.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Controlled Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 534)*

> [!definition] Controlled Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> Motivation that originates from external pressure or internal compulsion rather than genuine endorsement. Encompasses external regulation (acting to obtain reward or avoid punishment) and introjected regulation (acting under pressure from an internalized but not accepted standard; ego-involvement). Associated with external or internal-compulsive perceived locus of causality. Not synonymous with extrinsic motivation in the everyday sense — one can be extrinsically motivated (acting for outcomes other than the activity itself) in an autonomous way if one genuinely values those outcomes.

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Need Frustration (Deci & Ryan; Vansteenkiste & Ryan, 2013) *(Line 537)*

> [!definition] Need Frustration (Deci & Ryan; Vansteenkiste & Ryan, 2013)
> The active blocking or undermining of basic psychological need satisfaction, distinguished from need absence (the neutral state of neither satisfying nor frustrating a need). Need frustration produces reliable ill-being effects — anxiety, defensiveness, alienation, motivational rigidity — that need absence does not. The [[Satisfaction-Frustration-Asymmetry]] is now understood as one of SDT's more robust empirical contributions. Preventing need frustration (designing environments that do not actively undermine autonomy, competence, and relatedness) is as important as promoting need satisfaction.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Perceived Locus of Causality (deCharms, 1968; adopted by Deci & Ryan) *(Line 540)*

> [!definition] Perceived Locus of Causality (deCharms, 1968; adopted by Deci & Ryan)
> The person's attributional sense of whether their actions originate from themselves (internal PLOC) or from external forces (external PLOC). A key mediating variable in [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]]: external events shift the PLOC from internal to external, thereby reducing autonomous motivation and intrinsic motivation. Importantly, PLOC is a perceived construct — it may not accurately reflect objective sources of causation — and is susceptible to individual differences in attribution style and [[Causality-Orientation]].

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Organismic Integration Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 543)*

> [!definition] Organismic Integration Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> SDT's sub-theory describing the process by which externally regulated behavior is progressively internalized and integrated into the self. The internalization continuum runs from external regulation through introjected regulation, identified regulation, and integrated regulation. OIT's empirical claims about the benefits of higher-quality internalization are well-supported; its teleological claim that internalization naturally tends toward integration is the dialectically challenged element. The reconstructed version: internalization can proceed toward integration under sustained need-supportive conditions, but this progression is contextually reversible and should be understood as a conditional achievement rather than a natural trajectory.

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Volitional Congruence (Reconstructed Concept) *(Line 546)*

> [!definition] Volitional Congruence (Reconstructed Concept)
> An alternative formulation of SDT's autonomy concept that emerges from this dialectical process. Rather than framing autonomy as self-authorship or independence, volitional congruence understands autonomy as *acting in ways that align with one's own values and relational commitments*. This formulation preserves the predictive content of SDT's autonomy need while making it coherent with collectivist cultural contexts where choosing to conform to one's group — when that choice is experienced as genuinely one's own — constitutes authentic autonomy rather than controlled compliance.

#### 44. [DEFINITION] Moderator-Conditionality (General Methodological Concept) *(Line 549)*

> [!definition] Moderator-Conditionality (General Methodological Concept)
> The property of a behavioral science finding whereby the effect holds only under specific conditions defined by moderating variables — variables that affect the direction or magnitude of a relationship. Many sweeping behavioral science claims (including SDT's undermining effect, stereotype threat, ego-depletion, and others) are categorical in their popular formulation but moderator-conditional in their careful empirical form. The gap between categorical transmission and moderator-conditional evidence is a structural problem in science dissemination.

#### 45. [DEFINITION] Universal Need (SDT Usage) *(Line 552)*

> [!definition] Universal Need (SDT Usage)
> In SDT, a need is "universal" if its frustration produces ill-being for all humans regardless of cultural context, developmental stage, or individual difference. This is distinct from the claim that its satisfaction takes the same form across cultures. The dialectical process supports the former claim (universal frustration effects) much more strongly than the latter (universal satisfaction forms). A more precise SDT formulation would state: "All humans are reliably vulnerable to motivational deterioration when their autonomy, competence, or relatedness needs are frustrated, though what satisfies these needs varies across cultural and individual contexts."

#### 46. [DEFINITION] Internalization (General Psychological Usage) *(Line 555)*

> [!definition] Internalization (General Psychological Usage)
> The process by which external values, regulations, or standards are taken into the self and represented as internal. SDT's [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]] distinguishes qualitatively different forms of internalization by their degree of self-endorsement — from simple taking-in (introjection) to genuine valuing (identification) to full self-congruence (integration). Internalization is not all-or-nothing; its quality matters as much as its occurrence.

#### 47. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs Theory (SDT Mini-Theory) *(Line 558)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs Theory (SDT Mini-Theory)
> SDT's sub-theory specifying that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are the three organismic psychological needs whose satisfaction is necessary (not merely beneficial) for psychological health and integrated functioning. Distinguishes psychological needs from desires, preferences, or goals — needs are innate and universal, not learned or culturally constructed. The theory's claim that the three identified needs are *complete* (that there are no other basic psychological needs) is the weakest element; the claim that these three are genuinely necessary is substantially better supported.

#### 48. [DEFINITION] Conditional Positive Regard (Rogers, 1951; applied in SDT) *(Line 561)*

> [!definition] Conditional Positive Regard (Rogers, 1951; applied in SDT)
> [[Conditional-Positive-Regard]] describes the experience of being valued by significant others contingent on meeting specific standards or performing specific behaviors. SDT research connects conditional regard to introjected regulation: when significant others' approval is contingent on outcomes, children and students internalize the pressure as an internal "should" (introjection) rather than as a genuine value (identification). [[Conditional-Regard]] is among the more reliably documented antecedents of contingent self-worth and controlled motivation.

#### 49. [DEFINITION] Teleological Directionality (Philosophical Concept Applied to Developmental Theory) *(Line 564)*

> [!definition] Teleological Directionality (Philosophical Concept Applied to Developmental Theory)
> The implicit or explicit assumption in a developmental theory that development has a natural endpoint toward which it tends under appropriate conditions. In OIT, the teleological directionality is the assumption that internalization naturally tends toward integration. Philosophically, teleological directionality in developmental theories should be examined for: (1) the degree to which the "natural endpoint" is empirically demonstrated vs. normatively prescribed; (2) whether the endpoint reflects universal human development or the theorist's cultural context; and (3) whether the directionality is genuinely unidirectional or contextually reversible.

#### 62. [FURTHER-EXPLORATION] Deepening Your Practice *(Line 617)*

> [!further-exploration] Deepening Your Practice
> > [!topic-idea] [[Undermining-Effect]]
> > The meta-analytic wars between Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999) and Cameron/Pierce (1994, 1996) constitute one of motivational psychology's most instructive empirical disputes, combining genuine methodological disagreement with substantive differences in theoretical orientation. A focused analysis would reconstruct the specific methodological choices (how "undermining" was operationalized, which studies were included vs. excluded, how moderators were handled) and draw out the general lessons about how competing meta-analyses can generate different conclusions from overlapping evidence bases. The reconstructed SDT's moderator-conditional undermining effect is only as strong as your understanding of what those moderators actually are.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[The-Integration-Paradox]]
> > How can a person autonomously adopt values that were initially externally imposed? This paradox is at the heart of OIT's internalization account and connects to some of philosophy's deepest puzzles about freedom, socialization, and authentic self-authorship. This report's dialectical challenge to the teleological directionality of OIT reopens rather than resolves the Integration Paradox. A focused examination would draw on Frankfurt's work on higher-order volitions, Bratman's planning theory, and Korsgaard's constitutivism to ask what philosophical resources are actually needed to ground SDT's account of internalization quality.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Causality-Orientations-Theory]]
> > SDT's individual differences sub-theory — distinguishing autonomy orientation (dispositional tendency toward interest-based, self-determined engagement), control orientation (dispositional tendency toward reward-contingent, approval-seeking engagement), and impersonal orientation (dispositional tendency toward amotivation and helplessness) — intersects with the practical challenge raised in Phase III in a way that deserves its own examination. Students with control or impersonal orientations do not straightforwardly benefit from autonomy-supportive environments in the way the general theory predicts; they may find open choice anxiety-provoking and ambiguous rationale confusing. A focused analysis would examine what autonomy support should look like for students at different points on the causality orientations spectrum.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Relatedness-Need]]
> > SDT's most undertheorized need deserves its own dialectical examination. Relatedness receives substantially less theoretical development in SDT literature than autonomy (the theory's primary focus) or competence (well-connected to effectance research). Yet the emerging science of belonging, social connection, and perceived partner responsiveness suggests that the relational dimension of motivation is both more complex and more consequential than SDT's standard treatment implies. The connection between SDT's relatedness need and attachment theory, Bowlby's secure base, and belonging uncertainty research would substantially enrich the framework.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Goal-Content-Theory]]
> > SDT's extension to goal content — distinguishing intrinsic aspirations (personal growth, community contribution, meaningful relationships) from extrinsic aspirations (wealth, fame, physical attractiveness) — applies the needs framework to what people pursue rather than how they pursue it. The [[Aspiration-Index]] operationalizes this distinction and has generated substantial evidence that intrinsic goal orientation predicts well-being better than goal attainment per se. A dialectical re-examination of GCT would ask: is the intrinsic/extrinsic goal content distinction genuinely universal, or does it reflect specific cultural values about what constitutes meaningful life goals?
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Relationship-Motivation-Theory]]
> > SDT's most recently elaborated mini-theory applies the basic needs framework to close relationships, arguing that genuinely loving relationships require both partners to experience need satisfaction *within* the relationship rather than simply providing it to each other. This creates interesting predictions: relationships in which one partner provides autonomy support and the other does not are likely to be asymmetrically satisfying in ways that undermine the relationship's quality over time. A focused analysis would connect RMT to attachment theory, interdependence theory, and the growing literature on perceived partner responsiveness.
