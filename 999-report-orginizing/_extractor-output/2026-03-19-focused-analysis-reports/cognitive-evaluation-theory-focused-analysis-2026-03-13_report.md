#### 2. [DEFINITION] Intrinsic Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 281)*

> [!definition] Intrinsic Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> Motivation that arises from the inherent interest, enjoyment, or satisfaction of an activity itself, independent of separable outcomes. An intrinsically motivated person engages in an activity because it is experienced as freely chosen, interesting, and inherently rewarding. The prototypical markers are: voluntary initiation, persistence in the absence of external prompts, positive affect during engagement, and free-choice resumption after an interruption. Intrinsic motivation is NOT the same as liking an activity — one can like (be positively valenced toward) an activity one is extrinsically motivated to perform. The critical property is the perceived source of regulation: intrinsic motivation is internally regulated; extrinsic motivation is regulated by anticipated consequences outside the activity itself.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC) (de Charms, 1968; Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 284)*

> [!definition] Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC) (de Charms, 1968; Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The subjective perception of the source of one's own behaviour — experienced as either originating within oneself (internal PLOC, "I chose to do this") or determined by external forces (external PLOC, "I am doing this because of outside pressure"). PLOC is not a binary state but a continuum, and it is phenomenological rather than objective: the same objectively constrained situation may register as internally caused for one person and externally caused for another. CET treats perceived locus of causality as the primary mediator linking external events to intrinsic motivation: events that shift PLOC toward the external pole undermine intrinsic motivation; events that preserve or strengthen an internal PLOC protect it. This is distinct from actual causality — the point is what the person *perceives*.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 287)*

> [!definition] Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The psychological meaning an event carries for the person who experiences it, specifically with respect to two possible aspects: the **controlling aspect** (the degree to which the event is experienced as pressuring the person to behave in a particular way, implying that the event is managing or directing behaviour) and the **informational aspect** (the degree to which the event is experienced as providing competence-relevant feedback, conveying information about how well one is doing relative to a standard). Every external event — reward, praise, deadline, feedback, surveillance — carries *both* aspects to some degree, but one tends to be more salient. It is this relative salience, not the objective nature of the event, that determines its motivational effect. Events whose controlling aspect is most salient shift PLOC external and undermine intrinsic motivation. Events whose informational aspect is most salient enhance intrinsic motivation by supporting perceived competence, provided PLOC remains internal.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Reward Contingency Types *(Line 290)*

> [!definition] Reward Contingency Types
> The structural relationship between performance and reward. Three types matter most for CET: (1) **Task-contingent rewards** — given simply for participating in or completing the task, without reference to quality (e.g., "here is a sticker for drawing"). (2) **Completion-contingent rewards** — given for completing the task to a basic standard. (3) **Performance-contingent rewards** — given in proportion to quality of performance (e.g., "top marks get a prize"). CET predicts that task-contingent and completion-contingent rewards carry primarily a controlling functional significance and reliably undermine intrinsic motivation; performance-contingent rewards are more ambiguous because they also carry informational content about competence, though their controlling aspect typically dominates unless the framing is explicitly informational.

#### 6. [KEY-CLAIM] CET's Central Proposition *(Line 295)*

> [!key-claim] CET's Central Proposition
> No external event has an intrinsically fixed effect on intrinsic motivation. Its effect is determined by the cognitive-evaluative interpretation that establishes its functional significance. Controlling events (those perceived primarily as pressure) undermine intrinsic motivation by externalising PLOC. Informational events (those perceived primarily as competence feedback) can enhance intrinsic motivation when they convey positive competence information and when PLOC remains internal. Amotivating events (those that convey neither agency nor competence — such as meaningless failure or arbitrary incompetence feedback) undermine both autonomy and competence perceptions, producing the deepest motivational damage.

#### 10. [EVIDENCE] The Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973) Core Finding *(Line 324)*

> [!evidence] The Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973) Core Finding
> Children in the expected-reward condition showed significantly lower free-choice engagement with drawing materials 1–2 weeks after the reward phase, compared to unexpected-reward and no-reward controls. Effect sizes were substantial (Cohen's d estimates from later meta-analyses place the effect around 0.4–0.5 for expected tangible rewards). This study established the "overjustification effect" as a concept — the idea that externally justifying behaviour you already enjoy paradoxically weakens your motivation to engage in it.

#### 11. [EVIDENCE] Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) — Key Findings from 128 Studies *(Line 335)*

> [!evidence] Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) — Key Findings from 128 Studies
> (1) Expected tangible rewards reliably undermined intrinsic motivation as measured by free-choice behaviour (d ≈ −0.36) and self-reported interest (d ≈ −0.15). (2) Unexpected rewards did not undermine intrinsic motivation. (3) Verbal rewards (praise) enhanced intrinsic motivation when conveying positive competence information. (4) Engagement-contingent and completion-contingent tangible rewards showed the strongest undermining. (5) Performance-contingent tangible rewards showed an intermediate pattern, reflecting their dual controlling/informational character.

#### 12. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Meta-Analysis Interpretation Battle *(Line 338)*

> [!tension-identified] The Meta-Analysis Interpretation Battle
> Cameron/Pierce (1994) and Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999) analysed largely overlapping sets of studies and reached opposite conclusions. How? The disagreement is not primarily about the data — it is about *how to code the variables*. The two teams classified studies into contingency categories differently, defined "undermining" using different measurement criteria, and made different modelling choices about moderator variables. This reveals a methodological problem that is partly irresolvable: the functional significance construct is difficult to operationalise objectively. When an experiment labels a condition "expected reward," it is not obvious whether participants experienced this as primarily controlling or informational — and the researchers' coding decisions implicitly embed theoretical commitments. The dispute is not just empirical; it is partly about what counts as a fair test of the theory.

#### 13. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What the Pattern of Findings Actually Shows *(Line 347)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Pattern of Findings Actually Shows
> The undermining effect is real but not universal. Expected tangible rewards for task engagement (where reward receipt does not depend on quality) reliably undermine free-choice behaviour in laboratory paradigms. This effect is medium-sized and has been replicated across decades. However, the effect is highly conditional: it does not apply to unexpected rewards, it is weakened or reversed when rewards carry genuine positive competence information, it is sensitive to whether the controlling aspect is made salient by the administration context (how the reward is presented, by whom, and in what relational context), and it is moderated by individual differences in need for autonomy. The effect is NOT a universal property of rewards-as-objects; it is a property of the psychological meaning that particular reward interactions create in specific people in specific contexts.

#### 14. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Praise Finding Is Particularly Informative *(Line 350)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Praise Finding Is Particularly Informative
> Verbal rewards (praise) consistently emerge from the meta-analytic literature as enhancing rather than undermining intrinsic motivation — which is exactly what CET predicts. Praise is, structurally, a controlling event: someone in authority is evaluating you and finding you sufficient. But when praise is framed as conveying genuine competence information ("You did that correctly; you have real skill here"), it activates the informational pathway more than the controlling pathway, and the result is enhanced intrinsic motivation. This finding is important because it shows that CET's mechanism works in both directions: it is not that external events are inherently undermining; it is that the functional significance framework correctly predicts *which* events undermine and *which* enhance.

#### 18. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Invisible Transformation of Meaning *(Line 381)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Invisible Transformation of Meaning
> What the autonomy pathway mechanism reveals is that rewards do not merely add a new reason to perform an activity alongside the pre-existing intrinsic reason. They can transform the *experienced meaning* of the activity itself. Before the reward, drawing was an act of self-expression. After repeated reward administration, drawing is an act of earning. The activity's identity — what it *is* for the person — has changed. This is why the effect persists after rewards are removed: you cannot simply subtract the reward and expect to return to the previous motivational state, because the activity's meaning has been reorganised. This is a far more radical claim than merely saying "people become accustomed to rewards" — it is a claim about how reward structures reshape the self-world relationship.

#### 19. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Asymmetry Between Enhancement and Damage *(Line 392)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Asymmetry Between Enhancement and Damage
> The two-pathway architecture produces an interesting asymmetry: enhancing intrinsic motivation requires satisfying *both* the autonomy and competence needs simultaneously — an event must convey positive competence information *and* do so in a way that preserves internal PLOC. This is a conjunction requirement. Undermining intrinsic motivation, by contrast, requires only one pathway — either externalising PLOC *or* frustrating perceived competence will damage intrinsic motivation. This means that intrinsic motivation is harder to build than to erode. It is robust against threats from a single pathway, but only when the other pathway is actively supported. In instructional contexts, this implies that well-intentioned informational praise can be partially neutralized if delivered in a surveillance-heavy, controlling evaluative atmosphere — the competence benefit is offset by autonomy cost.

#### 20. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] CET and Communicative Pragmatics: The Same Sentence, Different Speech Acts *(Line 403)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] CET and Communicative Pragmatics: The Same Sentence, Different Speech Acts
> Cognitive Evaluation Theory's central insight — that the same event carries different meaning depending on its functional significance — has a precise structural parallel in linguistic pragmatics, specifically [[speech act theory]] (Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969). In speech act theory, an utterance's meaning is not exhausted by its propositional content (what it literally says); its force depends on the social act it performs (a promise, a threat, a directive, an assessment). "You're good at this" can be a genuine expression of admiration (informational), a manipulative tool to elicit compliance ("since you're so good at this, you should keep doing it" — controlling), or a performance of social role. The *same surface form* carries radically different illocutionary force depending on context, relationship, power dynamics, and history. CET's functional significance is, in essence, the motivational analogue of illocutionary force: it is not what the event literally delivers but what social-psychological act it performs in the relationship that matters. This parallel suggests that CET would benefit from closer engagement with sociolinguistic and relational accounts of how competence and control signals are communicated — and why the same reward, administered by different people in different relational contexts, has such different effects.

#### 21. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Operationalisation Problem: When Functional Significance Becomes Unfalsifiable *(Line 418)*

> [!tension-identified] The Operationalisation Problem: When Functional Significance Becomes Unfalsifiable
> CET's most significant theoretical vulnerability is related to its greatest strength. The concept of functional significance is powerful precisely because it does not tie motivational effects to the structural properties of events — it allows the same event to have different effects depending on how it is experienced. But this flexibility comes at a cost: functional significance is extraordinarily difficult to operationalise independently of the motivational outcomes it is supposed to predict. In most studies, the researcher manipulates the event (reward type, feedback framing) and then measures intrinsic motivation. The functional significance is inferred from the outcome, not measured directly. This creates the risk of circular explanation: "The reward undermined intrinsic motivation because it was controlling; how do we know it was controlling? Because it undermined intrinsic motivation." The studies that get closest to independent measurement of functional significance (using manipulation checks, thought-listing procedures, or phenomenological reports) provide more compelling evidence — but they remain the minority. This is not a fatal flaw but a persistent limitation that needs to be held in mind when interpreting the evidence base.

#### 24. [BEST-PRACTICE] Designing Informational Rather Than Controlling Feedback *(Line 444)*

> [!best-practice] Designing Informational Rather Than Controlling Feedback
> Effective feedback under CET principles: (1) References the person's own prior performance rather than comparative norms — progress feedback is more informational, rank feedback is more controlling. (2) Uses language that attributes performance to internal competencies ("you found an efficient approach") rather than external compliance ("you followed the instructions correctly"). (3) Is delivered in a context where the relationship is characterised by autonomy support — acknowledgment of the person's perspective, explanations rather than directives, non-controlling language. (4) Avoids surveillance-like monitoring that makes the evaluative relationship salient even in the absence of explicit evaluative events.

#### 25. [WARNING] The Gamification Paradox *(Line 451)*

> [!warning] The Gamification Paradox
> The activities most commonly targeted for gamification are those that educators and designers perceive as insufficiently engaging — and the addition of game mechanics is intended to supply the missing motivation. But CET predicts that if the activity was already somewhat intrinsically interesting (or had the potential to become so with appropriate autonomy-supportive framing), the addition of controlling reward structures will damage that potential. Gamification often imports the motivational dynamics of extrinsic contingency precisely where intrinsic motivation was most valuable to preserve. The question to ask before gamifying is not "will this increase engagement?" (it often will, in the short term) but "what kind of engagement, through what motivational mechanism, and at what cost to intrinsic interest?"

#### 28. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Interpretive Filter Model of Functional Significance *(Line 491)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Interpretive Filter Model of Functional Significance
> Synthesising CET's core architecture with the evidence on moderating factors and the temporal dynamics section, I propose a model that makes the functional significance concept more tractable for both research and practice. Rather than treating functional significance as a property of events (or even of events-in-context), the model treats it as the output of an *interpretive filter* — a relatively stable, individual-level attentional and evaluative orientation that determines which aspects of external events are most salient for a given person at a given time.
> 
> The interpretive filter has three components:
> 
> **(1) The relational frame**: The established meaning-structure of the evaluative relationship between the person and the source of the external event (teacher, employer, parent, system). This frame is built up through history and cannot be altered by a single event; it acts as a background context within which new events are interpreted. A controlling relational frame means that even genuinely informational events will be partly assimilated to a controlling interpretation; an autonomy-supportive relational frame allows informational events to be received as such.
> 
> **(2) The motivational history with the specific activity**: Whether the activity has previously been predominantly internally or externally regulated. Activities with a long history of intrinsic engagement have a "motivational baseline" that is more resistant to controlling events; activities with a history of extrinsic regulation are more vulnerable to further externalisation of PLOC because the internal referent for the activity's value has already been weakened.
> 
> **(3) The dispositional orientation**: The individual's relatively stable tendency to orient toward internal versus external causality (captured in SDT by Causality Orientations Theory). Autonomy-oriented individuals maintain internal PLOC more readily under controlling conditions; control-oriented individuals are more vulnerable to the undermining effect.
> 
> What this model adds to standard CET is a mechanism for understanding why the *same* event produces such different effects across people and contexts: the interpretive filter is doing real work, not as a passive receiver but as an active constructor of meaning. It also provides a leverage point for intervention: rather than focusing only on event properties (making rewards informational), practitioners should attend to the relational frame (building an autonomy-supportive relationship context over time) and the motivational history (avoiding early externalisation of regulation for activities where intrinsic interest is the goal). The event-level and relationship-level interventions are not substitutes; they operate at different timescales and through different mechanisms.

#### 29. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] Motivational Asymmetry and Instructional Design Sequencing *(Line 511)*

> [!original-synthesis] Motivational Asymmetry and Instructional Design Sequencing
> The temporal asymmetry between undermining (fast-acting, hard to reverse) and enhancement (slow-building, fragile) has direct implications for the *sequencing* of external events in instructional contexts. Specifically: the damage done by early controlling events in a learning sequence is disproportionately costly relative to late controlling events, because early events shape the interpretive filter through which subsequent events are processed. A teacher who begins the year with high-stakes, controlling evaluation practices, then shifts to autonomy-supportive practices mid-year, will likely achieve lower intrinsic motivation outcomes than a teacher who maintains autonomy-supportive practices throughout — even if the total "dose" of controlling events is identical. The order matters. This is not explicitly stated in standard CET, but it follows directly from the interaction between temporal dynamics and the interpretive filter model. Instructional designers should treat the first exposures to a domain or activity as particularly high-stakes for motivational architecture, not merely for knowledge structure.

Conversely, external reward structures that undermine intrinsic motivation (per CET) would be expected to simultaneously degrade self-regulatory quality — particularly the autonomous goal-setting and self-evaluation processes that SRL depends on. The CET → SRL pathway is undertheorised in the literature and represents a promising integration target.

Conversely, high intrinsic motivation, by producing deeper engagement with the material's structure, may facilitate the kind of elaborative processing that builds schemas (the goal of CLT-informed instruction). This is a connection neither theory makes explicit, but exploring it would deepen understanding of why motivationally-optimal conditions tend also to be cognitively-optimal conditions.
> 
> - **[[Attribution Theory]]** — Weiner's attribution theory addresses how people explain their successes and failures. CET's PLOC construct is conceptually related to Weiner's locus of control dimension, but they are not identical: PLOC in CET is specifically about the perceived cause of *behaviour* (internal vs. external), while locus of control in attribution theory is about the perceived cause of *outcomes* (success/failure). The intersection is particularly important for understanding how competence feedback interacts with attributional patterns: negative feedback attributed to controllable, internal causes (effort) has different motivational effects than the same feedback attributed to stable, internal causes (lack of ability) — and CET's competence pathway prediction depends on this attributional dimension without fully theorising it.

#### 32. [DEFINITION] Amotivation *(Line 549)*

> [!definition] Amotivation
> A state in which the person lacks any sense of agency with respect to an activity — neither intrinsic motivation (engagement for its own sake) nor extrinsic motivation (engagement for separable outcomes). Amotivation is the motivational consequence of events that simultaneously frustrate perceived competence and externalise PLOC to the point of helplessness. It is the deepest form of motivational damage in the SDT framework and is associated with disengagement, helplessness, and eventually dropout. Amotivation is distinct from low intrinsic motivation — one can be extrinsically motivated (working for rewards) without being amotivated.

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Autonomy Orientation (from Causality Orientations Theory) *(Line 552)*

> [!definition] Autonomy Orientation (from Causality Orientations Theory)
> An individual-level difference variable describing the general tendency to orient toward the autonomous aspects of environments — to notice and be regulated by features that support interest, value, and personal meaning. Autonomy-oriented individuals show attenuated undermining effects in response to controlling events. They are not *immune* to functional significance effects, but their interpretive filter has a stronger default bias toward internal PLOC. Contrasts with *control orientation* (tendency to be regulated by controlling features) and *impersonal orientation* (tendency to attribute regulation to uncontrollable forces, associated with amotivation).

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Causality Orientations Theory (COT) *(Line 555)*

> [!definition] Causality Orientations Theory (COT)
> A mini-theory within SDT that describes individual differences in people's tendencies to orient toward particular types of motivational regulation. Not to be confused with [[attribution theory]]. COT predicts that autonomy-oriented individuals will respond differently to controlling events than control-oriented individuals, and this moderates the functional significance mechanism in CET. An important implication: identical instructional interventions will produce different motivational outcomes for individuals with different causality orientations.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Ego Involvement *(Line 558)*

> [!definition] Ego Involvement
> A motivational state in which self-worth is contingent on performance outcomes — performance becomes a test of the person's value or adequacy rather than an expression of their interests. Ego involvement is theoretically related to the controlling pathway in CET: events that create ego involvement (typically comparative evaluation, normative feedback, and high-stakes assessment) introduce a self-esteem regulation layer that makes the activity extrinsically salient and shifts PLOC external. Harackiewicz and Manderlink (1984) showed that ego-involving praise ("you're much better than average") undermine intrinsic motivation while task-involving praise ("you've really mastered the strategy") does not.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Engagement-Contingent Reward *(Line 561)*

> [!definition] Engagement-Contingent Reward
> A reward contingent simply on engaging in or starting an activity, without reference to completion or quality. This is the most purely controlling reward type in CET's taxonomy: the contingency "if you do it, you get the reward" makes the external reason for the behaviour maximally salient. Empirically, engagement-contingent and task-contingent rewards show the strongest undermining effects in the Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999) meta-analysis.

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Free-Choice Behaviour *(Line 564)*

> [!definition] Free-Choice Behaviour
> The dependent variable most commonly used in CET research to measure intrinsic motivation: the amount of time a person voluntarily spends with an activity when they are not required to do so, typically measured during a period when the experimenter is absent and no external contingencies are operating. Free-choice behaviour is considered a more direct behavioural measure of intrinsic motivation than self-reported interest, because it does not depend on verbal self-report. However, it also has limitations: it may reflect habit or accessibility rather than intrinsic motivation, and it is difficult to measure in ecological (non-laboratory) contexts.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Informational Feedback *(Line 567)*

> [!definition] Informational Feedback
> Feedback whose primary functional significance is informational — it conveys genuine competence-relevant information to the recipient in a context where PLOC remains internal. Informational feedback is characterised by specificity (pointing to particular aspects of performance), mastery-referencing (comparing performance to personal standards or task standards rather than to other people), process orientation (attributing performance to strategies and effort rather than global ability), and non-controlling language (describing rather than evaluating). CET predicts that informational feedback enhances intrinsic motivation by satisfying the competence need without threatening the autonomy need.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Internalisation *(Line 570)*

> [!definition] Internalisation
> The process by which externally regulated behaviours are gradually taken in and re-regulated by the self — moving along the self-determination continuum from external regulation toward identified regulation and ultimately integrated regulation. While CET specifically addresses intrinsic motivation (the far end of the intrinsic pole), Organismic Integration Theory (another SDT sub-theory) addresses internalisation of extrinsic motivation. The two processes are related: creating conditions that support autonomy and competence facilitates both intrinsic motivation (when activities are inherently interesting) and internalisation (when activities are not inherently interesting but have personal or social value).

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Overjustification Effect *(Line 573)*

> [!definition] Overjustification Effect
> The specific psychological phenomenon in which adding an external justification (reward) to a behaviour that is already intrinsically motivated reduces the person's subsequent intrinsic interest in that behaviour. The term "overjustification" refers to the idea that the external reason provides more justification than is needed — and in the process, changes the person's interpretation of why they were performing the activity in the first place. Originally named by Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973); theoretically explained by CET's PLOC mechanism.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Perceived Competence *(Line 576)*

> [!definition] Perceived Competence
> The subjective sense of effectiveness in one's interactions with the environment — feeling capable, skilled, and able to produce intended outcomes. Distinct from objective competence: a person can be highly competent but have low perceived competence (as in the Impostor Phenomenon), or low competence but high perceived competence (overconfidence). In CET, perceived competence is a basic psychological need whose satisfaction by external events (through positive informational feedback) can enhance intrinsic motivation, and whose frustration (through negative feedback or task failure without informational framing) undermines intrinsic motivation.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Surveillance *(Line 579)*

> [!definition] Surveillance
> The awareness of being observed or monitored, even in the absence of explicit evaluative events. CET research (Lepper & Greene, 1975) shows that surveillance carries a controlling functional significance on its own: being watched introduces an external audience, makes the evaluative relationship salient, and tends to shift PLOC external. This finding has significant implications for digital learning environments in which learner behaviour is continuously logged and monitored, even if that monitoring is not connected to explicit rewards or consequences.

#### 55. [FURTHER-EXPLORATION] Deepening Your Analysis *(Line 626)*

> [!further-exploration] Deepening Your Analysis
> > [!topic-idea] [[Basic Psychological Needs Theory]]
> > CET is one of six SDT mini-theories, and it posits autonomy and competence as mediating mechanisms without fully developing their status as universal psychological needs. Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT) provides this foundational layer, arguing that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are three universal organismic needs whose satisfaction predicts well-being and whose frustration predicts ill-being — across cultures, activities, and developmental stages. A focused analysis of BPNT would deepen the CET account by examining the need satisfaction/frustration asymmetry more carefully, connecting to the hedonic and eudaimonic well-being literature, and addressing the cross-cultural evidence that is both supportive and challenging for the universality claim.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Feedback Effects on Motivation and Learning]]
> > CET's distinction between informational and controlling events maps most directly onto the feedback literature in educational psychology. A focused analysis here would synthesise CET with the Kluger and DeNisi (1996) meta-analysis of feedback effects, Hattie's feedback research, Shute's formative feedback framework, and the growing literature on computer-delivered feedback. The specific question is: what features of feedback operationalise CET's informational vs. controlling distinction in ways that are testable in educational contexts, and how does feedback design interact with the relational frame and motivational history constructs from the Interpretive Filter Model?
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Expectancy-Value Theory]]
> > EVT (Eccles, Wigfield) and CET approach motivation from different directions: EVT focuses on the cognitive appraisal of tasks (do I expect to succeed? does this task have value for me?), while CET focuses on the source of regulation (am I doing this for internal or external reasons?). These frameworks are frequently treated as competitors, but they are better understood as complementary: EVT explains the structure of motivation (what determines whether someone will engage), while CET explains the quality and sustainability of motivation (what determines whether engagement is internally vs. externally regulated). A focused comparative analysis would map the intersection points, identify where each is insufficient alone, and propose an integration.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Growth Mindset and CET]]
> > Dweck's growth vs. fixed mindset framework shares important territory with CET — particularly around praise, competence feedback, and the effects of evaluative contexts on motivation and persistence. But the two frameworks make somewhat different predictions and are grounded in different theoretical mechanisms (implicit theories of ability vs. functional significance of feedback). A focused analysis would compare their predictions for specific instructional scenarios, examine where they converge (process praise) and diverge (the role of challenge and difficulty), and ask what an integrated account would look like.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Gamification in Educational Contexts]]
> > Gamification is the application of game-design elements (points, badges, leaderboards, progress bars, challenges) to non-game contexts, typically with the goal of increasing engagement. CET predicts that many common gamification elements introduce controlling functional significance and will undermine intrinsic motivation in the medium-to-long term, even if they increase short-term engagement. But the picture is more complicated: some game elements (challenge structures, mastery feedback, narrative framing) may activate the informational pathway. A focused analysis would use CET as a primary theoretical lens to evaluate specific gamification elements, develop a CET-informed gamification design framework, and examine the emerging empirical literature on gamification outcomes.
