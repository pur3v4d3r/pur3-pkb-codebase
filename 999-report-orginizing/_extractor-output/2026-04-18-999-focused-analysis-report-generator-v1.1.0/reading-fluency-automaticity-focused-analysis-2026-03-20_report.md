#### 3. [DEFINITION] Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986) *(Line 181)*

> [!definition] Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986)
> Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Language Comprehension. Reading comprehension is the product — not the sum — of two distinct and separable components: (1) decoding ability, the capacity to translate print to language, and (2) oral language comprehension, the capacity to understand language once it has been decoded. The multiplicative relationship implies that weakness in either component drives the product toward zero regardless of strength in the other.

#### 4. [KEY-CLAIM] Fluency as the Bridge Between Decoding and Comprehension *(Line 186)*

> [!key-claim] Fluency as the Bridge Between Decoding and Comprehension
> The Simple View omits fluency as a third component, yet fluency is not merely a proxy for decoding skill — it is the mechanism by which adequate decoding becomes comprehension-supporting decoding. A reader who decodes accurately but slowly has not truly transitioned to reading; they have a bottleneck between the SVR's two components that prevents either from functioning at ceiling.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Reading Fluency (Wolf & Katzir-Cohen, 2001) *(Line 189)*

> [!definition] Reading Fluency (Wolf & Katzir-Cohen, 2001)
> Reading fluency is the ability to read connected text with accuracy, appropriate rate, and prosody — with sufficient ease that comprehension resources are available to focus on meaning-making rather than word identification. Fluency is not merely reading speed; it integrates accuracy (getting words right), automaticity (retrieving them without deliberate effort), and prosody (reading with appropriate expression, phrasing, and intonation that reflects comprehension of grammatical and semantic structure).

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Automaticity (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974) *(Line 198)*

> [!definition] Automaticity (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974)
> A cognitive process is automatic when it can be executed rapidly, without conscious effort, and — critically — without drawing on the limited attentional resources available for other simultaneous processes. In reading, automaticity refers specifically to the recognition of orthographic patterns (letters, letter clusters, and whole words) without the deliberate deployment of attentional resources. Automatic recognition is distinguished from non-automatic recognition by three criteria: it is fast (sub-300ms), obligatory (recognition occurs whether or not the reader intends it), and resource-free (it proceeds without degrading concurrent processing).

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Verbal Efficiency Theory (Perfetti, 1985, 1992) *(Line 203)*

> [!definition] Verbal Efficiency Theory (Perfetti, 1985, 1992)
> Charles Perfetti's verbal efficiency theory refines LaBerge and Samuels by specifying the locus of the bottleneck more precisely. Verbal efficiency refers to the speed and quality of local text processes — particularly word identification and the maintenance of decoded words in [[Working-Memory]] long enough to be integrated into propositions and sentences. Inefficient local processes create a resource drain that impairs not just the speed of comprehension but its quality and completeness, because the products of word recognition may fade from working memory before they can be integrated into the reader's text representation.

#### 9. [KEY-CLAIM] The Accuracy-Automaticity Distinction *(Line 215)*

> [!key-claim] The Accuracy-Automaticity Distinction
> Accuracy and automaticity are separable. A reader may be highly accurate (low error rate) without being automatic (resource-free). > [!evidence] Perfetti & Lesgold (1977) — The Resource Competition Paradigm
> In a foundational set of experiments, Perfetti and Lesgold demonstrated that lower-skill readers showed greater memory load effects during reading than higher-skill readers at equivalent text difficulty. The critical finding was that differences in comprehension between skill groups were most pronounced when texts required integration across sentences — precisely the condition where the working memory products of earlier decoding must remain available while later words are decoded. This is the signature of a working memory bottleneck, not merely a decoding accuracy difference.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Bottleneck Evidence Collectively Suggests

This is not a story about general cognitive ability differences between good and poor readers; it is a story about a specific, addressable architectural bottleneck.

#### 14. [EVIDENCE] Repeated Reading Research — Samuels (1979) *(Line 254)*

> [!evidence] Repeated Reading Research — Samuels (1979)
> Samuels' pioneering repeated reading paradigm — in which students read short passages multiple times until reaching a criterion fluency level — showed not only that fluency improved on practiced passages but that *generalization occurred to unpracticed text*. This transfer finding is analytically critical: if fluency improvement merely reflected familiarity with specific words, there would be no reason to expect transfer. The transfer evidence implies that what develops is not a list of memorized words but a *processing capability* — a more efficient decoding mechanism — that generalizes to new text.

#### 16. [EVIDENCE] Stanovich (1986) — The Compounding Inequality of Early Reading Skill *(Line 264)*

> [!evidence] Stanovich (1986) — The Compounding Inequality of Early Reading Skill
> Children who acquire fluency early read more, encounter more words, build larger vocabularies, develop richer background knowledge, and thereby become even more fluent readers. #### 17. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Matthew Effect as a Positive Feedback Loop in Cognitive Architecture *(Line 267)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Matthew Effect as a Positive Feedback Loop in Cognitive Architecture
> The [[Matthew-Effect]] is usually analyzed as a motivational and behavioral phenomenon — skilled readers enjoy reading, so they read more; struggling readers avoid it. But there is a deeper cognitive layer. Non-automatic decoding does not merely reduce the *pleasure* of reading; it reduces its *cognitive affordance*. A disfluent reader attempting to read text at grade level is operating near or above their cognitive capacity ceiling. At that load level, the [[Germane-Cognitive-Load|germane cognitive load]] required for schema construction is unavailable — meaning the reading experience does not build the knowledge structures that would make future reading easier. The struggling reader is not merely reading less enjoyable text; they are reading in a cognitive state that prevents the kind of deep processing that would generate lasting knowledge gains. The cognitive architecture itself guarantees that effortful reading produces less learning than effortless reading of the same text.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] WCPM as a Metric: Strengths and Systematic Blind Spots

#### 22. [DEFINITION] Working Memory in Reading (Baddeley's Model) *(Line 296)*

> [!definition] Working Memory in Reading (Baddeley's Model)
> [[Baddeley's-Working-Memory-Model|Baddeley's working memory model]] includes three primary components relevant to reading: (1) the [[Phonological-Loop]], which holds verbal material in a phonological code for approximately 2 seconds through active rehearsal; (2) the visuospatial sketchpad, which processes visual features of text (orthography, spatial layout); and (3) the central executive, which allocates attentional resources, switches between tasks, and coordinates the interaction of long-term memory with active processing. Working memory capacity is limited — typically 3-4 chunks for the phonological loop — and reading creates high demands on all three components simultaneously.

#### 23. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Invisible Comprehension Failure *(Line 303)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Invisible Comprehension Failure
> There is a profound asymmetry between the visibility of decoding failure and the invisibility of the comprehension failure it causes. When a child misreads a word, the error is detectable — by the child, the teacher, or any observer. But when a child decodes a word correctly yet so slowly that the phonological trace fades before sentence completion, the failure is invisible at the level of individual word reading. The child reads every word in the sentence correctly. Yet comprehension is impaired. This asymmetry means that purely accuracy-focused reading assessment systematically *misses* the most common source of comprehension difficulty in non-dyslexic struggling readers. The child's problem is not that they cannot read words — it is that they cannot read them fast enough to hold a sentence together. Accuracy-based assessment gives a false all-clear.

#### 24. [DEFINITION] Schema Automation in Reading *(Line 310)*

> [!definition] Schema Automation in Reading
> [[Schema-Automation]] in the reading context refers to the process by which orthographic patterns — from individual grapheme-phoneme correspondences to morphemes, syllables, and whole words — become encoded as unified long-term memory schemas that can be retrieved as wholes rather than constructed from parts. When the spelling pattern "tion" has been encountered sufficient times, its phonological equivalent /ʃən/ can be retrieved as a unit from long-term memory rather than assembled from four separate grapheme-phoneme correspondences. The automated schema bypasses the capacity-limited working memory construction process entirely.

#### 25. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Prosody as Mechanism, Not Symptom *(Line 321)*

> [!analytical-insight] Prosody as Mechanism, Not Symptom
> Prosody in oral reading is typically treated as an indicator of fluency — a symptom that reflects comprehension. But there is a compelling case that prosody is also partially a *mechanism* — that reading aloud with appropriate phrasing and intonation forces the reader to parse syntactic structure during oral production, which in turn scaffolds the same parsing process in comprehension. When students engage in repeated reading with explicit prosodic feedback (read it like you're telling the story), the explicit prosodic processing may strengthen syntactic parsing routines that aid comprehension. Prosody may be both a consequence of fluency and a training target that builds it.

#### 26. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Automaticity in Reading → Automaticity in Music: The Same Architecture *(Line 330)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Automaticity in Reading → Automaticity in Music: The Same Architecture
> When a beginning piano student learns a piece, their attention is entirely consumed by identifying the notes, finding the keys, and producing individual sounds. When a piece becomes automatic — when note-reading and finger-placement are handled by procedures running below conscious attention — the musician can attend to phrasing, dynamics, expression, and musical communication. The parallel to reading is structural, not merely metaphorical. Both cases involve the same cognitive architecture: (1) a limited attentional resource, (2) lower-level processes that initially consume that resource, (3) practice-driven automatization of those lower-level processes, and (4) consequent liberation of attention for higher-order processing. The Focal-Subsidiary-Attention-Distinction describes both. In music as in reading, the art — the meaning-making — only becomes possible after the mechanics become automatic.

#### 27. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Compensation Trap and Its Motivational Consequences *(Line 337)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Compensation Trap and Its Motivational Consequences
> The compensation trap has a particularly damaging motivational dimension. Students in the trap have learned, implicitly, that careful slow reading is the response to comprehension difficulty. But careful slow reading — precisely because it consumes the attentional resources needed for comprehension — does not actually solve the problem. The student reads carefully, still doesn't understand, and attributes the failure to insufficient effort or intelligence rather than to insufficient automaticity. This attribution error — "I'm not smart enough to understand" rather than "my decoding is still not automatic enough to free my comprehension resources" — is academically and motivationally devastating. The compensation trap manufactures [[Attribution-Theory|attributional]] patterns that generalize far beyond reading.

#### 30. [BEST-PRACTICE] The 80/20 Reading Principle for Fluency Development *(Line 360)*

> [!best-practice] The 80/20 Reading Principle for Fluency Development
> Research on independent reading and fluency development suggests that students developing automaticity benefit from the "Goldilocks" text level: texts they can read with approximately 95-98% accuracy (instructional-independent level) rather than frustration-level texts. This range — accurate enough that errors don't overwhelm, challenging enough that novel words are encountered — creates the conditions for both [[Schema-Automation]] (practice with frequent patterns) and vocabulary growth. Reading at frustration level (below 90% accuracy) consumes working memory for error management and produces less automaticity development despite more apparent effort.

#### 31. [BEST-PRACTICE] Silent vs. Oral Reading: A Non-Obvious Implication *(Line 365)*

> [!best-practice] Silent vs. Oral Reading: A Non-Obvious Implication
> The working memory mechanism implies a counterintuitive finding for assessment: struggling readers' comprehension may be *worse* on silent reading than on oral reading of the same text, because oral reading requires maintenance of the phonological representation (keeping the "voice" of reading active in the phonological loop) even when working memory would otherwise allow the trace to fade. This means that oral reading fluency assessments, while valuable for fluency measurement, may somewhat *overestimate* comprehension compared to the silent reading conditions in which most academic reading occurs. Assessments should include both oral and silent reading comprehension measures for students suspected of automaticity difficulties.

#### 32. [DEFINITION] Dyslexia (International Dyslexia Association) *(Line 370)*

> [!definition] Dyslexia (International Dyslexia Association)
> Dyslexia is a specific learning disability neurobiological in origin. It is characterized by difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition and by poor spelling and decoding abilities. These difficulties typically result from a deficit in the phonological component of language that is often unexpected in relation to other cognitive abilities and the provision of effective classroom instruction.

#### 33. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What Dyslexia Research Reveals About Automaticity *(Line 375)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What Dyslexia Research Reveals About Automaticity
> The dyslexia literature provides a kind of natural experiment for the automaticity-liberation hypothesis. Longitudinal studies of individuals with dyslexia who receive effective phonics instruction show interesting dissociations: some achieve high accuracy (low error rates on oral reading) but persistent slow rate; their comprehension, on timed tasks, remains impaired even though their accuracy is normal. This "accuracy-without-fluency" pattern — which would be invisible to accuracy-focused assessment — is specifically predicted by the working memory bottleneck mechanism. It also implies that effective reading disability treatment must target automaticity specifically, not merely accuracy — a distinction that remains underemphasized in much educational practice.

#### 34. [WARNING] The Speed-Reading Fallacy *(Line 380)*

> [!warning] The Speed-Reading Fallacy
> A pervasive misconception holds that fluency training is primarily about reading faster. The mechanism reveals why this is backwards: speed is a *consequence* of automaticity, not the target. Training students to read faster without building the underlying schema automation — as some speed-reading programs do, by promoting regression elimination and reducing subvocalization — tends to impair comprehension rather than liberate it. The bottleneck is in the automaticity of recognition, not in the surface behavior of rate. Addressing the surface behavior without addressing the underlying mechanism produces comprehension failures that look exactly like the problem fluency intervention was meant to solve.

#### 35. [WARNING] The Prosody-Without-Comprehension Problem *(Line 383)*

> [!warning] The Prosody-Without-Comprehension Problem
> Prosodic fluency — reading with appropriate expression — can be trained without comprehension. Students who practice oral reading can learn to mimic the prosodic patterns of skilled reading without building the syntactic parsing and semantic integration that prosody is supposed to reflect. This produces readers who *sound* fluent but are not comprehending — a phenomenon related to the [[Fluency-Illusion]] in cognitive psychology. Assessment of prosody must be accompanied by comprehension checks to avoid mistaking performed fluency for genuine cognitive fluency.

#### 37. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] Cognitive Bandwidth Liberation as the Unifying Mechanism *(Line 415)*

> [!original-synthesis] Cognitive Bandwidth Liberation as the Unifying Mechanism
> The multiple mechanisms identified in Phase IV — phonological trace decay, schema automation, spreading activation dissipation, the compensation trap — can be unified under a single architectural principle: **reading fluency is a cognitive bandwidth liberation problem**. The human cognitive architecture has a fixed attentional bandwidth. Word recognition, when non-automatic, consumes bandwidth that should be available for meaning-making. The educational project of fluency development is not primarily about reading faster — it is about expanding the effective cognitive bandwidth available for comprehension by driving the resource cost of decoding toward zero. This framing unifies the [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]] perspective (reducing extraneous load to free resources for germane processing) with the [[Dual-Process-Theory]] perspective ([[Type-1-Processing|Type 1]] automatic recognition enabling [[Type-2-Processing|Type 2]] deliberate meaning-making) and the [[Focal-Subsidiary-Attention-Distinction]] (from words-as-focal to words-as-subsidiary). The common structure in all three is the same liberation of higher-order capacity through automatization of lower-order processes.

#### 38. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Fluency-Metacognition Paradox *(Line 418)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Fluency-Metacognition Paradox
> A second, deeper synthesis emerges from connecting the automaticity framework to [[Metacognition]]. [[Comprehension-Monitoring]] — the metacognitive ability to detect when comprehension has failed — itself requires working memory resources. Ironically, disfluent readers — the readers whose comprehension is most likely to fail — have the least available working memory for detecting that it has failed. They are comprehending poorly, but their cognitive load from decoding leaves insufficient residual capacity to notice they are comprehending poorly. This creates a devastating double failure: impaired comprehension AND impaired monitoring of comprehension. The [[Fluency-Illusion]] described in metacognitive psychology has a structural parallel here — but instead of falsely believing one understands something just read, the disfluent reader falsely believes one doesn't understand when in fact the problem is decoding automaticity, not comprehension ability per se. The therapeutic implication is that comprehension strategy instruction is insufficient for truly disfluent readers because the metacognitive capacity required to execute comprehension strategies is also in short supply. The bottleneck must be addressed before metacognitive interventions can take hold.

> [!key-takeaway] The Central Analytical Contribution

This framing is not merely terminologically new; it has implications for assessment (target automaticity, not accuracy), for instruction (target volume and schema automation, not accuracy or speed per se), for reading disability (automaticity failure is a distinct clinical profile from language comprehension failure), and for understanding why early fluency gaps compound (the Matthew Effect is cognitively guaranteed, not merely motivationally mediated).

#### 41. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Automaticity in Reading → Mathematical Reasoning *(Line 441)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Automaticity in Reading → Mathematical Reasoning
> The principle of automatization liberating attentional bandwidth operates in mathematical reasoning with the same architectural clarity as in reading. A student who must effortfully reconstruct multiplication facts during multi-step problem solving has their working memory consumed by arithmetic retrieval — leaving insufficient resources for the algebraic or logical reasoning that is the actual point of the task. Research on mathematical fluency shows that students with automatized arithmetic facts produce better performance on complex problem solving *even when calculators are permitted*, because the cognitive benefit of automatization is not merely computation time — it is the working memory freed for higher-order mathematical reasoning. The [[Schema-Automation]] mechanism is identical: frequent exposure automates fact retrieval as schema chunks, enabling higher-order processing to proceed without a resource bottleneck.

#### 42. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Automaticity in Reading → Programming Fluency *(Line 444)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Automaticity in Reading → Programming Fluency
> Novice programmers consume enormous working memory on syntax — remembering that loops require colons, that function definitions precede calls, that parentheses must be balanced. Expert programmers do not think about syntax; they think about algorithm structure and design patterns, with syntax operating automatically in the background. The transition from novice to expert programming is, mechanistically, a transition from syntax-focal to design-focal attention — driven by exactly the [[Focal-Subsidiary-Attention-Distinction]] that characterizes reading fluency development. The practical implication is identical: programming education should include high-volume syntax practice with familiar patterns to automatize them, freeing attention for algorithmic reasoning.

#### 43. [BEST-PRACTICE] Application Bridge: Identifying Automaticity Bottlenecks in Skill Instruction *(Line 449)*

> [!best-practice] Application Bridge: Identifying Automaticity Bottlenecks in Skill Instruction
> In any domain where you are teaching a hierarchical skill, conduct a systematic audit for automaticity bottlenecks: identify the lower-level processes that the higher-order task requires, test whether learners can perform those processes automatically (accurately *and* without apparent effort), and target fluency-building practice specifically at any that are not yet automatic. The key adaptation from reading: unlike reading, many domains permit practice of lower-level components in isolated drill (arithmetic facts, syntax, music scales) that is more efficient than reading's whole-text approach.

#### 44. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Reading Fluency → Writing Composition *(Line 456)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Reading Fluency → Writing Composition
> Bereiter and Scardamalia's foundational work on writing identified a "knowledge-telling" vs. "knowledge-transforming" distinction that maps precisely onto the bottleneck principle. Knowledge-telling writers (typically less skilled) write what they know sequentially, driven by content retrieval. Knowledge-transforming writers (skilled) simultaneously manage the content domain and the rhetorical domain — restructuring knowledge to serve argument. Research shows that knowledge-telling is the default strategy when writers are under high cognitive load from lower-level writing processes (spelling, punctuation, sentence construction). When lower-level processes are automated, the cognitive resources for knowledge-transforming writing become available. The reading fluency mechanism operates in writing with the same architecture: automatized mechanics → available bandwidth → higher-order composing processes.

#### 45. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Matthew Effect in Reading → Matthew Effect in Vocabulary and World Knowledge *(Line 467)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Matthew Effect in Reading → Matthew Effect in Vocabulary and World Knowledge
> Stanovich's own analysis extended the Matthew Effect beyond reading fluency to vocabulary and world knowledge. Fluent readers encounter more words, across more domains, and build larger vocabularies and richer background knowledge — which makes future reading easier, more productive, and more intrinsically motivating. The structural analogue in any domain of declarative knowledge is identical: early fluency in processing a domain's vocabulary and conceptual schemas determines both the *rate* of future learning (more efficient processing) and the *probability* of future engagement (more enjoyable experience). Early knowledge begets subsequent knowledge acquisition in a compounding asymmetry that is architecturally guaranteed, not merely probabilistically likely.

#### 49. [DEFINITION] Automaticity (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974) *(Line 517)*

> [!definition] Automaticity (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974)
> A cognitive process is automatic when it executes rapidly (sub-300ms), obligatorily (without requiring conscious intention), and without drawing on limited attentional resources that would otherwise be available for concurrent processing. In reading, automaticity refers specifically to word recognition — the visual recognition of printed words that activates phonological and semantic representations without deliberate decoding effort. Distinguished from accuracy (correctness) by the resource-free criterion: automatic processes can occur simultaneously with other attentional demands without mutual interference.

#### 50. [DEFINITION] Oral Reading Fluency (ORF) *(Line 520)*

> [!definition] Oral Reading Fluency (ORF)
> The integrated ability to read connected text aloud with accuracy (low error rate), appropriate rate (words per minute), and prosody (expression, phrasing, and intonation reflecting comprehension of grammatical and semantic structure). ORF assessments, typically 1-minute timed passages, yield a Words Correct Per Minute (WCPM) score that is among the strongest brief predictors of reading comprehension. WCPM does not capture prosody and systematically underestimates fluency problems in accurate-but-slow readers.

#### 51. [DEFINITION] Simple View of Reading (SVR) (Gough & Tunmer, 1986) *(Line 523)*

> [!definition] Simple View of Reading (SVR) (Gough & Tunmer, 1986)
> The theoretical framework that posits Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Language Comprehension. The multiplicative formulation implies that weakness in either component drives comprehension to zero regardless of strength in the other. The SVR identifies two distinct reading disability profiles: decoding failure (dyslexia) and language comprehension failure (hyperlexia or specific language comprehension disorders).

#### 52. [DEFINITION] Verbal Efficiency Theory (Perfetti, 1985) *(Line 526)*

> [!definition] Verbal Efficiency Theory (Perfetti, 1985)
> The theoretical account proposing that skilled reading depends on the speed and quality of local text processes — specifically word identification and the maintenance of decoded word representations in working memory long enough for integration into propositional and discourse representations. Verbal inefficiency (slow, resource-costly decoding) impairs comprehension not through inaccuracy but through the fading of phonological traces before integration can occur.

#### 53. [DEFINITION] Matthew Effect in Reading (Stanovich, 1986) *(Line 529)*

> [!definition] Matthew Effect in Reading (Stanovich, 1986)
> The compounding inequality in reading development whereby early reading skill advantages generate increased reading volume, vocabulary growth, and world knowledge, creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates skill development in already-skilled readers — while early skill deficits generate reading avoidance, reduced word exposure, and slower vocabulary and knowledge growth, creating a negative feedback loop. Named after the biblical observation that "to he who has, more shall be given."

#### 54. [DEFINITION] Phonological Loop (Baddeley's Working Memory Model) *(Line 532)*

> [!definition] Phonological Loop (Baddeley's Working Memory Model)
> The [[Phonological-Loop]] is the component of working memory that maintains verbal information in a phonological (sound-based) code through active rehearsal. It holds approximately 3-4 items for approximately 2 seconds without rehearsal. In reading, the phonological loop maintains the sound representations of recently decoded words while subsequent words are processed — making it the critical resource for sentence-level integration. Non-automatic decoding consumes phonological loop resources, leaving insufficient capacity for maintaining sentence-length phonological representations.

#### 55. [DEFINITION] Prosody (in oral reading) *(Line 535)*

> [!definition] Prosody (in oral reading)
> The suprasegmental features of spoken language — including pitch variation, stress, rhythm, tempo, and phrasing — applied to oral reading. Prosodic oral reading requires simultaneous processing of syntactic structure and semantic content during production, making appropriate prosody both a marker of comprehension and potentially a mechanism that scaffolds syntactic parsing. Prosodic fluency assessment goes beyond rate and accuracy to capture the quality dimension of fluent reading.

#### 56. [DEFINITION] Decoding *(Line 538)*

> [!definition] Decoding
> The translation of printed symbols (graphemes) into their phonological equivalents (phonemes) and from there to word-level representations. Decoding is the primary focus of early reading instruction and is the foundation on which fluency and comprehension are built. Decoding accuracy (getting the correct phonological representation) is distinguished from decoding automaticity (getting it without consuming working memory resources).

#### 57. [DEFINITION] Repeated Reading (Samuels, 1979) *(Line 541)*

> [!definition] Repeated Reading (Samuels, 1979)
> An evidence-based fluency intervention in which students read short passages multiple times until reaching a fluency criterion (typically a target WCPM score), with corrective feedback from a tutor or teacher. Samuels demonstrated that repeated reading produces not only fluency gains on practiced passages but generalization to unpracticed text, indicating that practice builds transferable decoding efficiency rather than merely word-specific memorization.

#### 58. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Bandwidth Liberation *(Line 544)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Bandwidth Liberation
> An original analytical synthesis from this report. The principle that the educational project of fluency development is fundamentally about expanding the effective cognitive bandwidth available for comprehension by driving the resource cost of decoding toward zero — achieved through the automatization of orthographic schemas via high-volume practice. Unifies [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]], [[Dual-Process-Theory]], and [[Focal-Subsidiary-Attention-Distinction]] accounts of fluency under a single architectural principle.

#### 59. [DEFINITION] Compensation Trap *(Line 547)*

> [!definition] Compensation Trap
> A pattern in which a reader with inadequate decoding automaticity compensates by slowing down to maintain accuracy, producing low error rates on oral reading assessments while sustaining the working memory bottleneck and its comprehension consequences. The compensation trap is educationally dangerous because it renders the student invisible to accuracy-based screening while the Matthew Effect runs in the negative direction.

#### 60. [DEFINITION] Dyslexia (International Dyslexia Association) *(Line 550)*

> [!definition] Dyslexia (International Dyslexia Association)
> A specific learning disability neurobiological in origin characterized by difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition and by poor spelling and decoding abilities. These difficulties typically result from a deficit in the phonological component of language that is often unexpected in relation to other cognitive abilities and the provision of effective classroom instruction. Secondary consequences include reduced reading experience and reduced vocabulary and background knowledge growth.
