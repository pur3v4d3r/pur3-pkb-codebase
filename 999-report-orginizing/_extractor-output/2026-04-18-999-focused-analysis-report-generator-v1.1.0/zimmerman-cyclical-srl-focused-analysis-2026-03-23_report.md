#### 3. [DEFINITION] Zimmerman's Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning** (Zimmerman, 2000) *(Line 171)*

> [!definition] Zimmerman's Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning** (Zimmerman, 2000)
> A [[Social-Cognitive-Theory-—-Bandura|social cognitive]] account of self-regulated learning organized as a three-phase cycle — Forethought, Performance, and Self-Reflection — each containing two sub-process categories. The cyclical claim asserts that self-reflection outputs directly shape subsequent forethought inputs, making the cycle self-modifying: learners who close the cycle with accurate adaptive inferences enter the next forethought phase with calibrated expectations, while learners who close with defensive inferences enter the next forethought phase with distorted ones.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Self-Efficacy** (Bandura, 1977, 1986) *(Line 182)*

> [!definition] Self-Efficacy** (Bandura, 1977, 1986)
> Domain-specific and task-specific beliefs about one's capacity to execute the actions required to produce particular outcomes at particular performance levels. Self-efficacy is not global confidence — it is granular, contextual, and *updateable* through the SRL cycle's self-reflection phase. Its role in the cyclical model is that of a state variable: it enters the forethought phase as an input, shapes the quality of task analysis, and is revised by the self-reflection phase based on attributed performance outcomes.

#### 5. [KEY-CLAIM] The Phase-Transition Claim *(Line 201)*

> [!key-claim] The Phase-Transition Claim
> The output of the self-reflection phase (specifically: the self-satisfaction valence and the adaptive/defensive inference) becomes a direct input to the subsequent forethought phase, shaping (1) the specificity and ambition of task analysis in the next learning cycle, (2) the strength and accuracy of self-efficacy beliefs entering that cycle, and (3) the motivational orientation (mastery vs. defensive performance-avoidance) the learner brings to the next forethought phase. The cycle is not merely temporal — it is functionally connected. Break the connection at the self-reflection-to-forethought junction, and you have three independent processes, not a self-improving cycle.

#### 8. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Monitoring-Control Decoupling Problem *(Line 233)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Monitoring-Control Decoupling Problem
> The accumulated evidence from [[Monitoring-Control-Coupling]] research does not suggest that the primary failure mode in SRL is inadequate monitoring. Many students are reasonably accurate at detecting that their comprehension is failing or that their strategy isn't working. The primary failure mode is the *control* component: translating accurate monitoring signals into meaningful regulatory action. Students often know something is wrong but do not change what they are doing. This pattern suggests the bottleneck is not in the performance phase's self-observation sub-process but in the self-reflection phase's production of adaptive inferences — the cycle's regulatory junction.

#### 9. [EVIDENCE] Zimmerman & Kitsantas (1997): The Process-vs.-Outcome Goal Study *(Line 242)*

> [!evidence] Zimmerman & Kitsantas (1997): The Process-vs.-Outcome Goal Study
> Zimmerman and Kitsantas's study of novice and expert dart throwers provides direct experimental evidence for the self-reflection-to-forethought transition mechanism. Students assigned to process goals (focusing on technique) who were instructed to shift to outcome goals after achieving automaticity showed not only superior performance but — critically — higher self-efficacy beliefs in post-task forethought measures, indicating that their self-reflection produced stronger adaptive inferences that carried forward to subsequent forethought. Students maintaining only outcome goals showed weaker self-efficacy updating. The study demonstrates that the *type* of self-reflection content influences subsequent forethought quality — precisely the cycle's transition claim.

#### 10. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Calibration vs. Motivation Tension *(Line 251)*

> [!tension-identified] The Calibration vs. Motivation Tension
> There is a genuine tension in the SRL literature about whether accurate self-efficacy calibration or high (even somewhat overcalibrated) self-efficacy better predicts SRL development. Bandura consistently argued that slightly overestimated self-efficacy is motivationally adaptive — it sustains effort in the face of setbacks. Zimmerman's model implies that *accurate* self-efficacy updating is what makes the cycle self-correcting. These positions are not incompatible, but they pull in different directions when designing feedback for struggling learners: should feedback aim to recalibrate self-efficacy accurately, or to maintain it above-floor for motivational purposes? The evidence does not cleanly resolve this tension, and different learner populations may require different approaches.

#### 15. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Defensive Closure Paradox *(Line 287)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Defensive Closure Paradox
> Defensive inferences are not simply a failure of self-reflection — they are an active regulatory function that protects the learner from the costs of accurate self-assessment. A student who correctly attributes a failing grade to inadequate study strategies faces the obligation to actually change how they study, which is cognitively and motivationally expensive. A student who attributes the same failure to the instructor's unfairness faces no such obligation. The defensive inference is, in this sense, a locally rational response to the prospect of costly change. This means that simply teaching attribution accuracy is insufficient: it creates the accurate self-assessment without addressing the motivational cost of acting on it. SRL interventions that target attribution without simultaneously reducing the cost of strategy change will produce accurate self-assessors who continue to underperform.

#### 16. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Asymmetric Risk in Self-Efficacy Updating *(Line 296)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Asymmetric Risk in Self-Efficacy Updating
> There is an asymmetry in the self-efficacy updating mechanism that is not often noted. Under-attribution of success (defensive: "I just got lucky") produces a failure to update self-efficacy upward, which is relatively benign — the learner simply doesn't gain the self-efficacy benefit of their success. Over-attribution of failure (defensive: "I'm just not good at this") produces a self-efficacy decrease based on a single outcome attributed to stable ability. Because ability attributions are to a *stable* cause, this decrease generalizes across future occasions, not just to the specific task and strategy that produced the failure. The downside asymmetry is worse: defensive inferences after failure produce more durable damage to self-efficacy than defensive inferences after success. This asymmetry predicts that the self-reflection phase's malfunction is more consequential for learners who frequently encounter challenging tasks — exactly the learners most in need of effective SRL.

#### 17. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Negative Feedback Loops in Control Theory → Zimmerman's SRL Cycle *(Line 309)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Negative Feedback Loops in Control Theory → Zimmerman's SRL Cycle
> The SRL cycle's structure is formally equivalent to a [[Negative-Feedback-Control-System]]: a reference input (the goal set in forethought), a comparator (self-evaluation in self-reflection that compares observed performance to the reference), and a control signal (adaptive inferences that modify strategy selection for the next forethought phase). In control engineering, a feedback system's stability and responsiveness depend critically on the *gain* of the feedback loop — how strongly a detected error translates into corrective action. The monitoring-control coupling problem in SRL is precisely an insufficient loop gain problem: error detection (monitoring) does not produce sufficiently strong control signals (adaptive inferences and strategy modification). The control engineering literature offers solutions to insufficient loop gain problems — proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control tuning — that map interestingly onto the SRL literature on scaffolding strategy, pacing, and calibrated challenge. The structural isomorphism is not decorative; it suggests that the field might benefit from more formal treatment of SRL cycle dynamics.

#### 18. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Degenerate Cycle: How Most Learning Episodes Are Actually Organized *(Line 324)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Degenerate Cycle: How Most Learning Episodes Are Actually Organized
> Zimmerman's full three-phase cycle with high-quality phase transitions is not the default mode of most learners. Most learning episodes, especially in formal education, instantiate what might be called a *degenerate cycle* — one in which the learner enters the performance phase without meaningful forethought (no specific goal, no deliberate strategy selection) and exits with global self-evaluation ("I got a B" or "That was hard") rather than causal, strategy-focused self-reflection. The degenerate cycle's output to the subsequent forethought phase is minimal: no recalibrated self-efficacy, no updated strategy selection, no modified goal specificity. It is a cycle in name only — three phases that succeed each other temporally without the functional connections that make the cycle self-improving. The implication is significant: the apparent failure of SRL training to generalize may not reflect a failure of strategy knowledge transfer but a failure to establish non-degenerate cycles across domains. Students who know the strategies but execute degenerate cycles in every domain will not develop cross-domain SRL competence regardless of how many strategies they have been taught.

#### 20. [BEST-PRACTICE] Self-Reflection Before Strategy: Restructuring the Instructional Sequence *(Line 347)*

> [!best-practice] Self-Reflection Before Strategy: Restructuring the Instructional Sequence
> An SRL intervention that begins by teaching self-reflection quality — specifically, training learners to make specific, strategy-focused causal attributions after performance outcomes — creates the conditions under which newly acquired strategy knowledge can be incorporated adaptively. Contrast this with the standard sequence (teach strategies → practice → debrief). The standard sequence teaches content for the forethought phase but trusts that learners will naturally engage in adaptive self-reflection. The evidence suggests they will not, absent explicit instruction. The alternative sequence: (1) establish baseline understanding of the cycle's architecture; (2) train attribution specificity and controllability; (3) then introduce strategy repertoire. This sequence ensures learners have the cycle-closure mechanism in place before filling the forethought phase's strategy library.

#### 21. [BEST-PRACTICE] Feedback Design for Cycle Closure *(Line 354)*

> [!best-practice] Feedback Design for Cycle Closure
> Feedback designed to close the SRL cycle adaptively should include three components: (1) *performance information* — what the learner actually did relative to the reference standard (supporting self-evaluation); (2) *attribution information* — a specific causal account of the performance outcome in terms of controllable strategy use (supporting accurate self-judgment); and (3) *adaptive inference scaffolding* — a specific recommendation for how to modify the approach for the next attempt (scaffolding the adaptive inference process). Feedback that provides only performance information (e.g., a grade) closes the cycle evaluatively but not adaptively. See [[Feedback-Design-for-Autonomy-and-Mastery]] for complementary design principles from the autonomy-support tradition.

#### 22. [WARNING] The Temporal Scale Assumption *(Line 365)*

> [!warning] The Temporal Scale Assumption
> Zimmerman's model was developed and validated primarily with discrete, bounded tasks (writing, mathematics, dart throwing, music practice). Its application to extended, complex, open-ended learning (learning a programming language, developing professional expertise) involves temporal assumptions that have not been adequately tested. The self-reflection phase's adaptive inference output may have different properties at different timescales: immediate inferences after a 30-minute study session may be more accurate and behaviorally specific than inferences drawn at the end of a semester. Research on SRL in complex domains needs to address the temporal boundary conditions of the cyclical architecture explicitly.

#### 24. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The SRL Cycle as a Calibration Engine *(Line 396)*

> [!original-synthesis] The SRL Cycle as a Calibration Engine
> Zimmerman's cyclical model is most productively understood not as a *strategy execution cycle* but as a *calibration engine* — a system whose primary product is not the learning outcomes of individual episodes but the progressive refinement of the learner's internal models of their own capabilities, effective strategies, and task structure. The cycle's goal is not to execute tasks correctly but to update the learner's cognitive and motivational models so that subsequent cycles are executed with higher quality. This reframing has three implications: (1) the measure of a successful learning episode is not performance outcome but the accuracy of calibration update — a learner who performs perfectly on an easy task without learning anything about their strategy effectiveness has run an unproductive cycle, while a learner who performs poorly but accurately attributes the failure to a specific controllable strategy deficiency has run a highly productive cycle; (2) instruction should be designed to maximize calibration update per cycle, not performance per episode, which means deliberately introducing sufficient challenge and informative failure; (3) assessment should include measures of self-efficacy and attribution accuracy, not only performance outcomes, since these are the indicators of calibration engine health.
> 
> This reframing resolves a persistent puzzle in the SRL literature: why do some highly successful students have poorly developed SRL? The calibration engine framing predicts this: students whose academic success is achieved through talent, topic interest, or supportive contexts do not need calibration engine maintenance — tasks are too easy to generate informative failure. Their self-reflection is always positive, their adaptive inferences are vacuous ("keep doing what I'm doing"), and their calibration engine runs but does not develop. The first time these students encounter a task at the genuine edge of their capability, their calibration engine is underdeveloped precisely because success has precluded its exercise.

#### 25. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Institutional Misalignment *(Line 407)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Institutional Misalignment
> There is a structural misalignment between Zimmerman's cycle and the institutional structure of formal education. Educational institutions invest most assessment and instructional attention in the forethought phase (teaching content and skills) and the performance phase (testing and evaluating). The self-reflection phase — the cycle's developmental engine — is institutionally derelict: grades are issued without attribution scaffolding, the next unit begins before meaningful self-reflection on the previous unit has occurred, and the forethought phase of the next unit is therefore populated with undifferentiated self-efficacy beliefs and unrevised strategy selection. The SRL cycle is institutionally degenerate at scale, which means that the institutions most responsible for developing SRL are structurally organized in ways that prevent the cycle's self-improving mechanism from operating.

#### 27. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Deliberate Practice (Ericsson) → Zimmerman's SRL Cycle *(Line 428)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Deliberate Practice (Ericsson) → Zimmerman's SRL Cycle
> The most direct structural analogue is [[Deliberate-Practice|deliberate practice]] as theorized by [[Ericsson]] and colleagues (1993, 2006). Deliberate practice's defining feature is not the amount of practice but the quality of the feedback loop: experts engage in practice specifically designed to produce informative errors, receive immediate corrective feedback, and engage in structured reflection that modifies subsequent practice design. This is exactly the non-degenerate SRL cycle operating at high frequency. The deliberate practice literature's claim that expert performance develops through "10,000 hours" is more precisely stated as "10,000 hours of non-degenerate cycles with high-quality cycle closure" — a fact that explains why mere time-on-task without structured reflection does not produce expertise. The transfer principle: any domain where sustained performance improvement is the goal requires structuring practice as non-degenerate cycles with explicit cycle-closure mechanisms.
> 
> The analogy holds structurally: in deliberate practice, the "self-reflection phase" is the mentor/coach feedback and the performer's internal assessment; the "forethought phase" is the deliberate structuring of the next practice session; the "performance phase" is the practice execution itself. Systems that invest only in the performance phase without the structured cycle-closure mechanism plateau.

#### 28. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Argyris's Double-Loop Learning → Zimmerman's Adaptive/Defensive Inference *(Line 441)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Argyris's Double-Loop Learning → Zimmerman's Adaptive/Defensive Inference
> [[Donald-Schön]] and [[Double-Loop-Learning|Argyris's double-loop learning]] model (Argyris & Schön, 1978) identifies an analogous fork at the organizational level. Single-loop learning corrects detected errors within existing governing values and strategies — it is the organizational equivalent of Zimmerman's adaptive inference that modifies strategies without questioning the goal framework. Double-loop learning examines and potentially revises the governing values themselves — the organizational equivalent of an adaptive inference so fundamental that it revises the forethought phase's goal-setting framework, not just its strategy selection. Argyris's crucial observation — that organizations systematically favor defensive routines (producing "skilled incompetence") over genuine double-loop learning — is structurally parallel to Zimmerman's individual-level observation that learners favor defensive inferences over adaptive ones when stakes are high. The [[Single-Loop-Learning-vs.-Double-Loop-Learning|single/double-loop]] distinction maps onto Zimmerman's strategy-updating versus goal-structure-updating distinction with remarkable precision.
> 
> The cross-domain insight: defensive closure in the SRL cycle (attributing failure to uncontrollable causes to avoid the cost of strategic change) is the individual-level version of the organizational defensive routines that Argyris documented extensively. Both are rational responses to the high cost of genuine learning; both systematically prevent the development of adaptive capability.

#### 29. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Project Management → Zimmerman's Monitoring-Control Gap *(Line 452)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Project Management → Zimmerman's Monitoring-Control Gap
> Project management research documents a structural analogue: projects that have sophisticated monitoring systems (earned value tracking, Gantt chart variance detection) but lack effective change control processes consistently overrun despite early detection of schedule slippage. The monitoring systems detect schedule variance accurately; the control system (decision-making authority to revise scope, timeline, or resources) is insufficiently responsive to monitoring signals. This is the organizational-level monitoring-control decoupling problem. The transfer principle: monitoring system sophistication is not the limiting factor in adaptive systems — the limiting factor is the gain of the monitoring-to-control signal pathway.
> 
> The practical application: before investing in improved monitoring (better self-assessment tools, more frequent formative assessment, more sophisticated tracking), determine whether the control side of the system (the ability and motivation to act on monitoring signals) is the actual bottleneck. In many educational settings, learners have more monitoring data than they can act on adaptively, suggesting that the intervention priority is improving control system responsiveness, not monitoring fidelity.

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning** (Zimmerman, 2000) *(Line 518)*

> [!definition] Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning** (Zimmerman, 2000)
> **Definition:** A three-phase model of [[Self-Regulated-Learning]] — Forethought, Performance, and Self-Reflection — organized as a feedback cycle in which each phase's outputs function as inputs to subsequent phases. The cyclical claim (that self-reflection quality shapes subsequent forethought quality) distinguishes it from sequential or modular accounts of SRL.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The cycle's architectural claim — that phase-transition quality, not phase-content quality alone, determines SRL development — is the central analytical focus of this report.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** A learner is operating a high-quality cycle if, after a performance episode, they can identify specific causal explanations for their performance outcome in terms of controllable strategies, and if they can describe specific modifications to their next forethought phase based on that attribution. The absence of these indicators signals degenerate cycle operation.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** Developed by [[Barry-Zimmerman]] within [[Bandura]]'s social cognitive framework, integrating Bandura's self-efficacy theory with Flavell's metacognitive model and Weiner's attribution theory. Emerged through studies of expert performers (athletes, writers, musicians) in the 1990s.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Adaptive Inference** (Zimmerman, 2000) *(Line 529)*

> [!definition] Adaptive Inference** (Zimmerman, 2000)
> **Definition:** A post-performance self-regulatory decision, produced during the self-reflection phase's self-reaction sub-process, to modify learning approaches for future attempts based on accurate, controllable causal attributions. Distinguished from defensive inference by its willingness to implicate modifiable factors (effort, strategy choice) rather than uncontrollable factors (ability, task difficulty).
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The adaptive/defensive inference fork is identified as the cycle's primary regulatory valve — the point at which the cycle either compounds self-regulatory capability or stagnates.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Adaptive inference is present when a learner, after a suboptimal performance, identifies a specific strategy they used, evaluates why it was insufficient, and commits to a specific alternative strategy for the next attempt. Defensive inference is present when the explanation is in terms of external or stable factors without strategy modification commitment.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** Zimmerman derived the adaptive/defensive distinction from [[Attribution-Retraining]] research (Dweck, 1975; Försterling, 1985) and [[Bandura]]'s treatment of protective self-efficacy mechanisms.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Self-Reflection-Phase]], [[Attribution-Retraining]], [[Self-Efficacy]]

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Degenerate Cycle** (This Report — Original Terminology) *(Line 540)*

> [!definition] Degenerate Cycle** (This Report — Original Terminology)
> **Definition:** An SRL cycle in which the functional connections between phases are absent or minimal — the learner enters the performance phase without meaningful goal setting or strategic planning (no forethought), and exits with global evaluative self-reflection ("that was hard/easy") rather than causal, strategy-specific self-reflection. A degenerate cycle proceeds through three temporal phases without the inter-phase signal transmission that makes the cycle self-modifying.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The degenerate cycle is proposed as the primary explanation for why SRL training often fails to generalize: learners who have been taught strategies but run degenerate cycles in every domain cannot compound self-regulatory capability across contexts regardless of their strategy knowledge.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Indicators of degenerate cycle operation: absence of stated goals before beginning a task; inability to explain post-performance *why* one performed as one did in terms of specific controllable choices; no strategy modification between successive attempts at the same type of task.

#### 42. [TENSION] Accurate Calibration vs. Motivationally Optimal Self-Efficacy *(Line 607)*

> [!tension] Accurate Calibration vs. Motivationally Optimal Self-Efficacy
> **Position A (Accurate Calibration):** The cyclical model's adaptive functioning requires that self-efficacy beliefs be updated *accurately* based on attributed performance outcomes. Calibration accuracy — the alignment between self-efficacy and actual capability — is the hallmark of a well-functioning calibration engine.
> 
> **Position B (Motivationally Optimal Overestimation):** Bandura consistently argued that slightly optimistic self-efficacy — somewhat higher than objective performance data would warrant — is motivationally adaptive. It sustains effort in the face of setbacks without producing the paralysis associated with self-efficacy that has tracked every failure accurately.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The evidence suggests both claims have validity in different contexts and for different learner populations. For learners with strong domain interest and high prior self-efficacy, accurate calibration is safe and developmentally productive. For learners with fragile self-efficacy or low domain interest, some degree of protective overcalibration may be motivationally necessary even at the cost of calibration accuracy.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** The tension has direct instructional implications: should formative feedback aim to recalibrate self-efficacy accurately (supporting calibration engine health) or to maintain it above threshold (supporting motivation)? These goals can conflict when accurate feedback reveals significant performance deficits.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** This analysis argues that the tension is real but context-dependent, and that the primary design goal should be creating conditions where accurate calibration does not threaten self-efficacy below functional levels — typically by attributing failure to strategies rather than to self (the [[Attribution-Retraining]] approach), which allows accuracy without catastrophic self-efficacy updating.

#### 55. [PROTOCOL] The Adaptive Cycle Closure Protocol: A Structured Self-Reflection Sequence *(Line 721)*

> [!protocol] The Adaptive Cycle Closure Protocol: A Structured Self-Reflection Sequence
> **Context:** Use after any significant learning episode (a study session, a test, a performance, a meeting) where you want to ensure the cycle closes adaptively rather than degenerately or defensively.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Performance Reconstruction** (5 minutes): Without evaluation, reconstruct what you actually did during the learning episode. What strategies did you use? How much effort did you invest? What was your attention quality? (Builds self-observation data for attribution.)
> 2. **Outcome Assessment** (2 minutes): Compare your actual outcome to your goal. What was the gap, if any? Be specific: not "I didn't do well" but "I understood the main argument but couldn't recall specific evidence."
> 3. **Causal Attribution** (5 minutes): Explain the gap (or its absence) in terms of specific, controllable factors. Which strategy choices contributed to the outcome? Avoid ability and luck attributions. Force yourself to locate at least one controllable factor for any performance shortfall.
> 4. **Adaptive Inference** (3 minutes): Based on your attribution, identify one specific strategy modification for your next attempt. This is the cycle-closing step: write it down and ensure it is specific enough to actually change what you do next time.
> 5. **Self-Efficacy Update** (1 minute): Explicitly state your updated self-efficacy for the relevant task type: "Based on this cycle, I believe I can [specific capability] if I [specific strategy modification]."
> 
> **Success Criteria:** The protocol has closed the cycle adaptively if Step 4 produces a specific, actionable strategy modification (not a vague resolution to "try harder"). If Step 4 produces only a general intention without strategy specificity, return to Step 3 — the attribution was not specific enough.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** The most common failure is completing Steps 1–3 with high quality and then reverting to vague adaptive inferences in Step 4 ("I need to study more"). This is the junction between monitoring accuracy and control response quality — the monitoring-control decoupling problem. If you notice this pattern, set a timer for Step 4 and force yourself to specify *which study strategy* will change, not just *how much* you will study.

> **A:** A degenerate cycle is one in which the functional phase-transition connections are absent: the learner enters the performance phase without meaningful forethought and exits with global evaluative reflection rather than causal, strategy-specific self-reflection. It matters because learners who run degenerate cycles in every domain cannot compound self-regulatory capability across contexts, explaining why SRL training often fails to generalize.
> 
> **Source:** Phase IV, Degenerate Cycle section
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Definition/Application

#### 60. [FLASHCARD] Seed 5 *(Line 779)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 5
> **Q:** What is the "calibration engine" framing of the SRL cycle, and what does it predict about highly successful students?
> 
> **A:** The calibration engine framing holds that the SRL cycle's primary product is not performance outcomes but progressive refinement of the learner's internal models of capability, strategy effectiveness, and task structure. It predicts that highly successful students may have poorly developed SRL if they have never encountered tasks at the genuine edge of their capability — because success without informative failure provides no calibration data and leaves the calibration engine underdeveloped.
>

#### 61. [FLASHCARD] Seed 6 *(Line 789)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 6
> **Q:** What is the attribution dimension most critical for determining whether self-efficacy is updated accurately or defensively after failure?
> 
> **A:** The stability dimension. Attributing failure to stable causes (ability: "I'm just not good at this") produces self-efficacy decreases that generalize across future occasions. Attributing failure to unstable causes (insufficient effort, ineffective strategy) allows accurate downward recalibration for the specific strategy without catastrophic generalization to overall capability.
> 
> **Source:** Phase IV, Self-Efficacy State Variable section
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Process

#### 62. [FLASHCARD] Seed 7 *(Line 799)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 7
> **Q:** How does the deliberate practice framework (Ericsson) serve as a structural analogue for Zimmerman's SRL cycle's phase-transition mechanism?
> 
> **A:** Deliberate practice requires (1) tasks designed to produce informative errors, (2) immediate corrective feedback, and (3) structured reflection that modifies subsequent practice design. This is exactly the non-degenerate SRL cycle: informative performance → quality self-reflection with causal attribution → adaptive inference → modified forethought for next practice episode. The claim "10,000 hours produces expertise" is more precisely "10,000 hours of non-degenerate cycles with high-quality cycle closure."
> 
> **Source:** Phase VII, FAR Transfer — Deliberate Practice analogue
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection
> **Tags:** #deliberate-practice #far-transfer #SRL-cycle #Ericsson

#### 63. [FLASHCARD] Seed 8 *(Line 809)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 8
> **Q:** What makes formative assessment "externally scaffolded cycle closure" within Zimmerman's model?
> 
> **A:** Effective formative feedback compensates for deficient self-reflection by providing the three components that produce adaptive cycle closure: (1) performance information relative to a reference standard; (2) attribution information (specific causal account in terms of controllable strategy use); and (3) adaptive inference scaffolding (specific recommendations for strategy modification). Feedback that provides only outcome information (grades) closes the cycle evaluatively but not adaptively.
> 
> **Source:** Phase V, Formative Assessment section
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Application
> **Tags:** #formative-assessment #feedback-design #SRL-cycle #externally-scaffolded
