#### 3. [DEFINITION] Attribution (Heider, 1958) *(Line 185)*

> [!definition] Attribution (Heider, 1958)
> **Definition:** A causal explanation assigned by an observer (or actor) to account for an outcome — specifically, the assignment of that outcome to an internal source (properties of the person) or an external source (properties of the environment or situation). Attributions are not neutral descriptions; they carry implications about the future, about responsibility, and about appropriate responses.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The basic internal/external distinction is the foundation on which Weiner's three-dimensional model builds — but the analysis will show that locus alone is insufficient to explain the motivational consequences observed in achievement contexts.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** A causal statement qualifies as an internal attribution when the speaker locates causation in the self ("I failed because I'm not good at math") versus an external attribution when causation is located outside ("I failed because the test was unfair").
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Causal-Induction]], [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]], [[Actor-Observer-Bias]]

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Locus of Causality (Weiner, 1979) *(Line 200)*

> [!definition] Locus of Causality (Weiner, 1979)
> **Definition:** The location of a cause as internal to the person (ability, effort, mood, strategy) or external to the person (task difficulty, luck, other people's actions). This dimension is closely related to but distinct from [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]] in SDT — the SDT construct refers to whether the *regulation of behavior* feels internally initiated, whereas Weiner's locus refers to the *location of the cause* of an outcome.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Locus primarily determines the emotional valence of self-directed emotions — specifically pride and shame. Internal attributions for success amplify pride; internal attributions for failure amplify shame. External attributions for either outcome attenuate these self-directed affects.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Ask whether removing the person from the situation would change the probability of the outcome. If yes, the cause is internal; if no, the cause is external.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]], [[Self-Determination-Theory]]

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Stability (Weiner, 1979) *(Line 209)*

> [!definition] Stability (Weiner, 1979)
> **Definition:** The temporal persistence of a cause — whether it is relatively fixed (stable: ability, task difficulty) or variable (unstable: effort, luck, temporary mood). Stability is the dimension that Weiner argued most powerfully determines *expectancy change* — the degree to which an outcome changes a person's prediction about future outcomes.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the analytically central dimension. Stable attributions create rigid expectancies that are resistant to disconfirmation; unstable attributions leave expectancies open to revision. The self-sealing architecture of maladaptive patterns largely runs through this dimension.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Ask whether the cause will likely be present in the next similar situation. Ability and task difficulty are prototypically stable; effort expenditure and luck are prototypically unstable.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Implicit-Theory-of-Intelligence]], [[Incremental-Theory-of-Intelligence]], [[Growth-Mindset-Theory]]

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Controllability (Weiner, 1979) *(Line 218)*

> [!definition] Controllability (Weiner, 1979)
> **Definition:** Whether the cause is subject to volitional control by the actor or by others. Effort is the prototypical controllable cause; ability, luck, and task difficulty are paradigmatically uncontrollable. Critically, controllability is orthogonal to locus — an internal cause (ability) may be uncontrollable, while an external cause (another person's behavior toward you) may be controllable by them.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Controllability is the primary driver of guilt (for controllable failures attributed to self) and anger (for controllable failures attributed to others). It also generates interpersonal moral inferences about the appropriateness of help and pity — a mechanism with significant ethical implications.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Ask whether the actor could have produced a different level of the causal factor through an act of will. If yes, the cause is controllable; if no, it is uncontrollable.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Autonomous-Motivation]], [[Achievement-Goal-Theory]], [[Appraisal-Theory]]

> [!key-claim] The Mediational Core

#### 11. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Stability-Hopelessness Link Is the Strongest *(Line 264)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Stability-Hopelessness Link Is the Strongest
> Across the attribution literature, the stability-expectancy-hopelessness pathway shows the most consistent empirical support. Studies using both experimental manipulations (feedback framed as indicating stable versus unstable ability differences) and correlational designs (measuring [[Attribution-Style]] across individuals) reliably find that stable attributions for negative outcomes predict reduced persistence, lower subsequent effort investment, and affective states consistent with helplessness. The locus and controllability emotion-assignments, while supported, are more susceptible to cultural and individual moderators.

#### 12. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Cognitive-Emotion Priority Problem *(Line 269)*

> [!tension-identified] The Cognitive-Emotion Priority Problem
> A persistent tension in the empirical literature concerns whether the causal attribution genuinely *causes* the emotion (as Weiner's model claims) or whether prior emotional states *determine* the attribution (a mood-congruent attribution account). Studies by Forgas and colleagues using the Affect Infusion Model demonstrate that people in negative moods make more external, unstable attributions for others' behavior — suggesting that emotion influences attribution as much as attribution influences emotion. Weiner's model treats the cognitive appraisal as primary, but the evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship that the standard causal account cannot fully accommodate.

#### 13. [EVIDENCE] The Stigma and Helping Literature *(Line 278)*

> [!evidence] The Stigma and Helping Literature
> Graham and colleagues (1992) showed that the controllability-helping pathway holds even for racially stigmatized conditions — specifically, controllability attributions mediated racial disparities in reported helping intentions. Reisenzein (1986) demonstrated the anger-helping link in behavioral (not just self-report) measures. The robustness of this finding across cultures is more variable — individualistic cultures show stronger controllability effects on helping, consistent with a framework that places more moral weight on personal agency.

#### 14. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Attribution Theory as Hidden Moral Philosophy *(Line 283)*

> [!analytical-insight] Attribution Theory as Hidden Moral Philosophy
> The controllability dimension's function as a moral trigger reveals something important about the structure of folk moral psychology: the boundary between causal explanation and moral judgment is far more permeable than is usually acknowledged. When a person perceives another's obesity as controllable, they are making a causal judgment AND a moral judgment in a single cognitive act — and Weiner's model suggests these two judgments are structurally fused rather than sequentially related. This has significant implications for debates about responsibility, stigma, and policy — domains that are typically treated as involving distinct cognitive operations.

#### 15. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Stability × Globality Interaction Is Clinically Central *(Line 292)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Stability × Globality Interaction Is Clinically Central
> The most powerful predictor of helplessness and depression in the explanatory style literature is not any single dimension but the *stability × globality* interaction: attributing negative events to causes that are both temporally persistent AND cross-situationally general (e.g., "I'm fundamentally incompetent") generates the most severe expectancy deficits and the most pervasive motivational paralysis. Locus plays a secondary role — internal stable global attributions are worse than external stable global attributions, but both produce significant helplessness when stability and globality are high.

#### 18. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Confirmation Architecture of Stability Beliefs *(Line 318)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Confirmation Architecture of Stability Beliefs
> The self-sealing property of stability attributions is not merely psychological — it has a behavioral feedback structure that makes disconfirmation genuinely difficult. Unlike purely cognitive biases (where the person selectively attends to confirming evidence), stability attributions in achievement contexts generate *actual performance reductions* through their effect on effort investment. This means that even an observer with perfect rationality who tracked the student's performance over time would likely see a pattern consistent with the stable attribution — not because the attribution is correct but because the attribution has made itself correct. This is a motivational analogue of the self-fulfilling prophecy, but with a more precise causal mechanism than Merton's original formulation.

#### 19. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] The Stability Lock → Organizational Learned Helplessness *(Line 325)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] The Stability Lock → Organizational Learned Helplessness
> The stability-expectancy lock operates at the organizational level with striking structural fidelity to the individual level. Organizations that attribute persistent underperformance to stable factors ("this market is inherently difficult," "our workforce lacks the capability for this transformation") exhibit the same reduced investment in change-directed effort, the same expectancy deficits, and the same confirmation dynamics that individual-level research documents. The mechanism is domain-independent: stable causal attribution suppresses motivated action, which produces confirming performance, which reinforces the stable attribution. This is not a metaphor — it is the same causal structure operating at a different level of analysis.

#### 20. [DEFINITION] The Guilt-Shame Asymmetry in Motivational Function *(Line 334)*

> [!definition] The Guilt-Shame Asymmetry in Motivational Function
> **Definition:** In Weiner's framework, guilt and shame are both negative self-directed emotions but are generated by different attribution profiles and have opposite motivational implications. Guilt — generated by controllable internal causal attributions — is motivationally facilitative because it implies that the cause is changeable. Shame — generated by uncontrollable internal causal attributions — is motivationally paralyzing because it implies that the cause is fixed.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The practical implication is that not all negative self-directed affect is motivationally equivalent. Interventions that reduce guilt without replacing the attributional substrate may inadvertently reduce a motivationally useful signal. The goal of attribution-based interventions is not to eliminate negative affect but to shift the *type* of negative affect toward guilt and away from shame.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Guilt typically accompanies specific behavioral regret ("I wish I had studied more"); shame typically accompanies global self-evaluation ("I'm just not capable of this").

#### 21. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Descriptive-Normative Tension in Controllability Attributions *(Line 343)*

> [!tension-identified] The Descriptive-Normative Tension in Controllability Attributions
> Weiner's model documents that controllability attributions trigger reduced helping and increased anger — but it is not obvious that this is a *rational* or *normatively appropriate* response. The empirical finding that perceived controllability of stigmatized conditions (addiction, obesity, poverty) reduces helping motivation has been used both descriptively (to understand social behavior) and normatively (as an implicit justification for reduced social provision). The theory cannot adjudicate this normative question, but the analytical danger is that its descriptive accuracy is taken to imply normative warrant. The mechanism operates regardless of whether the controllability attribution is accurate — and there is substantial evidence that controllability attributions for complex conditions (addiction, mental illness, structural poverty) systematically overestimate personal control and underestimate systemic factors.

#### 22. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Weiner as Applied Appraisal Theory — and What That Reveals *(Line 356)*

> [!analytical-insight] Weiner as Applied Appraisal Theory — and What That Reveals
> The structural correspondence between Weiner's attribution dimensions and Lazarus's appraisal dimensions is too precise to be coincidental — they are independently converging on the same underlying cognitive structure. What makes this observation useful is that Lazarus's framework has a more developed account of the *social and relational* dimensions of appraisal (particularly his concept of coping potential and the distinction between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping). Reading Weiner through Lazarus suggests that the stability dimension's motivational consequences may be partly mediated by *coping potential appraisals* — and that interventions targeting coping potential may be as effective as those targeting causal attributions directly. This is an underexplored intervention direction in the attribution retraining literature.

#### 25. [BEST-PRACTICE] Design Attribution Interventions for Affective Change, Not Just Cognitive Change *(Line 382)*

> [!best-practice] Design Attribution Interventions for Affective Change, Not Just Cognitive Change
> When designing [[Attribution-Retraining-Interventions]], build in explicit mechanisms for affective reorganization, not just cognitive reattribution. This might include: (1) having participants describe emotional responses to new attributional framings (to make the affective component explicit), (2) behavioral experiments that generate success experiences under the new attribution (providing affective confirmation of the new framework), and (3) explicit discussion of the emotion-attribution correspondence so participants understand why the intervention targets emotions as much as beliefs.

#### 26. [WARNING] The Measurement Problem *(Line 391)*

> [!warning] The Measurement Problem
> A persistent limitation of attribution research is the discrepancy between verbal attribution measures (what people say caused their outcomes) and actual causal cognitions (which may be less accessible to introspection and more automatic). Much attribution research relies on self-report scales or open-ended causal statement coding, but there is evidence that attribution-behavior relationships are stronger when attributions are measured closer in time to the outcome and weaker when measured retrospectively — suggesting that post-hoc verbal attributions may partially reconstruct rather than accurately report actual causal cognitions.

#### 27. [WARNING] The Cultural Universality Problem *(Line 396)*

> [!warning] The Cultural Universality Problem
> Cross-cultural research on attributional patterns and their emotional consequences reveals significant variation. In collectivistic cultures, the locus-pride link is attenuated (public success credited to group rather than self), and internal attributions for failure are not always accompanied by reduced self-esteem (in some cultural contexts, self-criticism for controllable failures is normatively encouraged and motivationally facilitative). The stability-expectancy link appears more culturally robust than the locus-emotion correspondences, suggesting that the stability mechanism may be more fundamental to the architecture.

#### 29. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Two-Stage Attribution Intervention Architecture *(Line 423)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Two-Stage Attribution Intervention Architecture
> The emotion-mediation mechanism, taken seriously as a design constraint, implies that attribution interventions should be structured in two stages rather than the single-stage cognitive reattribution that most programs implement. Stage 1 targets *cognitive attribution accuracy* — specifically, the stability dimension, shifting from stable to unstable causal explanations for failure. This is the standard content of attribution retraining. Stage 2 targets *affective reorganization* — working explicitly with the specific emotions generated by the new attribution profile to ensure that the affective consequence of the cognitive change is (a) experienced and (b) connected to the cognitive reattribution in the learner's model.
> 
> The rationale is architectural: if hopelessness (the affect generated by stable attributions for failure) mediates the stability-persistence relationship, then a cognitive shift from stable to unstable attribution that does not produce a corresponding shift from hopelessness to hope has failed to propagate through the motivational chain. The new attribution generates a new affective prediction (hope rather than hopelessness) but this prediction must be tested through experience and confirmed behaviorally to be consolidated. Stage 2 provides this confirmation through structured success experiences under the new attribution framework, combined with explicit attention to the emotional experience of those successes. This is not a minor modification to existing programs — it requires a fundamentally different intervention structure that treats emotional experience as a necessary variable rather than an expected outcome.

#### 30. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Why the Common Account Undersells the Theory *(Line 432)*

> [!analytical-insight] Why the Common Account Undersells the Theory
> The common account — "attributing failure to effort is better than attributing it to ability" — captures one output of the model but misses its generative mechanism. The reason effort attributions are motivationally superior is not that they are more "positive" but that effort is unstable (hence generates hope, not hopelessness) AND controllable (hence generates guilt, not shame) AND internal (hence preserves the self-evaluation that makes future success meaningful). The three-dimensional analysis shows that the motivational superiority of effort attributions is *overdetermined* — it is simultaneously produced by three independent mechanisms. This overdetermination is why effort-attribution interventions are consistently effective: they operate on all three dimensions simultaneously.

#### 33. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Stability Attribution Lock → Organizational Change Failure *(Line 468)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Stability Attribution Lock → Organizational Change Failure
> In organizational change management, teams that attribute persistent underperformance to stable factors ("this culture will never change," "this workforce doesn't have the capability") exhibit the same self-sealing architecture as individuals with stable ability attributions. The stable organizational attribution generates reduced investment in change-directed effort (low expectancy of success makes effort seem wasteful), which produces performance patterns consistent with the original stable attribution. The analogy holds because both share the relational structure: stable causal attribution → expectancy deficit → reduced action → confirming evidence → attribution reinforcement. The diagnostic question is: is the organization reducing effort investment because of the attribution, or is the attribution tracking genuinely stable facts? The intervention prescription follows directly from the psychological literature — behavioral experiments that test the stability assumption by investing effort strategically in specific bounded change projects.

#### 34. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Emotion-Mediation Cascade → Psychotherapy and Cognitive-Behavioral Models *(Line 471)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Emotion-Mediation Cascade → Psychotherapy and Cognitive-Behavioral Models
> Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy's assumption that changing cognitive distortions will automatically improve emotional states represents an intervention architecture that the emotion-mediation mechanism directly challenges. CBT targets the cognitive level (catastrophic thinking, negative automatic thoughts) with the expectation that emotional change will follow. But if the relationship between cognition and emotion is bidirectional — as the attribution literature suggests — then purely cognitive interventions may insufficiently address the affective substrate. The structural analogue identifies why CBT works when it does (cognitive change that cascades into affective reorganization) and why it sometimes doesn't (cognitive change that fails to reorganize the affective patterns). The intervention prescription: cognitive reattribution should be paired with behavioral experiments that generate direct affective disconfirmation of the catastrophic prediction — not just cognitive evidence, but visceral affective experience.

#### 35. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Self-Sealing Architecture in AI Alignment *(Line 474)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Self-Sealing Architecture in AI Alignment
> A structurally isomorphic dynamic appears in discussions of AI systems trained on their own outputs: a system that makes predictions about its own performance capabilities, reduces its exploration based on those predictions, and then interprets the reduced output variance as confirmation of its capability ceiling. The structural elements are all present — stable "attribution" about capability, reduced motivated exploration, confirming evidence. #### 38. [DEFINITION] The Three-Dimensional Causal Taxonomy (Weiner, 1979) *(Line 542)*

> [!definition] The Three-Dimensional Causal Taxonomy (Weiner, 1979)
> **Definition:** The classification of achievement causes along three independent dimensions — locus (internal/external), stability (stable/unstable), and controllability (controllable/uncontrollable) — that together determine the emotional and motivational consequences of causal attributions. The taxonomy replaces the simpler internal/external distinction with a structure sufficient to generate distinct predictions about pride, shame, guilt, anger, pity, hope, and hopelessness.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This taxonomy is the generative architecture of the theory — not a descriptive catalogue of cause types but a predictive mechanism for emotion and motivation.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Classify any stated cause using three binary questions: (1) Is the cause in the person or outside? (2) Will the cause likely persist in future situations? (3) Could the person have controlled the cause through volition?
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Bernard-Weiner]], [[Causal-Induction]]

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Attribution Style / Explanatory Style (Abramson, Seligman & Teasdale, 1978) *(Line 551)*

> [!definition] Attribution Style / Explanatory Style (Abramson, Seligman & Teasdale, 1978)
> **Definition:** A habitual pattern of causal attribution across situations, characterized by three dimensions: internal/external, stable/unstable, global/specific. Depressive explanatory style (internal, stable, global attributions for negative events) predicts vulnerability to learned helplessness and depression.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Attribution style transforms the situational mechanism described by Weiner into an individual difference variable with developmental history and clinical implications.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Measured via the Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ) or the Children's Attributional Style Questionnaire (CASQ); coding of open-ended causal statements.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Attribution-Style]], [[Helpless-Response-Pattern]], [[Growth-Mindset]]

#### 40. [DEFINITION] The Stability-Expectancy Mechanism (Weiner, 1985) *(Line 560)*

> [!definition] The Stability-Expectancy Mechanism (Weiner, 1985)
> **Definition:** The causal pathway by which stable attributions for outcomes (particularly failures) generate expectancy deficits — reduced estimates of future success — through the logical inference that a cause that was present and stable in the past will be present in the future. Expectancy deficits in turn reduce the perceived instrumentality of effort investment.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the analytically central mechanism of the report — the primary driver of the most educationally and clinically significant motivational outcomes.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Measured via post-outcome expectancy ratings; behavioral observation of persistence after failure; response to challenge conditions.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Implicit-Theory-of-Intelligence]], [[Growth-Mindset-Theory]], [[Achievement-Goal-Theory]]

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Emotion Mediation in Attribution (Weiner, 1986) *(Line 569)*

> [!definition] Emotion Mediation in Attribution (Weiner, 1986)
> **Definition:** The structural claim that the causal path from attribution to behavior is not direct but runs through specific discrete emotions generated by dimensional classification. The attribution generates the emotion; the emotion drives the behavior. Interventions that change attributions without producing affective change fail to propagate through to behavior.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the most theoretically important and most frequently overlooked feature of Weiner's mature model.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Demonstrated by showing that controlling for emotion eliminates the attribution-behavior association; and that inducing emotions without attribution change produces behavior changes consistent with the emotion-behavior rather than the attribution-behavior link.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Appraisal-Theory]], [[Motivational-Psychology]], [[Attribution-Retraining]]

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Controllability-Moral Inference Bridge (Weiner, 1993, 1995) *(Line 578)*

> [!definition] Controllability-Moral Inference Bridge (Weiner, 1993, 1995)
> **Definition:** The automatic cognitive process by which perceived controllability of another person's negative condition triggers moral evaluation — specifically, reduced sympathy and increased anger — rather than purely descriptive causal cognition. The attribution and the moral judgment are generated in a single cognitive act rather than sequentially.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Reveals that Attribution Theory is simultaneously a descriptive motivational theory and an implicit moral philosophy — and that the normative use of controllability attributions in policy contexts is not automatically warranted by their descriptive accuracy.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Operationalized via helping intention measures conditioned on controllability manipulations; anger and pity ratings as a function of controllability attributions for stigmatized conditions.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Actor-Observer-Bias]], [[Belonging-Uncertainty]], [[Attribution-Style]]

#### 46. [TENSION] Attribution as Cause vs. Attribution as Symptom *(Line 618)*

> [!tension] Attribution as Cause vs. Attribution as Symptom
> **Position A (Weiner's model):** Causal attributions are the proximate cognitive cause of differential emotional and motivational outcomes. Change the attribution, change the motivation.
> 
> **Position B (Mood-congruence view):** Prior emotional states and motivational dispositions are the primary determinants of causal attributions — people in negative affective states make more external, stable attributions; failure-expecting students make stable attributions for failure not because of logical inference but as an expression of a broader negative self-concept.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** Bidirectional. Experimental studies can demonstrate both directions of influence. The most parsimonious account is a reciprocal causal model in which attributions and emotions mutually constitute each other over time — but this account is more complex and less therapeutically tractable than the unidirectional cognitive-primacy model.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** If attributions are partly symptoms rather than causes, cognitive attribution retraining may have limited efficacy without addressing the underlying emotional and self-concept substrate. This is consistent with the clinical literature showing that attribution interventions have larger effects in early-stage helplessness than in established depressive episodes.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** The two-stage intervention architecture proposed in Phase VI partially dissolves this tension by treating the attribution-emotion relationship as bidirectional — the intervention targets both the cognitive attribution AND the emotional reorganization simultaneously.

#### 59. [PROTOCOL] Two-Stage Attribution Retraining Protocol (Derived from Phase VI Synthesis) *(Line 737)*

> [!protocol] Two-Stage Attribution Retraining Protocol (Derived from Phase VI Synthesis)
> **Context:** Use with individuals or groups showing established helpless response patterns — persistent attributions for failure to stable, uncontrollable causes (low ability, fixed personality traits), with associated expectancy deficits and reduced persistence.
> 
> **Stage 1 — Cognitive Reattribution:**
> 1. Introduce the stability dimension explicitly — explain the distinction between stable and unstable causes, using examples from the person's own domain
> 2. Map the person's current attribution profile onto the dimensional model — make explicit which dimension is problematic
> 3. Present alternative unstable attributions for recent failure experiences (insufficient specific strategy, insufficient targeted effort, task unfamiliarity)
> 4. Review evidence that supports the unstable interpretation — prior successes in similar tasks, the role of practice and strategy in performance improvement
> 
> **Stage 2 — Affective Reorganization:**
> 1. Identify the emotional consequence of the current stable attribution (hopelessness, shame, resignation)
> 2. Generate a prediction: if the unstable attribution is accurate, what emotion should follow? (Hope, guilt as motivation, interest in strategy)
> 3. Design and execute a behavioral experiment — a challenging task with high probability of success under sufficient effort and appropriate strategy
> 4. Process the emotional experience during and after the task — explicitly connect the affective experience to the attribution reframe
> 5. Repeat with incrementally increasing challenge
> 
> **Success Criteria:** The intervention is working when the person (a) spontaneously generates unstable attributions for failures without prompting, (b) reports emotional responses consistent with guilt rather than shame after controllable failures, and (c) shows increased persistence under failure conditions.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** (1) Stopping at Stage 1 — cognitive change without affective reorganization is insufficient; (2) using success experiences that are too easy and don't require genuine effort (undermines the attribution mechanism by providing unearned disconfirmation); (3) failing to connect the behavioral experiment explicitly to the attribution reframe, so the success experience is emotionally processed as "I got lucky" rather than "I succeeded because of unstable factors I can deploy again."

> **Q:** What does the Two-Stage Intervention Architecture propose, and what is the theoretical rationale?
> **A:** Stage 1: cognitive reattribution (shifting stable to unstable causal explanations). Stage 2: affective reorganization (using behavioral experiments to generate visceral confirmation of the new attribution's emotional implications). The rationale: if emotion mediates the attribution-behavior link, cognitive change without affective change fails to propagate through to behavior.
