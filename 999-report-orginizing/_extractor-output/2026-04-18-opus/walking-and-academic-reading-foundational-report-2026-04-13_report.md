#### 13. [DEFINITION] Dual-Task Interference (Pashler, 1994; Wickens, 2008) *(Line 276)*

> [!definition] Dual-Task Interference (Pashler, 1994; Wickens, 2008)

>

#### 19. [DEFINITION] Automaticity (Shiffrin & Schneider, 1977; Logan, 1988) *(Line 347)*

> [!definition] Automaticity (Shiffrin & Schneider, 1977; Logan, 1988)
> The property of cognitive or motor processes that can be executed with minimal attentional demand, minimal conscious awareness, and minimal interference with concurrent tasks. Classical criteria include: (1) efficiency (low resource demand), (2) lack of intentionality (can be triggered involuntarily), (3) lack of awareness (operates below conscious threshold), and (4) lack of controllability (difficult to suppress once initiated). > 
> **Boundary:** Automaticity is not an all-or-nothing property. Modern views (Moors & De Houwer, 2006) argue that automaticity is better understood as a continuum with multiple partially independent features. A process can be automatic on some criteria (e.g., efficient) but not others (e.g., controllable). This "graduated" view is critical for understanding walking.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The degree to which walking is truly automatic — in the full sense required for zero-cost dual-task processing — is the linchpin of whether walking while reading has a meaningful cognitive cost.
>

#### 24. [DEFINITION] Embodied Cognition (Varela, Thompson & Rosch, 1991; Clark, 1997; Shapiro, 2019) *(Line 395)*

> [!definition] Embodied Cognition (Varela, Thompson & Rosch, 1991; Clark, 1997; Shapiro, 2019)
> A broad theoretical movement within [[Cognitive-Science|cognitive science]] arguing that cognitive processes are fundamentally shaped by the body's morphology, sensory systems, and motor capabilities. In its strong form, embodied cognition holds that the body is constitutive of cognition — that thinking does not merely happen *in* the brain but is *realized through* brain-body-environment interactions. In its weaker but more empirically supported form, it claims that bodily states, motor experience, and sensorimotor engagement significantly modulate cognitive processing.
> 
> **Boundary:** Embodied cognition is not a single unified theory but a family of related proposals (grounded cognition, enacted cognition, extended cognition, situated cognition) with varying degrees of radicalism. The claim that "walking helps thinking" draws primarily on the moderate versions, not the most radical claims about the constitutive role of the body.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** If embodied cognition effects are robust for reading comprehension — not just for creative thinking or problem-solving — they could offset or even reverse the dual-task costs documented in Sections 3-4.
> 
> **See also:** [[Embodied-Cognition]], [[Grounded-Cognition]], [[Enactivism]], [[Extended-Cognition]], [[Situated-Cognition]]

#### 25. [WARNING] The Creativity-Comprehension Distinction *(Line 408)*

> [!warning] The Creativity-Comprehension Distinction
> Oppezzo and Schwartz's results apply specifically to **[[Divergent-Thinking|divergent thinking]]** — the generation of novel ideas and creative associations. They did NOT find benefits for **[[Convergent-Thinking|convergent thinking]]** — the focused, analytical, single-correct-answer reasoning that characterizes reading comprehension of structured academic arguments. In fact, walking slightly *impaired* convergent thinking in their study. Reading an academic report is overwhelmingly a convergent task: you are trying to understand a specific argument with specific components and specific relationships, not brainstorm creative alternatives. The Stanford walking study, properly interpreted, is actually *evidence against* walking while reading complex analytical material.

#### 30. [DEFINITION] Yerkes-Dodson Law (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908; revisited by Diamond et al., 2007) *(Line 449)*

> [!definition] Yerkes-Dodson Law (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908; revisited by Diamond et al., 2007)
> The principle that the relationship between [[Arousal|arousal]] (physiological activation) and [[Cognitive-Performance|cognitive performance]] follows an inverted-U curve: performance improves with increasing arousal up to an optimal point, then declines as arousal becomes excessive. Critically, the optimal arousal level varies inversely with task complexity — simple tasks tolerate or benefit from higher arousal, while complex tasks require lower optimal arousal.
> 
> **Boundary:** The Yerkes-Dodson law is often oversimplified. The original 1908 finding was specifically about task difficulty and punishment intensity in mice. Modern formulations distinguish between the effects of arousal on different cognitive functions: attention, working memory, executive function, and learning each have different arousal-performance functions.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Walking at a comfortable pace produces mild physiological arousal. The Yerkes-Dodson framework predicts whether this arousal level helps or hurts performance on a complex cognitive task like reading academic reports.
>

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Generation Effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978; Bertsch et al., 2007) *(Line 507)*

> [!definition] Generation Effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978; Bertsch et al., 2007)
> The robust finding that information which is actively generated by the learner — through recall, paraphrasing, elaboration, summarization, or transformation — is remembered significantly better than information that is merely read or passively received. The generation effect is one of the most replicated findings in the [[Learning-Sciences|learning sciences]], with effect sizes typically ranging from d = 0.40 to d = 0.87 depending on the type of generation and the retention interval.
> 
> **Boundary:** The generation effect requires successful generation — if the learner generates incorrect information, the benefit is reduced or eliminated. It also operates most strongly when generation requires effortful retrieval or transformation, not simple copying. The "desirable difficulties" principle ([[Desirable-Difficulties|Bjork, 1994]]) captures the broader pattern: learning improves when encoding is made more effortful in productive ways.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Walking-reading is definitionally passive — you cannot generate, annotate, summarize, or connect while walking and holding a device. Desk-based PKB engagement is definitionally generative — it requires you to produce notes, connections, wiki-links, and reformulations. This is the most consequential difference between the two modes, and it outweighs the dual-task interference question entirely.
> 
> **See also:** [[Generation-Effect]], [[Active-Recall]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Testing-Effect]], [[Elaboration]]

#### 37. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] Original Synthesis: Reframing as Opportunity Cost *(Line 530)*

> [!original-synthesis] Original Synthesis: Reframing as Opportunity Cost
> The walking-reading question has been framed throughout the literature — and implicitly in your question — as "does walking impair reading?" But this is the wrong framing. The right framing is: **what is the opportunity cost of walking-reading compared to the best available alternative?** The answer is not merely the modest dual-task cost of walking (perhaps 10-20% comprehension reduction). The answer is the *entire generation effect advantage* of active PKB note-making (perhaps 40-80% improvement in retention and depth of understanding). The dual-task cost is a drip; the generation-effect opportunity cost is a flood. Even if walking-reading is 95% as effective as passive desk-reading, it is perhaps 50-60% as effective as active desk-based PKB engagement. This reframing resolves the practical question conclusively: the advantage of transitioning to desk-based active reading is not about avoiding walking's modest costs — it is about accessing the transformative power of generative learning that walking precludes.

#### 43. [PROTOCOL] Multi-Pass Active Reading Protocol for PKB *(Line 592)*

> [!protocol] Multi-Pass Active Reading Protocol for PKB
> **Pass 1 — Survey (5-10 minutes):** Skim the report structure: title, abstract, section headings, callouts, expansion topics. Activate relevant schemas. Identify connections to existing permanent notes. Set reading goals: "What do I need to learn from this?"
> 
> **Pass 2 — Deep Read with Annotation (30-45 minutes):** Read sequentially. Highlight key claims, definitions, and insights. Write marginal notes: "This connects to [[X]]," "This contradicts what I thought about Y," "Need to understand this mechanism better." Flag passages for PKB note creation.
> 
> **Pass 3 — PKB Processing (20-30 minutes):** Create or update permanent notes for key concepts. Write wiki-linked connections. Generate flashcard seeds. Update relevant MOCs. This is where the generation effect operates most powerfully — transforming passive annotations into active knowledge construction.
> 
> **Pass 4 — Consolidation Walk (15-20 minutes):** Walk while mentally reviewing what you learned. Try to explain the three most important ideas from memory. Notice where your understanding breaks down — these gaps become targets for re-reading.

#### 50. [DEFINITION] Working Memory (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974; Baddeley, 2000) *(Line 714)*

#### 51. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988, 2011) *(Line 723)*

#### 65. [TENSION] Automaticity vs. Residual Attentional Cost *(Line 871)*

> [!tension] Automaticity vs. Residual Attentional Cost
> **Position A (Strong Automaticity):** Walking in a familiar environment is fully automatic and imposes zero attentional cost (classical Shiffrin & Schneider view). **Advocates:** Logan (instance theory suggests overlearned skills bypass central processing entirely).
> **Position B (Graded Automaticity):** Walking always retains some central executive monitoring requirement, particularly for balance, turning, and gaze stabilization (Woollacott & Shumway-Cook, 2002). **Advocates:** Yogev-Seligmann et al. (2008), Al-Yahya et al. (2011).
> **Current Evidence:** Dual-task gait research consistently shows measurable gait changes during concurrent cognitive tasks even in young adults, supporting Position B.
> **Why It Matters:** If Position A is correct, walking-reading should be cost-free; if Position B is correct, the cost is real but may vary with conditions.
> **This Report's Stance:** This report sides with Position B based on the weight of dual-task gait evidence and the graded automaticity framework of Moors & De Houwer (2006).

#### 66. [TENSION] Embodied Enhancement vs. Dual-Task Cost *(Line 878)*

> [!tension] Embodied Enhancement vs. Dual-Task Cost
> **Position A (Net Benefit):** Walking's embodied cognition benefits (arousal, creative facilitation, spatial metaphor grounding) outweigh its dual-task costs, producing a net positive effect on overall cognitive processing.
> **Position B (Net Cost):** Walking's benefits are domain-specific (creativity, not comprehension) and arousal benefits are better accessed post-exercise than during exercise, making concurrent walking-reading a net negative for analytical reading.
> **Current Evidence:** Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014) support creativity benefits but not comprehension benefits; Lambourne & Tomporowski (2010) show during-exercise cognitive costs for complex tasks.
> **Why It Matters:** Resolves whether walking-reading is a productive or counterproductive habit for academic learning.
> **This Report's Stance:** Position B for high-complexity analytical material; Position A may hold for creative or generative thinking tasks.

#### 82. [PROTOCOL] Quick Decision Protocol: Walk or Read at Desk? *(Line 1033)*

> [!protocol] Quick Decision Protocol: Walk or Read at Desk?
> Before starting a reading session, ask three questions:
> 
> 1. **Is this material novel or review?**
>    - Novel (new concepts, unfamiliar frameworks) → DESK
>    - Review (re-reading familiar material) → Walking is acceptable
> 
> 2. **Is the element interactivity high or low?**
>    - High (multi-step arguments, interdependent concepts) → DESK
>    - Low (narrative, biographical, sequential) → Walking is acceptable
> 
> 3. **Will I actively process (annotate, note-make, PKB-link)?**
>    - Yes → DESK (active processing requires hands, tools, and focused attention)
>    - No → Walking is acceptable, but consider whether active processing would be more valuable

#### 84. [PROTOCOL] Walk-Read-Walk Sandwich Protocol *(Line 1058)*

> [!protocol] Walk-Read-Walk Sandwich Protocol
> **Pre-Walk (15-20 min):** Walk at comfortable pace. Mentally preview: what do you already know about this topic? What questions do you bring? What permanent notes in your PKB relate? This activates schemas and primes encoding.
> 
> **Desk Reading (45-60 min):** Deep read with annotation → PKB processing → summarization. Apply the four-pass protocol from Section 8.3.
