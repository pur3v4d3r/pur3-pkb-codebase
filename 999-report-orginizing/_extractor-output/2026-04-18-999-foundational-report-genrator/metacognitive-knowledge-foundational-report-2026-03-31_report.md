#### 2. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979, 1981) *(Line 271)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979, 1981)
> Metacognitive knowledge refers to the relatively stable, consciously accessible beliefs and understandings that an individual possesses concerning cognitive processes and states — including those of the self, other persons, cognitive tasks, and cognitive strategies. It is knowledge stored in [[Long-Term-Memory]] that can be retrieved, verbalized, and applied to the monitoring and management of cognitive activity. Metacognitive knowledge is distinguished from moment-to-moment metacognitive experiences (feelings of knowing, felt difficulty, surprise at comprehension failure) and from executive control processes (planning, monitoring, evaluating, regulating). The construct thus names the *content* of the metacognitive system — the declarative and procedural knowledge that informs regulatory action — rather than the regulatory processes themselves.
> 
> **Boundary:** Metacognitive knowledge does not encompass all self-knowledge, nor does it reduce to knowledge about one's own cognitive limitations. It specifically concerns beliefs about the conditions, processes, strategies, and factors that influence cognitive performance. A belief that one "is bad at math" is proto-metacognitive at best; a belief that one processes mathematical information more accurately when working without time pressure is genuinely metacognitive.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Metacognitive knowledge is frequently conflated with metacognitive monitoring — the real-time tracking of one's comprehension or performance during a task. Monitoring draws *on* metacognitive knowledge but constitutes a distinct process: it is a regulatory activity rather than a knowledge state. The knowledge provides the standards and criteria against which monitoring operates; the monitoring is the operation itself.
> 
> **Research Status:** While the core construct is well-established, ongoing debate concerns the degree to which metacognitive knowledge is domain-general versus domain-specific, the accuracy of self-reported metacognitive knowledge, and the mechanisms through which metacognitive knowledge translates (or fails to translate) into regulatory improvement.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognition]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Knowledge-of-Cognition]], [[Regulation-of-Cognition]]

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Declarative Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris, Lipson & Wixson, 1983; Schraw & Moshman, 1995) *(Line 302)*

> [!definition] Declarative Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris, Lipson & Wixson, 1983; Schraw & Moshman, 1995)
> Declarative metacognitive knowledge — also termed *knowing that* — is propositional knowledge about oneself as a learner, about cognitive tasks, and about cognitive strategies. It is the stable, consciously articulable knowledge that a person can state, explain, and reflect upon: "I know that spaced repetition produces more durable memory than massed practice," "I know that I tend to lose comprehension when reading for more than forty-five minutes without a break," "I know that summarization requires active construction rather than passive selection." Declarative metacognitive knowledge resides in [[Long-Term-Memory]] as a network of propositions about cognitive functioning.
> 
> **Boundary:** Declarative metacognitive knowledge does not entail that a person can execute the strategy or process it describes. A student may accurately state that elaborative interrogation is more effective than rereading while consistently choosing rereading in practice — this gap between declarative knowledge and procedural action is a central puzzle in the metacognitive knowledge literature.
> 
> **See also:** [[Declarative-Memory]], [[Procedural-Knowledge]], [[conditional-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[Elaborative-Interrogation]], [[Metacognitive-Strategy]]

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Procedural Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983; Schraw & Moshman, 1995) *(Line 309)*

> [!definition] Procedural Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983; Schraw & Moshman, 1995)
> Procedural metacognitive knowledge — *knowing how* — is knowledge about the execution sequences, operational steps, and implementation details of cognitive strategies. Where declarative metacognitive knowledge concerns what a strategy is and what general effect it produces, procedural metacognitive knowledge concerns how to actually carry it out: the specific operations, their ordering, and their quality control checkpoints. A skilled reader's procedural metacognitive knowledge includes the specific operations involved in comprehension monitoring — the precise ways she pauses, signals confusion, selects a fix-up strategy, and checks for resolution.
> 
> **Boundary:** Procedural metacognitive knowledge differs from automatized procedural knowledge in that it retains some degree of conscious accessibility, at least during skill development. Fully automatized procedures (e.g., the decoding processes of a fluent reader) operate below the threshold of metacognitive accessibility and are thus no longer procedural *metacognitive* knowledge in the strict sense.
> 
> **See also:** [[Procedural-Knowledge]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring]], [[Metacognitive-Strategy]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Automaticity]]

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983) *(Line 316)*

> [!definition] Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983)
> Conditional metacognitive knowledge — *knowing when and why* — is knowledge about the contextual conditions under which particular strategies are effective or ineffective: the circumstances, task types, learner states, and goal structures that render a given strategy appropriate or inappropriate. It is the most sophisticated dimension of metacognitive knowledge and the dimension most closely linked to flexible, adaptive self-regulation. Conditional metacognitive knowledge enables a learner to move from "I know that elaborative interrogation exists and how to execute it" to "I know that elaborative interrogation is particularly effective for expository text with high conceptual density when my goal is transferable understanding rather than immediate recall, and that it is ineffective when the content exceeds my prior knowledge threshold."
> 
> **Boundary:** Conditional metacognitive knowledge is not the same as general heuristic reasoning about strategy selection. It is specifically *metacognitive* in that it concerns the conditions of cognitive effectiveness — the learner's own cognitive states, the task's cognitive demands, and the strategy's cognitive mechanisms — rather than simply contextual considerations of a more general kind.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Conditional metacognitive knowledge is often the least explicitly taught dimension in educational settings, where instruction frequently addresses declarative strategy knowledge (here is what elaborative interrogation is) and sometimes procedural strategy knowledge (here is how to execute it) while rarely providing explicit treatment of conditional knowledge (here are the conditions under which it is and is not appropriate).
> 
> **See also:** [[Elaborative-Interrogation]], [[Metacognitive-Strategy]], [[Transfer-of-Learning]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Experiences (Flavell, 1981; Efklides, 2001, 2006) *(Line 333)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Experiences (Flavell, 1981; Efklides, 2001, 2006)
> Metacognitive experiences are conscious phenomenological states that arise during cognitive processing and that carry information about the progress, quality, and appropriateness of cognitive activity. They include feelings of knowing (the sense that one knows something that cannot be immediately retrieved), judgments of learning (assessments of how well material has been acquired), feelings of difficulty (the sense that a task is more demanding than expected), feelings of confidence (assessments of the correctness of a response), and feelings of familiarity (the sense that material has been previously encountered). Metacognitive experiences are distinguished from metacognitive knowledge in that they are situationally specific, affectively toned, and arise online during cognitive activity rather than being retrieved from stored knowledge.
> 
> **Boundary:** Metacognitive experiences are not simply metacognitive knowledge applied to current tasks. They are phenomenologically distinct states with their own processing mechanisms — often generated by fluency effects, familiarity effects, and other heuristic shortcuts that can yield experiences that are systematically inaccurate (as in the [[Fluency-Illusion]], where the ease of processing a well-printed text is misattributed to deep comprehension of its content).
> 
> **See also:** [[Feeling-of-Knowing]], [[Judgment-of-Learning]], [[Fluency-Illusion]], [[Calibration]], [[Metacognitive-Calibration]], [[Metacognitive-Feelings]]

#### 12. [DEFINITION] Monitoring-Control Hierarchy (Nelson & Narens, 1990) *(Line 381)*

> [!definition] Monitoring-Control Hierarchy (Nelson & Narens, 1990)
> The monitoring-control hierarchy is a two-level cognitive architecture proposed by Nelson and Narens to formalize the metacognitive system. The *object level* is where direct cognitive work occurs — reading, calculating, problem-solving, memorizing. The *meta-level* is where cognitive processes are represented, assessed, and managed. Monitoring is the upward flow from object-level processes to meta-level representations: the meta-level receives information about how object-level processing is proceeding. Control is the downward flow from meta-level representations to object-level processes: the meta-level modifies ongoing cognitive activity in light of monitored information. Crucially, both flows depend on the accuracy and richness of stored metacognitive knowledge — the meta-level cannot monitor what it does not have conceptual categories for, and it cannot issue appropriate control commands without knowledge of which interventions are available and effective.
> 
> **Boundary:** The Nelson-Narens model is a normative architecture — it specifies how the metacognitive system *could* operate optimally. It does not claim that actual metacognitive processing always achieves this architecture; indeed, the model has been productive precisely because it specifies failure modes: monitoring can be inaccurate, control can be miscalibrated, and the coupling between monitoring signals and control responses can be weak or absent.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Monitoring-Regulation-Coupling]], [[Monitoring-Control-Coupling]], [[The-Nelson-Narens-Monitoring-Control-Model]], [[Judgment-of-Learning]], [[Feeling-of-Knowing]]

#### 15. [TENSION] Domain Generality vs. Domain Specificity in Metacognitive Knowledge *(Line 418)*

> [!tension] Domain Generality vs. Domain Specificity in Metacognitive Knowledge
> **The Tension:** To what extent does metacognitive knowledge constitute a domain-general cognitive resource that transfers across task types, and to what extent is it a collection of domain-specific knowledge stores that must be separately acquired in each domain?
> 
> **Position A — Generalist (Swanson, 1990; Schraw & Moshman, 1995):** Metacognitive knowledge of cognitive processes, learning strategies, and self-assessment procedures is sufficiently abstract to function as a domain-general resource. Students who develop strong metacognitive knowledge in one domain can apply its general principles to new domains, accelerating metacognitive development in those domains.
> 
> **Position B — Domain-Specific (Alexander, 1995; Tobias & Everson, 2009):** The conditional and procedural dimensions of metacognitive knowledge are domain-specific, tied to the particular structures, demands, and epistemic conventions of specific domains. A skilled metacognitive learner in mathematics may show impoverished metacognitive knowledge in literary analysis, because the relevant conditional knowledge — when to use which strategies, what signals indicate comprehension failure in this domain, what constitutes adequate performance — is domain-indexed.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** The preponderance of evidence supports a layered model: declarative metacognitive knowledge (particularly knowledge of broad cognitive principles) is domain-general and does transfer, while conditional metacognitive knowledge is domain-specific and transfers poorly. Educational interventions are most effective when they embed metacognitive knowledge instruction within domain-specific content while making the domain-general principles explicit.
> 
> **This Report's Position:** The evidence warrants a hybrid model in which domain-general declarative knowledge provides scaffolding for domain-specific calibration — the general principles do not substitute for domain-specific conditional knowledge but do accelerate its acquisition by providing a conceptual framework within which domain-specific experience can be efficiently organized and interpreted.
> 
> **See also:** [[Transfer-of-Learning]], [[Metacognition-&-Critical-Thinking]], [[Expertise]], [[Cognitive-Architecture]]

#### 21. [EVIDENCE] Untitled *(Line 502)*

> [!evidence] Untitled
> Koriat and Bjork's research program on delayed judgments of learning (dJOLs) demonstrates that the timing of metacognitive judgments significantly affects their accuracy: judgments made immediately after studying are dominated by fluency and familiarity effects that inflate confidence, while judgments made after a delay (when the transient effects of recent processing have dissipated) are substantially more accurate predictors of actual retention. This finding has direct practical implications: immediate JOLs are systematically overconfident, and learners should be instructed to delay their self-assessments or to use retrieval-based cues (testing themselves) rather than processing-fluency cues (rereading feels smooth) when assessing mastery.

#### 22. [BEST-PRACTICE] Untitled *(Line 519)*

> [!best-practice] Untitled
> **Evidence-Supported Metacognitive Knowledge Instruction Protocol:**
> 1. Identify the declarative metacognitive knowledge gaps most consequential for the target domain (what do students not know about how to approach this domain's cognitive tasks?)
> 2. Provide explicit declarative instruction with worked examples and think-aloud demonstrations
> 3. Structure initial practice with metacognitive scaffolding that prompts conditional knowledge deployment (what strategy should you use here, and why?)
> 4. Provide retrieval-based feedback (structured self-testing) that makes calibration errors visible
> 5. Support explicit reflection on strategy effectiveness across task types, building conditional metacognitive knowledge from guided experience
> 6. Gradually reduce scaffolding as procedural and conditional knowledge is internalized

#### 23. [DEFINITION] Pseudometacognition *(Line 542)*

> [!definition] Pseudometacognition
> Pseudometacognition refers to metacognitive-appearing knowledge, beliefs, or behaviors that do not reflect genuine awareness of one's actual cognitive processes, accurate calibration of cognitive states, or conditionally sophisticated understanding of cognitive strategies. It is characterized by the production of metacognitive language and self-reports that are socially appropriate, instructionally endorsed, or generically scripted but that are not grounded in accurate monitoring of actual cognitive functioning. Pseudometacognition is distinguishable from genuine metacognitive knowledge by its insensitivity to performance feedback, its context-insensitivity (applying the same metacognitive narrative regardless of task or performance variation), and its failure to produce adaptive regulatory responses when monitoring signals indicate they are needed.
> 
> **Boundary:** Pseudometacognition is not deliberate deception. Learners may genuinely believe they possess the metacognitive knowledge they report while their regulatory behavior reveals the absence of genuine calibration. The phenomenon is best understood as a form of metacognitive self-deception enabled by the [[Fluency-Illusion]] and by instructional environments that reward metacognitive talk without assessing metacognitive accuracy.
> 
> **See also:** [[Pseudometacognition]], [[Fluency-Illusion]], [[Calibration]], [[Metacognitive-Calibration]], [[Dunning-Kruger-Effect]]

The research base, while robust in its broad conclusions (metacognitive knowledge matters; it can be developed through instruction; explicit, embedded, conditional instruction is more effective than declarative-only transmission), is thinner at the level of specific implementation decisions. #### 25. [KEY-INSIGHT] Untitled *(Line 582)*

> [!key-insight] Untitled
> The architecture of metacognitive knowledge instruction that these conclusions imply is more demanding than the architecture of most existing programs. It must be domain-embedded (for conditional knowledge), practice-rich (for proceduralization and indexing), explicitly calibration-focused (for monitoring accuracy), and attentive to the distinction between knowing and doing (for closing the Knowledge-Activation Gap). > **[[Fluency-Illusion]]** — This node addresses the phenomenological mechanism through which reading ease is misattributed to comprehension depth, producing the most common form of overconfident calibration in academic reading contexts. The present report provides the metacognitive knowledge framework within which the fluency illusion should be understood: it is a failure of metacognitive knowledge (not knowing that fluency is a poor proxy for comprehension) combined with a miscalibrated monitoring signal.
> 
> **[[Elaborative-Interrogation]]** — This report connects to the elaborative interrogation node by providing the metacognitive knowledge-level account of why elaborative interrogation is effective: it develops conditional metacognitive knowledge (why does this work in this context?) and triggers accurate monitoring (self-questioning reveals what one does and does not understand) simultaneously.
> 
> **[[Schema-Theory]]** — Metacognitive knowledge is stored in long-term memory as schematic knowledge structures — it is not a list of propositional beliefs but an integrated network of interconnected beliefs organized by domain, strategy type, task category, and self-knowledge. The schema theory node provides the memory-level architecture within which metacognitive knowledge should be understood as organized and retrieved.
> 
> **[[Cognitive-Load-Theory]]** — CLT intersects with metacognitive knowledge in two directions: metacognitive knowledge about task demands and element interactivity provides a basis for accurate forethought-phase load anticipation, and metacognitive knowledge about one's own working memory limitations supports adaptive load management during task performance. The CLT node should be linked to the present report as providing the cognitive resource constraints within which metacognitive strategies must operate.
> 
> **[[Metacognition-&-Critical-Thinking]]**, **[[Metacognition-&-Motivation]]**, and **[[Metacognition-&-Lifelong-Learning]]** — Each of these existing PKB nodes addresses a major application domain for metacognitive knowledge. The present report provides the construct-level foundation that these application nodes presuppose; they should be linked downstream from this foundational treatment.
> 
> **Key Insight:** The pattern of connections reveals that metacognitive knowledge occupies a central integrating position in the PKB's learning science cluster. It provides the epistemic infrastructure for self-regulated learning, the knowledge content that makes monitoring informative, the calibration basis for accurate self-assessment, and the conditional intelligence that distinguishes adaptive strategy use from algorithmic strategy application. Understanding metacognitive knowledge deeply is thus a prerequisite for fully understanding the adjacent nodes rather than merely a topic of parallel interest.

#### 28. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 635)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> Stored, consciously accessible beliefs and understandings concerning cognitive processes, states, and strategies — including those of the self, other persons, cognitive tasks, and regulatory strategies. Distinguished from metacognitive regulation (the executive processes that govern cognitive activity) and metacognitive experiences (the online phenomenological states that arise during cognitive activity). Resides in [[Long-Term-Memory]] as a schematically organized knowledge network that provides the epistemic basis for self-regulated learning.
> 
> **Historical Development:** The construct emerged from Flavell's developmental studies of memory monitoring in children and was refined through parallel work by Brown on reading comprehension regulation. The initial formulation was primarily cognitive-developmental; subsequent extensions by Schraw, Nelson and Narens, and Efklides have broadened and deepened the theoretical treatment.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognition]], [[Knowledge-of-Cognition]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 29. [DEFINITION] Person Knowledge (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 642)*

> [!definition] Person Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> The subcomponent of metacognitive knowledge concerning beliefs about human cognitive processes in general, beliefs about the cognitive characteristics of other specific individuals, and beliefs about one's own cognitive strengths, limitations, and typical patterns (intrapersonal knowledge). Intrapersonal person knowledge is the most extensively studied dimension; its accuracy (calibration) is one of the primary determinants of self-regulatory quality.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Person knowledge is not equivalent to self-concept or academic self-efficacy, though these constructs are correlated. Person knowledge specifically concerns beliefs about cognitive functioning — how one processes information, what kinds of tasks are more or less demanding for one's specific cognitive profile — rather than general beliefs about one's academic competence.
> 
> **See also:** [[Self-Efficacy]], [[Calibration]], [[Metacognitive-Calibration]], [[Dunning-Kruger-Effect]], [[Growth-Mindset]]

#### 30. [DEFINITION] Task Knowledge (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 649)*

> [!definition] Task Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> The subcomponent of metacognitive knowledge concerning beliefs about how the characteristics of cognitive tasks — their structure, demands, complexity, format, and goals — influence the difficulty of processing and the strategies required for successful performance. Task knowledge includes understanding that recall is more demanding than recognition, that tasks with high element interactivity place greater demands on working memory, and that different assessment formats require different encoding strategies.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Task knowledge governs the quality of forethought-phase task analysis in the SRL cycle. Impoverished or inaccurate task knowledge produces mismatch between task demands and strategy selection before cognitive engagement begins, a particularly damaging form of metacognitive failure because it is not correctable through within-task monitoring alone.
> 
> **See also:** [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]], [[Intrinsic-Cognitive-Load]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]], [[Forethought-Phase]]

#### 31. [DEFINITION] Strategy Knowledge (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 656)*

> [!definition] Strategy Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> The subcomponent of metacognitive knowledge concerning beliefs about cognitive strategies (techniques that directly advance cognitive goals: elaboration, organization, imagery, retrieval practice) and metacognitive strategies (techniques that monitor progress toward cognitive goals: self-testing, comprehension monitoring, error detection). Strategy knowledge in its most sophisticated form includes conditional knowledge — knowing not only what strategies exist and how to execute them, but the conditions under which each is effective.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Strategy]], [[Elaborative-Interrogation]], [[Retrieval-Practice]], [[Spaced-Repetition]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring]]

#### 32. [DEFINITION] Declarative Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris, Lipson & Wixson, 1983) *(Line 661)*

> [!definition] Declarative Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris, Lipson & Wixson, 1983)
> Propositional, explicitly statable knowledge about oneself as a learner, about cognitive tasks, and about cognitive strategies — corresponding to *knowing that*. The most accessible dimension of metacognitive knowledge; the most readily assessed through self-report; and the dimension most commonly targeted in metacognitive instruction. Distinguished from procedural and conditional metacognitive knowledge by its propositional form and its independence from execution competence.
> 
> **See also:** [[declarative-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[Declarative-Memory]], [[procedural-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[conditional-metacognitive-knowledge]]

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Procedural Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983) *(Line 666)*

> [!definition] Procedural Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983)
> Knowledge about the operational steps, execution sequences, and quality-control checkpoints involved in implementing cognitive and metacognitive strategies — corresponding to *knowing how*. More closely linked than declarative metacognitive knowledge to actual strategy deployment, though still distinct from fully automatized procedural skill. Represents the bridge between declarative knowledge of strategy existence and reliable, fluent strategy execution.
> 
> **See also:** [[procedural-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[Procedural-Knowledge]], [[Automaticity]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring]]

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983) *(Line 671)*

> [!definition] Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge (Paris et al., 1983)
> Knowledge about the contextual conditions under which particular strategies are effective or ineffective — corresponding to *knowing when and why*. The most sophisticated and educationally consequential dimension of metacognitive knowledge; the most domain-specific; and the dimension most frequently neglected in educational instruction. Enables the adaptive, flexible deployment of strategies appropriate to specific task demands, learner states, and goal structures.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Conditional metacognitive knowledge is sometimes treated as simply "more detailed" declarative knowledge. The distinction is qualitative rather than quantitative: conditional knowledge concerns the enabling and disabling conditions of strategy effectiveness, which requires a fundamentally different epistemic relationship to the strategy than declarative knowledge of its existence.
> 
> **See also:** [[conditional-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[Transfer-of-Learning]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Expertise]]

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Calibration *(Line 678)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Calibration
> The degree of alignment between a learner's metacognitive judgments (predictions of performance, assessments of comprehension, estimates of mastery) and actual cognitive states or performance outcomes. Accurate calibration enables appropriate regulatory responses; miscalibration — particularly overconfident calibration — generates false positive monitoring signals that suppress necessary strategy deployment. Calibration is improvable through retrieval practice, delayed judgments of learning, and explicit instruction in the sources of processing-fluency illusions.
> 
> **Research Status:** Calibration accuracy is the single metacognitive parameter with the most direct and robust relationship to learning outcomes in experimental studies. The finding that calibration predicts performance beyond raw cognitive ability has been replicated across numerous domains and populations.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Calibration]], [[Calibration]], [[Feeling-of-Knowing]], [[Judgment-of-Learning]], [[Fluency-Illusion]], [[Retrieval-Practice]]

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Pseudometacognition *(Line 685)*

> [!definition] Pseudometacognition
> Metacognitive-appearing knowledge, beliefs, or behaviors that do not reflect genuine awareness of one's actual cognitive processes, accurate calibration of cognitive states, or conditionally sophisticated understanding of cognitive strategies. Characterized by context-insensitivity (applying the same metacognitive narrative regardless of task or performance variation) and failure to generate adaptive regulatory responses when monitoring signals indicate they are needed. Distinguishable from genuine metacognitive knowledge by its insensitivity to performance feedback.
> 
> **See also:** [[Pseudometacognition]], [[Fluency-Illusion]], [[Calibration]], [[Monitoring-Regulation-Decoupling]]

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Knowledge-Activation Gap [Original Construct — Speculative Synthesis] *(Line 690)*

> [!definition] Knowledge-Activation Gap [Original Construct — Speculative Synthesis]
> The systematic failure of stored, declaratively accurate metacognitive knowledge to activate in the relevant regulatory contexts during task performance, even when monitoring conditions that should trigger activation are present. Hypothesized to result from three contributing mechanisms: indexing insufficiency (declarative knowledge indexed to instruction contexts rather than task-embedded monitoring signals), proceduralization deficit (insufficient supported practice to proceduralize declarative knowledge), and monitoring-knowledge decoupling (weak associative links between monitoring state representations and metacognitive knowledge elements).
> 
> **Epistemic Status:** This construct is a speculative synthesis proposed to integrate disparate findings on the knowledge-regulation dissociation. It is well-motivated by existing evidence but lacks direct experimental validation as a distinct mechanism.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Monitoring-Regulation-Coupling]], [[Monitoring-Regulation-Decoupling]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]]

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Feelings (Efklides, 2001) *(Line 697)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Feelings (Efklides, 2001)
> The immediate, online affective-cognitive phenomenological states that arise during cognitive processing and carry information about the progress, quality, and appropriateness of ongoing cognitive activity. Distinct from metacognitive knowledge (stored beliefs) and metacognitive judgments (deliberate assessments), metacognitive feelings are generated by rapid, heuristic processes responsive to fluency, familiarity, and effort signals. They serve as the primary activators of regulatory responses during task performance and are the primary interface through which stored metacognitive knowledge is engaged.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Feelings]], [[Metacognitive-Experience]], [[Efklides]], [[Feeling-of-Knowing]], [[Calibration]]

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Monitoring-Control Coupling (Nelson & Narens, 1990) *(Line 702)*

> [!definition] Monitoring-Control Coupling (Nelson & Narens, 1990)
> The functional relationship between the monitoring processes that track object-level cognitive activity (generating information about current states) and the control processes that modify that activity in response to monitored information. Adequate monitoring-control coupling requires that monitoring signals reliably and promptly trigger appropriate control responses — that the metacognitive system responds to detected comprehension failure with strategy adjustment rather than continuing to process as if no failure had occurred. Monitoring-control decoupling produces the distinctive pattern of learners who detect their own performance failures without taking corrective action.
> 
> **See also:** [[Monitoring-Control-Coupling]], [[Monitoring-Regulation-Coupling]], [[Monitoring-Regulation-Decoupling]], [[The-Nelson-Narens-Monitoring-Control-Model]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]]

#### 46. [TENSION] Metacognitive Knowledge vs. Domain Knowledge: Separability and Interaction *(Line 755)*

> [!tension] Metacognitive Knowledge vs. Domain Knowledge: Separability and Interaction
> **The Tension:** Is metacognitive knowledge a separable cognitive resource that operates independently of domain knowledge, or is it so deeply interwoven with domain knowledge as to be inseparable?
> 
> **Position A — Separable (Schraw & Moshman, 1995):** Domain-general metacognitive knowledge (understanding of broad cognitive principles, generic strategy effectiveness, self-knowledge of learning style) constitutes a separable cognitive resource that can be developed and assessed independently of domain expertise. Metacognitive knowledge interventions improve outcomes across domains precisely because metacognitive knowledge transfers where domain knowledge does not.
> 
> **Position B — Inseparable (Alexander, 1995; Tobias & Everson, 2009):** Conditional metacognitive knowledge is inextricably domain-specific: knowing when and why a strategy is effective in mathematics is not separable from knowing mathematics at some level of proficiency. The appearance of domain-general metacognitive knowledge is an artifact of the domain-general *form* of declarative instruction — learners can repeat general principles without possessing functional conditional knowledge in any specific domain.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** The evidence supports separability of the declarative dimension and inseparability of the conditional dimension. Generic metacognitive instruction improves declarative metacognitive knowledge; improvement in conditional knowledge requires domain-embedded practice.
> 
> **See also:** [[Transfer-of-Learning]], [[Expertise]], [[Cognitive-Architecture]], [[Schema-Theory]]

#### 47. [TENSION] Explicit Instruction vs. Implicit Development of Metacognitive Knowledge *(Line 767)*

> [!tension] Explicit Instruction vs. Implicit Development of Metacognitive Knowledge
> **The Tension:** Does metacognitive knowledge develop most effectively through explicit instruction (direct transmission of declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge) or through well-structured experience that generates metacognitive knowledge implicitly?
> 
> **Position A — Instruction-First:** Explicit metacognitive instruction is necessary because implicit development is slow, inaccurate, and susceptible to systematic biases (particularly overconfidence and pseudometacognition). Without explicit instruction, experience-based metacognitive knowledge development is unguided and unreliable.
> 
> **Position B — Experience-First:** Metacognitive knowledge transmitted through explicit instruction remains inert — declarative knowledge without procedural instantiation. Authentic, well-structured problem-solving experience with appropriate feedback is the primary driver of genuinely functional metacognitive knowledge.
> 
> **This Report's Position:** The evidence supports a scaffolded combination in which explicit instruction provides the conceptual categories that make experiential learning more efficient, but functional metacognitive knowledge requires experiential grounding. Neither pathway alone is adequate.
> 
> **See also:** [[Scaffolding]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Productive-Struggle]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]]

#### 48. [OPEN-QUESTION] What Are the Precise Neurological Bases of Metacognitive Knowledge? *(Line 779)*

> [!open-question] What Are the Precise Neurological Bases of Metacognitive Knowledge?
> **The Question:** What brain structures and processes subserve the storage, retrieval, and application of metacognitive knowledge? How do these differ from the structures involved in first-order domain knowledge?
> 
> **What We Know:** Prefrontal cortex regions — particularly the anterior prefrontal cortex and the medial prefrontal cortex — are consistently implicated in metacognitive judgments. The hippocampus is involved in the episodic memories of past performance that ground metacognitive self-knowledge. But the precise mapping of the metacognitive knowledge *taxonomy* (person-task-strategy, declarative-procedural-conditional) onto neural substrates is almost entirely unspecified.
> 
> **What We Don't Know:** Whether the different types of metacognitive knowledge (declarative vs. conditional, person vs. strategy) are subserved by distinct neural substrates; whether individual differences in metacognitive knowledge are primarily architectural (structural brain differences) or experiential (accumulated through learning); and how the neural substrates of metacognitive knowledge interact with emotional regulation systems.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Cognitive-Architecture]], [[Executive-Function]]

#### 66. [PROTOCOL] Metacognitive Knowledge Audit Protocol *(Line 934)*

> [!protocol] Metacognitive Knowledge Audit Protocol
> *For use during the Forethought Phase of a new learning project*
> 
> **Purpose:** Explicitly inventory and evaluate one's metacognitive knowledge before engaging a new domain or significant learning project, identifying gaps that require deliberate attention.
> 
> **Step 1 — Person Knowledge Audit:**
> - What are my characteristic strengths in acquiring new knowledge? (processing style, preferred modalities, optimal session length)
> - What are my characteristic failure modes in this type of domain? (prior experience with similar domains)
> - What motivational and affective patterns typically accompany my engagement with demanding material?
> 
> **Step 2 — Task Knowledge Audit:**
> - What does this domain's assessment structure require? (recall, application, analysis, generation)
> - What specific cognitive demands does this material make? (element interactivity, prior knowledge requirements, inferential density)
> - What does adequate comprehension look like in this domain, and how will I know I have achieved it?
> 
> **Step 3 — Strategy Knowledge Audit:**
> - What strategies do I know to be effective for this type of material and these types of assessments?
> - Under what conditions are these strategies effective, and do those conditions obtain here?
> - What is my procedural knowledge of these strategies — can I execute them, or do I only know they exist?
> 
> **Step 4 — Calibration Audit:**
> - What are the specific ways my monitoring is likely to mislead me in this domain? (fluency illusions, familiarity effects, overconfident recognition)
> - What feedback structures will I put in place to detect calibration errors?

#### 67. [PROTOCOL] Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge Development Protocol *(Line 958)*

> [!protocol] Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge Development Protocol
> *For ongoing use during domain-specific learning*
> 
> **Purpose:** Systematically develop conditional metacognitive knowledge through structured reflection on strategy effectiveness across task instances.
> 
> **Step 1 — Strategy Prediction:** Before using a strategy, explicitly record: "I am using [strategy] because [condition] obtains. I predict this will produce [outcome]."
> 
> **Step 2 — Outcome Recording:** After task completion, record actual outcomes against predictions, noting any gaps.
> 
> **Step 3 — Conditional Analysis:** For each gap between predicted and actual outcome, identify the specific condition that was absent or misidentified: Was the task demand different from what I assumed? Was my prior knowledge insufficient? Was the strategy correctly executed?
> 
> **Step 4 — Conditional Knowledge Update:** Revise your conditional metacognitive knowledge explicitly: "Strategy X is effective when condition Y obtains and ineffective when condition Z obtains."
> 
> **Step 5 — Cross-Task Pattern Recognition:** Over time, compare conditional analyses across multiple tasks to identify domain-level patterns in the conditions of strategy effectiveness.

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the difference between declarative, procedural, and conditional metacognitive knowledge?
> **A:** Declarative = knowing *that* (a strategy exists and what it does); Procedural = knowing *how* (the operational steps for executing a strategy); Conditional = knowing *when and why* (the contextual conditions under which a strategy is effective or ineffective). Conditional knowledge is the most sophisticated, most domain-specific, and most frequently neglected in instruction.

Metacognitive knowledge provides the meta-level's representational content: the standards for monitoring and the inventory of control responses.

> [!flashcard] Seed 9 — Evidence
> **Q:** What does calibration research reveal about the accuracy of metacognitive monitoring, and how can calibration be improved?
> **A:** Calibration research consistently shows overconfident monitoring in novice and moderately experienced learners in domains with delayed or indirect feedback. #### 77. [FLASHCARD] Seed 10 — Connection *(Line 1040)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** Why is conditional metacognitive knowledge the most domain-specific dimension of the taxonomy, and what are the implications for transfer?
> **A:** Conditional knowledge (knowing when and why a strategy is effective) requires knowledge of the specific task structures, epistemic conventions, and performance standards of a domain to be genuinely functional. Generic conditions (e.g., "use elaborative interrogation for expository text") are only marginally functional — truly conditional knowledge specifies conditions at the level of text genre, comprehension goal, prior knowledge threshold, and task demand. Transfer of conditional metacognitive knowledge across domains is poor because these conditions are domain-indexed.

> **Q:** How does the domain-generality debate in metacognitive knowledge research resolve, and what are the implications for instruction?
> **A:** Evidence supports a layered model: declarative metacognitive knowledge (broad cognitive principles) is domain-general and transfers; conditional metacognitive knowledge is domain-specific and transfers poorly. The general principles do not substitute for domain-specific conditional knowledge but accelerate its acquisition by providing conceptual frameworks for organizing domain-specific experience.

#### 79. [FLASHCARD] Seed 12 — Mechanism *(Line 1054)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 12 — Mechanism
> **Q:** What are the three pathways through which metacognitive knowledge is acquired?
> **A:** (1) Experience-based acquisition: patterns of success/failure encoded as conditional knowledge through direct task experience — slow, susceptible to systematic biases, dependent on accurate feedback. (2) Instruction-based acquisition: explicit transmission of declarative metacognitive knowledge, providing conceptual categories that structure subsequent experience. (3) Social transmission: expert-novice dialogue, observational learning, and internalization of regulatory speech (Vygotsky) — transmits conditional knowledge that would otherwise require years to develop experientially.

#### 81. [FLASHCARD] Seed 14 — Process *(Line 1068)*

> [!flashcard] Seed 14 — Process
> **Q:** Why is the retrieval timing of metacognitive judgments of learning significant for calibration accuracy?
> **A:** Immediate JOLs are dominated by fluency and familiarity effects from recent processing — material that was just studied is highly accessible, producing inflated confidence. Delayed JOLs (made after a delay allowing processing-fluency effects to dissipate) are substantially more accurate predictors of actual retention because they depend more on genuine memory strength signals and less on transient fluency. This implies learners should use delayed JOLs and retrieval-based tests rather than rereading fluency as mastery indicators.
> **Source:** Phase IV.5
