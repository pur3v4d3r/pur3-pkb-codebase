#### 4. [DEFINITION] Surface Code *(Line 202)*

> [!definition] Surface Code
> The surface code is the verbatim linguistic representation of the text — the specific words, syntactic structures, and grammatical forms as they appeared in the original text. It is the most transient of the three levels of representation, decaying rapidly in memory under normal reading conditions. Readers retain surface code only for the most recently processed clauses; within a few sentences, verbatim memory for specific phrasing degrades precipitously while meaning-level representations persist. The surface code is functionally important during reading — it carries syntactic cues, emphasis, and stylistic markers — but it is not the target of comprehension.
> 
> **Boundary:** The surface code is not what comprehension produces and is not what skilled reading targets. The ability to reproduce exact phrasing does not constitute evidence of understanding; conversely, the inability to remember exact wording does not indicate failure of comprehension. Sachs's (1967) classic experiments demonstrated that surface form is forgotten while propositional content persists even within seconds of processing.
> 
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Working-Memory]], [[Reading-Fluency]]

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 209)*

> [!definition] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> The textbase is a propositional representation of the semantic content of the text — the network of propositions that can be derived from the text through semantic analysis, stripped of surface linguistic variation. A proposition, in the technical sense employed here, is the smallest unit of meaningful content that can receive a truth value. It is represented as a predicate-argument structure: e.g., FAIL(TREATY) or CAUSE(MUTUAL-DISTRUST, FAIL(TREATY)). The textbase is a network of such propositions connected by coherence relations — reference (shared arguments), causation, contrast, and elaboration — that spans the semantic content of the entire text.
> 
> **Boundary:** The textbase represents what the text *asserted*, not what the described situation *is like*. A complete textbase supports recall of the text's claims and can sustain answers to text-explicit questions. It does not, however, necessarily support the generation of text-implicit inferences, the application of knowledge to novel problems, or the explanation of mechanisms underlying described events. A reader with a rich textbase but no situation model can answer "what did the text say?" but struggles consistently with "what does this mean in practice?" or "why is this the case?"
> 
> **Common Misconception:** The most consequential misconception in applied reading practice is conflating textbase-level comprehension with genuine understanding. The ability to summarize a text's propositional content — even in one's own words — does not demonstrate situation model construction if the summary merely recombines the text's explicit propositions without generating inferentially derived content. Paraphrase tests are textbase tests, not situation model tests.
> 
> **See also:** [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Chunking]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Kintsch, 1998) *(Line 218)*

> [!definition] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Kintsch, 1998)
> The situation model is a multidimensional mental representation of the situation, state of affairs, events, or abstract domain described or argued about in a text. It integrates information derived from the text with information drawn from the reader's prior knowledge, world knowledge, and episodic memories to produce a representation of the *referent world* — what the text is about — rather than the text itself. The situation model captures the causal structure, the spatial configuration, the temporal sequence, the agents and their goals, and the mechanisms underlying the described phenomena, organized into a coherent simulation.
> 
> **Boundary:** A situation model is not identical to the reader's general prior knowledge activated by the text, nor is it identical to the text's propositional content. It is a newly constructed, text-specific representation that synthesizes both. Two readers with identical prior knowledge but reading different texts will construct different situation models; two readers with different prior knowledge but reading the same text will also construct different situation models. The situation model is always a product of the intersection of text and reader.
> 
> **Historical Development:** The term was introduced by [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|van Dijk and Kintsch (1983)]] drawing partly on Johnson-Laird's contemporaneous concept of [[Mental-Models|mental models]] (1983). The two constructs overlap substantially — Zwaan & Radvansky (1998) note that "situation model" tends to appear in discourse processing research while "mental model" appears in reasoning research, but both refer to analogical, simulation-based representations of described states of affairs. The situation model concept has been further developed through the Event Indexing Model's five-dimensional structural account (Zwaan et al., 1995) and through embodied simulation accounts (Zwaan, 2004; Barsalou, 1999) that propose the simulation is implemented in perceptual and motor systems.
> 
> **Research Status:** The situation model is one of the most extensively validated constructs in cognitive psychology. Converging evidence from reading time studies, think-aloud protocols, primed recognition experiments, inference probing paradigms, and neuroimaging consistently supports its psychological reality as a distinct level of representation beyond the textbase.
> 
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Mental-Models]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch]]

#### 7. [INSIGHT] Untitled *(Line 235)*

> [!insight] Untitled
> The referential shift from textbase to situation model is cognitively analogous to the difference between memorizing a map's legend and actually developing spatial knowledge of the territory. The legend tells you what the symbols represent; spatial knowledge lets you navigate routes the map never depicted, estimate travel times between locations, and identify alternate paths when a route is blocked. Situation models are navigational knowledge. Textbases are legend knowledge. Navigation requires the former; examination performance often only requires the latter — which is why examination performance is a poor proxy for genuine understanding.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Local Coherence *(Line 242)*

> [!definition] Local Coherence
> Local coherence refers to the connectedness of adjacent or nearby textual units — the degree to which each sentence or clause can be linked to its immediate neighbors through shared reference, causal connection, or explicit discourse connectors. Local coherence is maintained when the reader can track how the current sentence relates to the previous one. It is primarily a textbase-level property and is supported by surface cues: repeated nominals, pronoun referents, temporal connectors ("then," "subsequently"), and causal connectors ("therefore," "consequently").
> 
> **Boundary:** Local coherence is necessary but not sufficient for situation model construction. A text can be locally coherent — each sentence following naturally from the last — while being globally incoherent, conveying no comprehensible overall situation.
> 
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Model]]

#### 9. [DEFINITION] Global Coherence *(Line 249)*

> [!definition] Global Coherence
> Global coherence refers to the unity and consistency of the mental representation as a whole — the degree to which all elements of the constructed representation fit together into a unified, non-contradictory situation model. Global coherence is a situation-model-level property: it requires that the reader have integrated not just adjacent sentences but all relevant portions of the text with each other and with prior knowledge into a representation in which the described agents, events, causal sequences, spatial contexts, and temporal structure form an integrated whole.
> 
> **Boundary:** Global coherence requires substantially more inferential work than local coherence, because it demands the simultaneous integration of text content with prior knowledge across multiple representational dimensions. When domain knowledge is insufficient to support the required inferences, global coherence cannot be achieved even when local coherence is fully maintained — a pattern characteristic of expert-difficulty texts read by domain novices.
> 
> **Research Status:** Global coherence failures are the dominant comprehension failure mode for skilled readers dealing with unfamiliar domains. The interaction between text coherence and reader knowledge is one of the most robustly established findings in the applied reading research literature (McNamara & Kintsch, 1996).
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]]

#### 12. [KEY-CLAIM] Untitled *(Line 283)*

> [!key-claim] Untitled
> The C-I model proposes that text comprehension proceeds through two distinct phases: a fast, constraint-unconstrained **construction phase** that generates an initial, partially incoherent representation drawing on text propositions and weakly associated prior knowledge; and an **integration phase** that applies a constraint-satisfaction process to the constructed representation, suppressing elements that fail to cohere with the emerging interpretation and strengthening elements that do. These two phases operate cyclically across reading — at roughly each sentence or clause boundary — rather than as two sequential passes over the entire text.

#### 13. [INSIGHT] Untitled *(Line 290)*

> [!insight] Untitled
> The C-I model has a counterintuitive but practically significant implication: knowledge that is "wrong" in the sense of being context-inappropriate is nonetheless *activated* during the construction phase. Comprehension breakdowns can therefore occur not because relevant knowledge fails to activate, but because the integration process fails to adequately suppress inappropriate associations — a pattern documented in poor comprehenders by Gernsbacher and colleagues. This mechanism also explains why expert readers in an unfamiliar domain sometimes construct worse situation models than expected: prior knowledge activates strongly but in ways that compete with rather than support correct interpretation of the new domain's claims.

#### 14. [DEFINITION] Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser, 1995) *(Line 299)*

> [!definition] Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser, 1995)
> The Event Indexing Model proposes that readers construct situation models as multidimensional event spaces, tracking information simultaneously across five situational dimensions: (1) **temporal** — when events occur and how they are ordered; (2) **spatial** — where events occur and how agents and objects are positioned; (3) **causal** — what events causally produce what other events; (4) **intentional** — what goals agents are pursuing and how their actions serve those goals; and (5) **protagonist/entity** — who is present, what their states are, and how their participation changes over the course of the text. Changes in the situation along any dimension signal an event boundary and trigger increased processing effort, indexed empirically by reading time increases at situational transition points.
> 
> **Boundary:** The five dimensions were specified for narrative texts. For expository and argumentative texts, interpretive adaptation is required: the "intentional" dimension maps onto authorial argumentative goals and the goals of theorists discussed; the "causal" dimension maps onto explanatory and inferential connections rather than narrative event chains.
> 
> **Research Status:** Each dimension has received substantial empirical support through reading time and recognition memory studies. Causal discontinuities produce the largest reading time increases, suggesting the causal dimension is the most cognitively central — consistent with Constructionist Theory's explanation assumption and with the foundational causal network research of Trabasso & van den Broek (1985).
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Textbase]]

#### 16. [INSIGHT] Untitled *(Line 323)*

> [!insight] Untitled
> The suppression mechanism has a practically significant implication for reading strategy. Strategies that increase the salience of task-relevant information — pre-reading structural surveys, scenario priming questions, active annotation of causal structure — may work partly by providing a richer set of constraints for the integration phase to operate on, making suppression of irrelevant activations more efficient. Strategies that do not provide such constraints — passive re-reading, indiscriminate highlighting — leave the integration phase with insufficient guidance, potentially resulting in incomplete suppression and a less coherent situation model.

#### 17. [KEY-CLAIM] Untitled *(Line 332)*

> [!key-claim] Untitled
> Constructionist Theory's specification of *which* inferences are generated automatically is the theoretical foundation for the prescriptive claim that reading strategies should explicitly target elaborative inference generation. Causal antecedent inferences (explaining why events occurred) and goal-motive inferences (explaining what agents are trying to achieve) are generated automatically because they directly serve the coherence and explanation assumptions. But elaborative depth — the richness of the situation model beyond minimal causal coherence — is *not* automatic. It requires deliberate prompting. This is why passive reading by skilled readers produces situation models that are causally coherent but mechanistically thin.

#### 19. [DEFINITION] Bridging Inference *(Line 361)*

> [!definition] Bridging Inference
> A bridging inference is an inference generated to establish local coherence between adjacent or nearby propositions — to bridge an apparent gap in the text's explicit content that would otherwise produce a coherence failure. When a reader encounters "Mary put the cake in the oven. When the timer rang, she took it out and cut it," the bridging inference BAKED(cake) is required to make the second sentence cohere with the first: without it, the referent of "it" is ambiguous and the causal sequence connecting the two clauses is unclear.
> 
> **Boundary:** Bridging inferences are generated automatically and obligatorily during skilled reading; they are the minimum inferential requirement for maintaining local textbase coherence. Their automatic generation constitutes evidence of textbase construction, not of situation model construction. A reader who generates only bridging inferences — sufficient to connect each sentence to its neighbors — has a locally coherent textbase, not a situation model.
> 
> **Research Status:** Extensively supported by reading time paradigms; bridging inference generation is indexed by increased reading times at inference-requiring locations, with reliable effect sizes across multiple research groups.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

#### 20. [DEFINITION] Elaborative Inference *(Line 370)*

> [!definition] Elaborative Inference
> An elaborative inference is an inference generated to enrich the situation model beyond the minimum required for local coherence — adding mechanistic depth, causal explanations, spatial structure, or integration with prior knowledge that the text implies but does not provide. When a reader processing a description of enzymatic catalysis generates a mental image of molecular geometry, or when a reader of a historical account infers the political pressures that must have shaped a particular decision, they are generating elaborative inferences that directly instantiate situation model dimensions.
> 
> **Boundary:** Elaborative inferences are not generated automatically as a default during reading. They require deliberate cognitive engagement, are strongly modulated by prior knowledge availability and reading goals, and will not appear spontaneously in the situation model of a reader who is processing the text passively. This is the primary mechanistic explanation for why passive reading — even by skilled readers — produces shallow situation models.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Elaborative inference generation is sometimes perceived as a sign of distracted or unfocused reading — that the reader is "adding things that aren't there." The opposite is true. Elaborative inference generation is the primary mechanism of deep comprehension; think-aloud studies consistently show that better comprehenders generate more elaborative inferences, not fewer.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]]

#### 21. [DEFINITION] Causal Inference *(Line 379)*

> [!definition] Causal Inference
> A causal inference is an inference specifying causal antecedents or consequences of described events — the *why* and *how* underlying the text's narrative or explanatory structure. Causal inferences are generated with higher priority and greater automaticity than other elaborative inference types, consistent with Constructionist Theory's explanation assumption. They are the primary mechanism through which the causal dimension of the situation model (per the Event Indexing Model) is populated, and their quality is the strongest single predictor of subsequent comprehension performance.
> 
> **Research Status:** Among the most robustly established findings in reading research. Trabasso & van den Broek (1985) demonstrated that causal connectivity — the number of causal connections a text event has within the causal network — predicts recall probability more reliably than serial position or subjective importance ratings.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Schema-Theory]]

#### 22. [INSIGHT] Untitled *(Line 394)*

> [!insight] Untitled
> The cognitive difficulty of constructing a situation model is inversely related to the quality and relevance of prior knowledge. When [[Schema-Theory|prior knowledge schemas]] provide rich, well-organized structures into which incoming text content can be rapidly assimilated, the demand on working memory is reduced — situation model construction work is partially offloaded onto long-term memory schemas. When prior knowledge is absent or poorly organized, every aspect of the situation model must be constructed from scratch within working memory's limited capacity. This is why [[Cognitive-Load-Theory|cognitive load theory]]'s concept of germane load — the cognitive effort devoted to schema construction and automation — is so relevant to reading comprehension: reading demanding texts builds schemas that subsequently reduce the cost of comprehending similar texts, expanding the effective working memory available for situation model construction.

#### 24. [WARNING] Untitled *(Line 439)*

> [!warning] Untitled
> **The Over-Annotation Trap**: The most common annotation pathology is not under-annotation but over-annotation — marking so much text that annotations cease to carry signal. Over-annotation typically co-occurs with fluency illusions: the reader feels that highlighting and marking demonstrate engagement, while this activity actually substitutes for the effortful inferential work that situation model construction requires. If annotation feels comfortable and automatic, it is almost certainly capturing textbase content rather than situation model structure. A useful heuristic: if you are annotating more than 10-15% of text content, you are annotating propositions, not architecture.

#### 25. [BEST-PRACTICE] Untitled *(Line 446)*

> [!best-practice] Untitled
> **Causal Chain Annotations (C-dimension):** Use directional markers (→) to make explicit the causal relationships between described events, mechanisms, or claims. When a text implies causation without stating it, writing the causal annotation IS the bridging inference made explicit and externalized: "[Event/state A] → [Event/state B] because [your generated causal explanation]." Causal annotations directly target the dimension of the situation model that most strongly predicts comprehension quality and transfer.

#### 26. [BEST-PRACTICE] Untitled *(Line 449)*

> [!best-practice] Untitled
> **Dimensional Gap Annotations (T, S, I, E-dimensions):** When you notice that one of the Event Indexing dimensions is unclear — you have lost track of when, where, who, or why — annotate the gap explicitly rather than reading over it: "T?: unclear when this follows from [prior event]" or "I?: unclear why this agent is pursuing this goal at this point." Gap annotations make comprehension failures explicit and located, converting vague confusion into specific, addressable deficits.

#### 27. [BEST-PRACTICE] Untitled *(Line 452)*

> [!best-practice] Untitled
> **Integration Annotations:** When you recognize a connection between current content and earlier text, other texts, or prior knowledge, annotate the connection immediately with a linking label: "CONNECTS TO: [[relevant-prior-concept]]" or "CONTRADICTS: [prior claim]." These annotations externalize the lateral structure of the situation model — the connections between sub-models and between text content and world knowledge — which are the most fragile elements of an emerging situation model and the first to be lost under reading time pressure.

#### 28. [BEST-PRACTICE] Untitled *(Line 455)*

> [!best-practice] Untitled
> **Question Annotations:** The most powerful single annotation type for situation model construction. Rather than noting that something is interesting, note the question it raises: "WHY does this follow from the previous argument?" or "WHAT WOULD FALSIFY this claim?" or "HOW does this mechanism account for [counterexample you are generating]?" Question annotations activate elaborative inference generation in a way that assertive annotations do not — they create an obligation to generate the answer, which is the act of situation model construction itself.

#### 29. [BEST-PRACTICE] Untitled *(Line 458)*

> [!best-practice] Untitled
> **Compression Annotations:** After annotating a section, attempt to compress your marginal notes into a single sentence capturing the causal structure of the section. If the compression cannot be produced, your annotations captured detail, not structure. The compression annotation at the section end is the highest-level summary of your situation model for that section.

#### 30. [WARNING] Untitled *(Line 471)*

> [!warning] Untitled
> **Common Annotation Mistakes:** (1) Highlighting without commentary — highlighted text is a flag that content seemed important, not a record of why it mattered or how it connects to anything. (2) Annotating as a substitute for comprehension monitoring — marking a confusing passage without articulating what is confusing does not repair the situation model gap. (3) Single-pass annotation — the most effective annotation systems involve at minimum a structural first pass (seed the situation model) and a detailed second pass (annotate dimensions and connections); single-pass annotation typically results in textbase-level notes with minimal structural capture.

> 
> **[[Schema-Theory]]** — Schema theory provides the prior knowledge architecture that situation model construction draws upon. The C-I model's construction phase is the mechanism by which schema-based activation occurs; the integration phase is the mechanism by which schema content is assimilated into the emerging situation model. A reader's existing schemas determine which elaborative inferences are possible and which Event Indexing dimensions can be rapidly populated. Schema theory specifies *from what* situation models are built; situation model theory specifies *what* is built.
> 
> **[[Mental-Models-Theory]]** — Johnson-Laird's mental models (1983) and van Dijk & Kintsch's situation models are closely related, near-contemporaneous constructs arising independently in reasoning and discourse processing research respectively. Both posit that comprehension produces analogical simulations rather than propositional copies of the described domain. Mental Models Theory was developed primarily in the context of deductive reasoning; Situation Model Theory in the context of text comprehension. Understanding both yields a unified account of how analogical representation supports both reasoning and reading — and explains why the same reader who constructs a good situation model from a text also tends to reason better about the domain the text describes.
> 
> **[[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]** — The working memory constraints literature maps directly onto situation model construction: capacity predicts comprehension quality because the maintenance of the current-state situation model representation and the generation of elaborative inferences are both working memory-intensive operations. The [[Working-Memory-—-Baddeley-Model|Baddeley model]]'s episodic buffer is the most plausible locus for situation model maintenance, though this remains theoretically contested.
> 
> **[[Advance-Organizers]]** — Pre-reading situation model seeding operationalizes advance organizer theory at the representational level. The advance organizer provides a high-level organizational framework that facilitates assimilation of subsequent content; the mechanism through which it operates is the reduction of working memory demand for situation model construction — the framework already exists, so incoming information can be mapped onto it rather than requiring construction from scratch.
> 
> **[[Self-Explanation-Effect]]** — Self-explanation is the most empirically robust reading strategy that situation model theory predicts should work. The mechanism is direct: self-explanation prompts elaborative causal inference generation (the reader must explain *why*, not just encode *what*), directly instantiating the processes most critical for situation model construction. Chi et al.'s (1994) finding that self-explainers significantly outperform re-readers on inference questions provides the primary empirical validation of situation model theory's prescriptive claims.
> 
> **[[Metacognitive-Monitoring]]** — Situation model construction requires continuous comprehension monitoring. The metacognitive monitoring literature specifies the detection mechanisms (comprehension monitoring signals, feelings of knowing); situation model theory specifies what is being monitored — the coherence and dimensional completeness of the emerging simulation. The DIME monitoring protocol developed in this report is the application of metacognitive monitoring theory to situation model-specific tracking.
> 
> **[[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]]** — Situation model construction is, functionally, the deepest available level of processing — it requires integrating new information with prior knowledge at the mechanism and causal level, producing elaborated, distinctively encoded memory traces. The levels of processing framework explains *why* situation model construction produces better retention; situation model theory explains *what* deep processing actually consists of in the reading context.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 549)*

> [!definition] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> A multidimensional mental simulation of the situation, event sequence, or abstract domain described in a text. The situation model represents what the text is *about* — the referents, causal structures, agents, goals, and mechanisms of the described world — rather than the text's propositional content. It integrates text-derived propositions with prior world knowledge through elaborative inference generation.
> 
> **Boundary:** Not identical to prior knowledge, not identical to the text's propositions. A newly constructed, text-specific representation synthesizing both. Two readers with different prior knowledge will construct different situation models from the same text.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** That comprehension is demonstrated by ability to paraphrase the text's content. Paraphrase demonstrates textbase access; situation model quality is indexed by the ability to correctly answer inference questions about content the text did not explicitly state.
> 
> **Research Status:** Among the most extensively validated constructs in cognitive psychology; supported by reading time, recall, recognition, inference probing, and neuroimaging paradigms.
> 
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Mental-Models]], [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Schema-Theory]]

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 560)*

> [!definition] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> A network of propositions representing the semantic content of a text, abstracted from surface linguistic form. Each proposition is a predicate-argument structure capturing a minimal unit of assertable meaning. The textbase represents *what the text asserted*, organized by coherence relations.
> 
> **Boundary:** Textbase construction is necessary but not sufficient for genuine comprehension. A complete textbase supports text-explicit recall but not text-implicit inference generation.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** That fluent paraphrase of a text's content demonstrates deep understanding. Paraphrase is a textbase-level achievement.
> 
> **See also:** [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Construction-Integration Model (Kintsch, 1988, 1998) *(Line 569)*

> [!definition] Construction-Integration Model (Kintsch, 1988, 1998)
> A computational model of text comprehension proposing two processing phases: (1) a construction phase in which text propositions and weakly associated prior knowledge elements are rapidly and non-selectively activated; and (2) an integration phase in which constraint-satisfaction spreading activation strengthens coherent elements and suppresses incoherent ones, producing a coherent interpretation. The two phases operate cyclically at each reading unit.
> 
> **Boundary:** Primarily a process model of *how* situation models are built. Its structural account of *what* they contain is supplemented by the Event Indexing Model.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Working-Memory]], [[Schema-Theory]]

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser, 1995) *(Line 576)*

> [!definition] Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser, 1995)
> A structural model of situation models proposing that readers track information simultaneously across five situational dimensions: temporal (when), spatial (where), causal (why/how events connect), intentional (agent goals and plans), and protagonist/entity (who is present and in what state). Situational discontinuities on any dimension produce reading time increases, with causal discontinuities producing the largest effects.
> 
> **Boundary:** Originally specified for narrative texts; application to expository texts requires adaptation — "intentionality" maps onto authorial argumentative goals; "causality" maps onto logical and explanatory connections.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]]

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Bridging Inference *(Line 583)*

> [!definition] Bridging Inference
> An inference generated obligatorily to establish local coherence between adjacent propositions — to bridge a referential or causal gap that would otherwise produce a textbase-level coherence failure. Generated automatically during skilled reading; indexed by increased reading times at gap-requiring locations.
> 
> **Boundary:** Bridging inferences establish textbase coherence, not situation model depth. Their automatic generation does not constitute situation model construction.
> 
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Elaborative Inference *(Line 590)*

> [!definition] Elaborative Inference
> An inference generated to enrich the situation model beyond minimal local coherence — adding mechanistic depth, causal explanations, spatial structure, predictive content, or prior knowledge integration. Not generated automatically; requires deliberate strategy activation and is strongly modulated by prior knowledge availability.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** That elaborative inference generation is a sign of unfocused reading. In fact, it is the primary mechanism of deep comprehension.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]]

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Local vs. Global Coherence *(Line 597)*

> [!definition] Local vs. Global Coherence
> **Local coherence**: the connectedness of adjacent text units — whether each sentence links to its immediate neighbors. A textbase-level property. **Global coherence**: the unity and consistency of the situation model as a whole — whether all representation elements integrate into a non-contradictory simulation. A situation-model-level property. A text can be locally coherent throughout while failing to produce global coherence.
> 
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Schema-Theory]]

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Suppression (Gernsbacher, 1990) *(Line 602)*

> [!definition] Suppression (Gernsbacher, 1990)
> The active inhibition of initially activated but ultimately context-inappropriate information during comprehension. The mechanism underlying the C-I model's integration phase. Individual differences in suppression efficiency reliably predict comprehension skill independently of vocabulary and decoding ability.
> 
> **Boundary:** Suppression is active and measurable in real time (through primed recognition paradigms showing reduced accessibility of suppressed content); it is distinct from passive forgetting.
> 
> **See also:** [[Working-Memory]], [[Automaticity]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]]

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Constructionist Theory (Graesser, Singer & Trabasso, 1994) *(Line 609)*

> [!definition] Constructionist Theory (Graesser, Singer & Trabasso, 1994)
> A theory of inference generation specifying that causal antecedent inferences (explaining why events occurred) and goal-motive inferences are generated automatically during comprehension, while predictive and elaborative inferences are not. Based on three assumptions: reader goal (inference generation is goal-modulated), coherence (readers default to seeking coherent representations), and explanation (readers automatically seek causal explanations).
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]]

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Foregrounding Effect *(Line 614)*

> [!definition] Foregrounding Effect
> The empirical finding that information causally relevant to current events and protagonist goals is more accessible in working memory during reading than other text content, even when the other content is equally important by global coherence standards. Reflects working memory's selective maintenance of the current-state situation model representation.
> 
> **Research Status:** Well-established through multiple experimental paradigms; consistent with both the Landscape Model and Event Indexing Model predictions.
> 
> **See also:** [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Working-Memory-Capacity]]

#### 52. [TENSION] Propositional vs. Analogical/Embodied Representation *(Line 686)*

> [!tension] Propositional vs. Analogical/Embodied Representation
> **The Tension:** Whether situation models are propositional structures (enriched networks of propositions with situational specifications) or genuinely analogical/simulation-based representations implemented in perceptual and motor systems.
> 
> **Position A — Propositional (Kintsch, 1998):** The C-I model treats situation models as formally propositional. "Simulation" is a useful metaphor, not a literal description of the representational format.
> 
> **Position B — Analogical/Embodied (Zwaan, 2004; Barsalou, 1999):** The Event Indexing Model and embodied simulation accounts propose that situation models are genuinely simulation-based, activating perceptual and motor systems. Neuroimaging evidence (somatosensory cortex activation during comprehension of action sentences) supports the analogical position.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** The embodied simulation account has gained substantially more support over the past 20 years. The scientific debate has shifted from "propositional vs. analogical" to "how much is simulation, and across which domains?"
> 
> **Why It Matters:** The representational format has direct implications for optimal reading strategies. Analogical accounts suggest that active visualization and motor imagery may specifically enhance situation model quality.
> 
> **See also:** [[Mental-Models]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Propositional-Representation]]

#### 53. [TENSION] Automatic vs. Strategic Situation Model Construction *(Line 700)*

> [!tension] Automatic vs. Strategic Situation Model Construction
> **The Tension:** Whether situation model construction is primarily automatic (driven by default processes in skilled reading) or primarily strategic (requiring deliberate metacognitive intervention).
> 
> **Position A — Primarily Automatic (Constructionist Theory):** Skilled readers automatically generate causal coherence-preserving inferences as a default; situation model construction is a by-product of skilled fluent reading.
> 
> **Position B — Primarily Strategic (Self-explanation literature):** Chi et al. (1994) and related research demonstrate that even skilled readers construct substantially better situation models when using deliberate self-explanation strategies, indicating that automatic processes are not sufficient for deep comprehension.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** A middle position is best supported: automatic processes produce a minimally coherent situation model; strategic processes are necessary to extend it to the depth required for inferential transfer.
> 
> **This Report's Position:** Strategic processes are necessary for situation model depth that supports transfer; automatic processes establish minimum causal coherence but not explanation-level depth.
> 
> **See also:** [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]], [[Automaticity]]

#### 54. [TENSION] Working Memory Capacity as Fixed vs. Functionally Expandable *(Line 714)*

> [!tension] Working Memory Capacity as Fixed vs. Functionally Expandable
> **The Tension:** Whether WMC constraints on situation model construction are stable individual difference parameters or are effectively expandable through strategy development and domain knowledge acquisition.
> 
> **Position A — Fixed:** The WMC literature has historically treated reading span as stable. Low-WMC readers are structurally disadvantaged.
> 
> **Position B — Functionally Expandable:** Cognitive load theory's schema automation account and [[Verbal-Efficiency-Theory|Verbal Efficiency Theory]] suggest that increasing fluency and domain knowledge expands working memory *available* for situation model construction by reducing lower-level processing demands.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** WMC itself shows limited trainability, but functional WMC available for situation model construction can be expanded through strategy instruction and domain knowledge development.
> 
> **See also:** [[Working-Memory-Capacity]], [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Automaticity]]

> [!protocol] Pre-Reading Situation Model Seeding Protocol (derived from C-I Model + Advance Organizer theory)
> *Purpose: construct a low-resolution situation model before full-text reading, reducing working memory demands during the reading itself.*
> 
> **Step 1 — Scenario Priming (2-3 min):**
> Write one sentence: *What situation, mechanism, event sequence, or domain is this text going to help me understand?* This is a scenario question, not a topic question. It should name the referent world, not the text's subject heading.
> 
> **Step 2 — Structural Survey (5-10 min):**
> Read, in order: abstract or introduction, all section headings and subheadings, conclusion. Do not read body paragraphs. Goal: extract the causal skeleton — the main claims and the logical connectives between them.
> 
> **Step 3 — Prior Knowledge Activation (2-3 min):**
> Write 3-5 sentences: what you already know about the described situation or domain, including what causes what, what the main mechanisms are, and what the main controversies are. This is your initial situation model.
> 
> **Step 4 — Prediction Generation:**
> Write two specific predictions about what the text will argue, demonstrate, or claim. Predictions commit you to an existing situation model that the text will confirm, disconfirm, or complicate.

#### 72. [PROTOCOL] The DIME Annotation Protocol (derived from Event Indexing Model — original synthesis) *(Line 836)*

> [!protocol] The DIME Annotation Protocol (derived from Event Indexing Model — original synthesis)
> *Purpose: externalize the situation model across all five situational dimensions through targeted annotation of structure rather than content.*
> 
> **The Five Annotation Types:**
> 
> **C — Causal annotations (highest priority):** Use → to mark causal relationships. When the text implies causation without stating it, write the causal annotation as the generated inference: "[A] → [B] because [your causal explanation]." This is the most critical annotation type.
> 
> **T — Temporal annotations:** Mark when the temporal sequence is unclear: "T?: unclear when this follows [prior event]." Or confirm sequence: "T✓: this follows [timeframe] after [prior event]."
> 
> **S — Spatial annotations:** For texts describing physical mechanisms, spatial configurations, or structural relationships: "S: [brief spatial description or diagram]."
> 
> **I — Intentional annotations:** Mark the goals and motivations of agents (narrative) or the argumentative purposes of authors/theorists (expository): "I: [Agent/author] is trying to [goal] because [your explanation]."
> 
> **E — Entity annotations:** Track key actors' state changes: "E[Name]: [current state, changed from prior state]."
> 
> **Quality check:** After each section, verify all five dimensions are represented (or deliberately absent for good reason). Unmonitored dimensions are the typical location of situation model gaps.

#### 73. [PROTOCOL] The Annotation Threshold Decision Framework *(Line 854)*

> [!protocol] The Annotation Threshold Decision Framework
> *Purpose: determine when annotation is worth interrupting reading flow.*
> 
> **Stop and annotate when ANY of the following:**
> 1. A causal mechanism has just been explained — mark with C-annotation
> 2. A foundational distinction has been drawn that will bear weight throughout the text
> 3. A coherence failure has been detected — flag the dimensional gap explicitly
> 4. An integration opportunity has been recognized — annotate the connection immediately
> 5. A claim has been made that requires justification the text has not provided — mark "WHY?"
> 
> **Do not annotate for:** Interesting but structurally peripheral content; examples and illustrations unless the example IS the argument; material already integrated from prior knowledge.
> 
> **Target density:** 10-15% of text content annotated. If exceeding this, you are annotating propositions, not architecture.

#### 74. [PROTOCOL] The Section-Ending Compression Protocol *(Line 869)*

> [!protocol] The Section-Ending Compression Protocol
> *Purpose: convert annotations into situation model elements; diagnose model quality at section boundaries.*
> 
> **After each major section:**
> 
> **Step 1:** Close the text.
> 
> **Step 2 — Produce the three-part narrative:** "This section is about [X], because [Y], which means [Z]." X = topic; Y = causal grounding (why X is so, or why the author claims what they claim); Z = implication (what follows for the broader argument or for your understanding).
> 
> **Step 3 — Diagnose:**
> - All three parts: situation model functioning for this section.
> - X but not Y/Z: textbase, not situation model. Reread targeting causal structure.
> - Cannot produce X clearly: coherence failure. Return to text; targeted rereading at section opening and closing.
> 
> **Step 4:** Write the compression as a margin annotation at the section end. This is the highest-level situation model summary for this section.
