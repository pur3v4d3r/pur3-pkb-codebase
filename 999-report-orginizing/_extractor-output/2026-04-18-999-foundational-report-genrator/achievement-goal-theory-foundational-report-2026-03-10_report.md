#### 2. [DEFINITION] Achievement Goal *(Line 286)*

> [!definition] Achievement Goal
> An achievement goal is a cognitive representation of the purpose a person is pursuing in an achievement context. It specifies what "success" means to the person, which in turn determines what counts as a competent performance, which tasks feel threatening or inviting, and which behaviours are rational responses to difficulty. Achievement goals are not merely preferences or wishes; they are organising frameworks that structure perception, attention, affect, and effort. The concept should not be confused with specific performance targets (e.g., "I want to score 90 on this test") — AGT goals are about the *type* of competence being sought, not the *level* of performance being targeted.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Undifferentiated Conception of Ability *(Line 295)*

> [!definition] Undifferentiated Conception of Ability
> In an undifferentiated conception of ability, effort and ability are treated as the same thing — or at minimum, as inseparable. A person who works harder is understood to be more able, and ability is seen as something that grows through effort. Under this conception, success means improving and learning, and failure means simply "I haven't learned this yet." This conception is developmentally earlier (young children tend to operate under it) and is characteristic of what Nicholls called *task involvement*. It corresponds roughly to what [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]] would later call the [[Growth-Mindset-Implicit-Theories-of-Intelligence|Growth Mindset]].

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Differentiated Conception of Ability *(Line 298)*

> [!definition] Differentiated Conception of Ability
> In a differentiated conception of ability, effort and ability are understood as *separate* entities that can partially cancel each other out. If I have to try very hard to accomplish something that others accomplish easily, this is evidence that my ability is lower, not higher. Under this conception, demonstrating high ability means achieving more with less effort (or the same outcomes with less effort) relative to others. This is the conception of ability operative in *ego involvement*, and it is cognitively demanding — children typically do not fully develop it until late childhood or early adolescence. It corresponds to what [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]] calls the [[Fixed-Mindset|Fixed Mindset]] or [[Entity-Theory-of-Intelligence|Entity Theory of Intelligence]].

#### 5. [KEY-CLAIM] The Foundational Proposition of AGT *(Line 301)*

> [!key-claim] The Foundational Proposition of AGT
> The standard of competence a person is using to evaluate their performance determines the psychological meaning of effort, difficulty, and failure — and therefore determines the motivational consequences of those experiences. Achievement Goal Theory proposes that much of the variability in adaptive and maladaptive motivation in achievement contexts can be traced to which standard of competence is operative at a given moment.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Task Involvement *(Line 310)*

> [!definition] Task Involvement
> Task involvement is a motivational state in which the person's primary concern is mastering the task itself — understanding it, improving at it, making progress. In this state, the person is using their own prior performance or the inherent demands of the task as the standard of reference. Success is defined as learning, understanding, or improving, and effort is seen as the natural means of achieving these things. Task involvement is associated with deep engagement, challenge-seeking, and resilience in the face of failure.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Ego Involvement *(Line 313)*

> [!definition] Ego Involvement
> Ego involvement is a motivational state in which the person's primary concern is demonstrating superior ability — or, critically, avoiding the demonstration of inferior ability — relative to others. In this state, normative social comparison is the operative standard. Effort becomes a double-edged sword: it is necessary for success, but high effort combined with poor relative performance is damning evidence of low ability. Ego involvement is associated with defensive strategies, challenge avoidance (because failure is more costly), and vulnerability when outcomes are unfavourable.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Mastery Goal (Learning Goal, Task Goal) *(Line 320)*

> [!definition] Mastery Goal (Learning Goal, Task Goal)
> A mastery goal is an achievement goal in which the person defines success as developing competence — acquiring new skills, deepening understanding, or improving relative to one's own prior performance. The standard of reference is intrapersonal and/or task-inherent. Mastery goals are sometimes called *learning goals* (Dweck's terminology) or *task goals* (Nicholls's terminology). They are associated with the adoption of deep cognitive strategies, help-seeking when confused, persistence in the face of difficulty, positive affect during challenge, and long-term retention. Under mastery-oriented conditions, effort is unambiguously a sign of engagement and is positively valued.

#### 9. [DEFINITION] Performance Goal (Ego Goal) *(Line 323)*

> [!definition] Performance Goal (Ego Goal)
> A performance goal is an achievement goal in which the person defines success as outperforming others or appearing more competent than others. The standard of reference is normative — anchored in social comparison. Performance goals are sometimes called *ego goals* or *ability goals*. The motivational consequences of performance goals are, as we will see, more complex than early theorists recognised, and they have been significantly refined by subsequent work distinguishing approach from avoidance orientations.

#### 11. [COUNTER-ARGUMENT] The Performance Goal Anomaly *(Line 347)*

> [!counter-argument] The Performance Goal Anomaly
> Harackiewicz, Barron, and Elliot (1998) and subsequent longitudinal studies demonstrated that performance-approach goals — goals focused on outperforming others — were positively associated with course grades in college settings, even while mastery goals better predicted long-term interest and intrinsic motivation. This finding created a genuine theoretical problem for the dichotomous model and motivated the development of more differentiated frameworks.

#### 12. [DEFINITION] Performance-Approach Goal *(Line 354)*

> [!definition] Performance-Approach Goal
> A performance-approach goal is oriented toward the positive possibility of normative competence — demonstrating that one is more competent than others. The motivational focus is appetitive: moving toward success in the normative sense. Under this orientation, competition and social comparison are energising rather than threatening, and success in outperforming others is the primary desired outcome.

#### 13. [DEFINITION] Performance-Avoidance Goal *(Line 357)*

> [!definition] Performance-Avoidance Goal
> A performance-avoidance goal is oriented toward the negative possibility of normative incompetence — avoiding demonstrating that one is less competent than others. The motivational focus is aversive: moving away from the threat of looking stupid or inferior. Under this orientation, the primary motivation is fear of failure and social humiliation rather than desire for success. This goal type is consistently associated with the most maladaptive outcomes in the research literature: surface processing, avoidance of challenging tasks, high anxiety, poor long-term learning, and burnout.

#### 14. [DEFINITION] Mastery-Approach Goal *(Line 368)*

> [!definition] Mastery-Approach Goal
> Oriented toward the positive possibility of intrapersonal competence: developing one's skills, deepening understanding, mastering the task. The person asks "Am I learning as much as possible?" This is the goal orientation most consistently associated with adaptive outcomes: deep processing, intrinsic motivation, positive affect, and genuine intellectual engagement.

#### 15. [DEFINITION] Mastery-Avoidance Goal *(Line 371)*

> [!definition] Mastery-Avoidance Goal
> Oriented toward the negative possibility of intrapersonal incompetence: failing to learn, declining in ability, not living up to one's prior standard. The person asks "Am I avoiding regression?" This goal type is theoretically interesting but practically less common and somewhat harder to study. It may be common among experts, high-performing athletes, or older adults who are concerned with avoiding cognitive decline. It is associated with worry and exhaustion even when performance remains high.

#### 16. [DEFINITION] Performance-Approach Goal *(Line 374)*

> [!definition] Performance-Approach Goal
> Oriented toward the positive possibility of normative competence: outperforming others, winning, appearing highly able. The person asks "Am I better than others?" Associated with competitive engagement, higher grades in evaluative settings, but typically not with deep intrinsic interest or durable learning.

#### 17. [DEFINITION] Performance-Avoidance Goal *(Line 377)*

> [!definition] Performance-Avoidance Goal
> Oriented toward the negative possibility of normative incompetence: avoiding appearing stupid or inferior relative to others. The person asks "Am I avoiding looking worse than everyone else?" Consistently associated with the most maladaptive outcomes in the research: surface processing, challenge avoidance, high anxiety, poor intrinsic motivation, and diminished learning.

#### 18. [EVIDENCE] 2×2 Model Validation *(Line 380)*

> [!evidence] 2×2 Model Validation
> Elliot and McGregor (2001) validated the 2×2 model across two studies using confirmatory factor analysis, demonstrating that the four goal types are empirically distinct (not merely conceptually separable) and have different patterns of relationships to cognitive strategies, exam anxiety, and academic performance. Their results showed: mastery-approach goals predicted deep processing and positive test affect; performance-approach goals predicted surface processing but also higher exam performance; performance-avoidance goals predicted surface processing, disorganisation, and test anxiety; mastery-avoidance goals predicted disorganisation and worry despite strong GPA association.

#### 19. [INSIGHT] The Pattern Across Frameworks *(Line 387)*

> [!insight] The Pattern Across Frameworks
> Looking across the dichotomous, trichotomous, 2×2, and 3×2 models, a clear pattern emerges: theoretical progress in AGT has consistently been driven by two moves — finer-grained differentiation of the competence standard (what counts as "doing well") and the incorporation of fundamental motivational distinctions (approach vs. avoidance, task vs. self vs. other). Each refinement has clarified the empirical landscape by separating what earlier models conflated.

#### 20. [DEFINITION] Motivational Climate *(Line 394)*

> [!definition] Motivational Climate
> Motivational climate refers to the achievement goal structure perceived to be operative in a particular setting. It is determined by the structural features of the environment — how tasks are designed, how authority is distributed, how recognition is given, how students are grouped, how evaluation is conducted, and how time is managed. Ames (1992) used the acronym TARGET (Task, Authority, Recognition, Grouping, Evaluation, Time) to identify the key structural dimensions that shape perceived motivational climate. Crucially, motivational climate influences which goals individuals *adopt* in that setting — independently of their personal goal orientation tendencies.

#### 22. [KEY-INSIGHT] Self-Handicapping as a Motivational Adaptation *(Line 418)*

> [!key-insight] Self-Handicapping as a Motivational Adaptation
> The phenomenon of self-handicapping — creating obstacles to one's own performance in advance, so that failure is attributable to the obstacle rather than to low ability — is a direct prediction of AGT under performance-avoidance conditions. Students who stay up all night before an exam, who avoid studying a subject they fear they may be bad at, or who procrastinate until productive work becomes impossible are often engaging in self-handicapping. From the outside, these behaviours look like laziness or poor self-regulation. From the inside, they are rational strategies for protecting the self-concept in an environment where failure signals low ability.

#### 23. [EXAMPLE] The Study Strategy Divergence *(Line 429)*

> [!example] The Study Strategy Divergence
> Research by [[Ames-and-Archer|Ames and Archer]] (1988) and numerous subsequent studies has documented systematic differences in reported learning strategies between students in mastery-oriented versus performance-oriented conditions. Mastery-oriented students more frequently report using tactics associated with deep processing: self-testing for understanding, connecting new material to prior knowledge, seeking to understand the logic behind procedures rather than merely memorising them, and identifying areas of confusion and seeking help. Performance-oriented students (particularly those high on performance-avoidance) more frequently report surface strategies: rote memorisation, selective attention to likely test items, and avoidance of difficult material that might undermine confidence.

#### 24. [THOUGHT-EXPERIMENT] The Diagnostic Power of a Failure *(Line 440)*

> [!thought-experiment] The Diagnostic Power of a Failure
> Imagine two students, both of whom fail the same difficult exam. Student A, operating under mastery goals, asks: "What did I misunderstand? What do I need to study differently?" She schedules more practice, seeks a study partner, asks the instructor about the concepts she missed, and returns to the next exam with stronger understanding. Student B, operating under performance-avoidance goals, asks: "What does this say about my ability?" He concludes that he is not a "maths person," avoids asking for help (which would reveal his confusion publicly), reduces the effort he invests in subsequent assessments (offering himself the excuse of "not really trying"), and eventually withdraws from maths-related coursework. These divergent responses are not determined by the failure itself — they are determined by the goal framework through which the failure is interpreted.

#### 25. [OBSERVATION] The Power of Perceived Climate *(Line 451)*

> [!observation] The Power of Perceived Climate
> Studies by Ames and Archer (1988), Midgley and colleagues, and Ntoumanis and Biddle (1999) in sports contexts have consistently shown that *perceived* motivational climate — how students perceive the goal structure of their environment — predicts their goal adoption, strategy use, and affect more strongly than their measured individual goal orientation tendencies. This is a remarkable finding with strong practical implications: environment matters enormously. The goal orientation a person endorses in a research questionnaire may not be the goal orientation they actually pursue in a particular classroom.

#### 27. [BEST-PRACTICE] Task Design for Mastery Climate *(Line 473)*

> [!best-practice] Task Design for Mastery Climate
> When designing learning tasks, include features that make growth visible and valued: open-ended components that reward elaborated thinking, opportunities for revision and improvement, problems that can be approached multiple ways, and explicit framing that positions difficulty as an expected and valuable part of learning rather than a signal of failure. Avoid designs that make ability rankings immediately obvious (e.g., public performance boards, visibly stratified task sheets) unless the competitive signal is intentional and the context supports it.

#### 28. [BEST-PRACTICE] Assessment Language for Mastery Orientation *(Line 480)*

> [!best-practice] Assessment Language for Mastery Orientation
> Feedback that emphasises the learner's developing understanding ("You've clearly grasped the underlying principle here, and your next step might be to test this understanding by applying it to a novel problem") is more mastery-consistent than feedback that emphasises rank or comparison ("You scored in the top quartile"). This does not mean eliminating grading — it means attending carefully to what the *language* of evaluation signals about what matters.

#### 29. [IMPORTANT] WEIRD Sample Problem *(Line 493)*

> [!important] WEIRD Sample Problem
> Much of the foundational AGT research was conducted with North American and Western European samples, predominantly college students. The generalisability of AGT predictions to non-Western cultures — where, for example, the relationship between social comparison and motivation may differ significantly, or where collective rather than individual competence standards are normative — is an area of ongoing investigation and legitimate uncertainty.

#### 30. [IMPORTANT] Measurement Inconsistency *(Line 496)*

> [!important] Measurement Inconsistency
> The field has struggled with inconsistent measurement. Different research groups have used different questionnaire instruments to assess the same goal orientations, often with different item wording that creates subtle but consequential differences in what is actually being measured. Meta-analyses (notably Hulleman et al., 2010) have found that the specific wording of performance-goal items moderates the direction of their relationship to outcomes — performance goals assessed with "doing better than others" language have somewhat different relationships to outcomes than those assessed with "demonstrating ability" language. This measurement heterogeneity makes the integration of findings across studies more difficult than it might appear.

#### 31. [IMPORTANT] The Performance-Approach Controversy *(Line 499)*

> [!important] The Performance-Approach Controversy
> The motivational status of performance-approach goals remains the most actively contested area in the field. Harackiewicz, Barron, and colleagues have defended the adaptive potential of performance-approach goals, particularly in competitive educational settings where high grades have real-world consequences. Elliot, Dweck, and others have argued that these effects are contextually limited and do not generalise to deep learning, creativity, or sustained motivation. A nuanced reading of the evidence suggests that performance-approach goals may be conditionally adaptive — beneficial for short-term competitive performance under high competence beliefs, but not beneficial for long-term intrinsic motivation or for learning that requires genuine understanding rather than strategic performance.

#### 32. [WARNING] Avoiding the Mastery-Goal Prescriptivism Trap *(Line 502)*

> [!warning] Avoiding the Mastery-Goal Prescriptivism Trap
> A common misreading of AGT — one worth flagging explicitly — is to conclude that mastery goals are simply "good" and performance goals are simply "bad," and that the practical implication is to lecture students or athletes into adopting mastery goals. The evidence does not support this prescriptive conclusion, for several reasons. First, the relationship between goal orientation and outcomes is moderated by multiple factors including competence beliefs, task type, and cultural context. Second, simply telling people to adopt mastery goals rarely works — goal orientation is shaped more strongly by environmental structures than by explicit exhortation. Third, there are genuine motivational functions served by performance goals in certain contexts. The appropriate practical response to AGT is to design environments that support mastery orientation, not to diagnose and correct individuals who are responding rationally to their environment.

AGT predicts that mastery-oriented learners will engage in more sophisticated metacognitive monitoring — because their goal is to understand, not merely to perform, they have a stronger incentive to accurately detect their own comprehension gaps. Performance-avoidance learners, by contrast, may engage in motivated inaccuracy: deliberately *not* monitoring their understanding too carefully, because accurate self-assessment might reveal incompetence. >

An entity theory of intelligence (fixed mindset) predicts performance goal adoption because, if ability is fixed, the rational goal is to demonstrate it rather than develop it. An incremental theory (growth mindset) predicts mastery goal adoption because, if ability can be developed, then development is the rational focus. The two frameworks are therefore deeply complementary, with mindset theory explaining *why* individuals differ in goal orientation tendencies, and AGT specifying the *mechanisms* through which those tendencies translate into learning behaviours.
> 
> - **[[Metacognition-&-Critical-Thinking|Metacognition & Critical Thinking]]** — Critical thinking requires intellectual risk-taking: the willingness to question assumptions, to entertain uncomfortable possibilities, and to revise beliefs in light of evidence. All of these behaviours are more natural under mastery orientation than under performance orientation. In a performance-oriented environment, intellectual risk-taking is potentially costly (you might be wrong, publicly), while in a mastery-oriented environment it is potentially rewarding (you might learn something new).
> 
> - **[[Schema-Theory|Schema Theory]]** — Schema formation and elaboration depend on the kind of deep processing that mastery goals preferentially support. When learners are focused on understanding rather than performance, they are more likely to engage in the effortful assimilation and accommodation that schema development requires. This connection suggests that schema formation is not merely a cognitive process but a motivationally conditioned one — the conditions under which deep learning occurs are also the conditions that AGT identifies as mastery-conducive.
> 
> - **[[Critical-Thinking-in-Education|Critical Thinking in Education]]** — Instructional approaches that aim to develop critical thinking are unlikely to succeed in performance-climate environments, because critical thinking requires the very intellectual behaviours — challenge engagement, error tolerance, cognitive risk — that performance-avoidance goals suppress. This suggests that critical thinking pedagogy and motivational climate design are not separate problems but deeply interconnected aspects of a single design challenge.
> 
> The pattern these connections reveal is significant: Achievement Goal Theory is not merely a theory about motivation. It is a theory about the motivational conditions under which deep cognitive engagement becomes possible. It connects to virtually every aspect of your PKB focused on how learning actually works — because goal orientation shapes not just how much people learn, but what kind of learning they are capable of in a given moment.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Performance Goal (Ames, 1992; Nicholls's "Ego Goal") *(Line 565)*

> [!definition] Performance Goal (Ames, 1992; Nicholls's "Ego Goal")
> An achievement goal in which success is defined as demonstrating superior competence relative to others. The standard of reference is normative. In later frameworks, distinguished as performance-approach (approaching normative superiority) and performance-avoidance (avoiding normative inferiority). Associated with surface cognitive processing, vulnerability to failure, and contextually variable outcomes.

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Performance-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & Harackiewicz, 1996) *(Line 568)*

> [!definition] Performance-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & Harackiewicz, 1996)
> An achievement goal oriented toward avoiding the demonstration of normative incompetence — the goal of not looking worse than others. Associated with the most consistently maladaptive outcomes in the research literature: surface processing, challenge avoidance, high anxiety, disorganised study, and diminished intrinsic motivation. Arises most naturally when both competence concerns are salient and self-efficacy is low.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Mastery-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & McGregor, 2001) *(Line 571)*

> [!definition] Mastery-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & McGregor, 2001)
> An achievement goal oriented toward avoiding intrapersonal incompetence — not failing to learn, not declining in ability relative to one's own prior standard. Theoretically predicted by the 2×2 framework and empirically supported in expert and high-performer populations. Associated with worry and perfectionism-related anxiety even at high performance levels.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Ego Involvement (Nicholls, 1984) *(Line 574)*

> [!definition] Ego Involvement (Nicholls, 1984)
> A motivational state in which ability concerns are salient and the person is preoccupied with demonstrating (or protecting) their ability relative to others. Ego involvement is the psychological state corresponding to performance goal adoption, particularly performance-avoidance. It activates the differentiated conception of ability, making effort a double-edged sword.

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Motivational Climate (Ames, 1992) *(Line 577)*

> [!definition] Motivational Climate (Ames, 1992)
> The goal structure perceived to be operative in a particular achievement environment, shaped by structural features including task design, authority distribution, recognition practices, grouping, evaluation methods, and time management (TARGET). Motivational climate is distinct from individual goal orientation — it is an environmental variable that powerfully shapes which goals individuals adopt in a given setting.

#### 44. [DEFINITION] 2×2 Framework (Elliot & McGregor, 2001) *(Line 580)*

> [!definition] 2×2 Framework (Elliot & McGregor, 2001)
> A theoretical model of achievement goals that crosses two dimensions — goal content (mastery vs. performance) with motivational valence (approach vs. avoidance) — to yield four distinct goal types: mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance. Currently the dominant framework in the field, though contested regarding the adaptive potential of performance-approach goals.

#### 45. [DEFINITION] TARGET Framework (Ames, 1992) *(Line 583)*

> [!definition] TARGET Framework (Ames, 1992)
> An acronym identifying the key structural dimensions of learning environments that shape motivational climate: Task (design and complexity), Authority (distribution of decision-making power), Recognition (how achievement is acknowledged), Grouping (how learners are sorted and structured), Evaluation (how competence is assessed), and Time (how temporal pressure is structured). Used as an audit and design tool for motivational climate engineering.

#### 46. [DEFINITION] Differentiated Conception of Ability (Nicholls, 1984) *(Line 586)*

> [!definition] Differentiated Conception of Ability (Nicholls, 1984)
> The implicit theory of ability in which effort and ability are understood as separate entities: high effort combined with poor performance implies low ability, while high performance with low effort implies high ability. This conception is cognitively more complex than the undifferentiated conception, emerges later developmentally, and is the basis of ego involvement and performance goal orientation.

#### 47. [CITE] Nicholls, J. G. (1984). Achievement motivation: Conceptions of ability, subjective experience, task choice, and performance. *Psychological Review, 91*(3), 328–346. *(Line 593)*

> [!cite] Nicholls, J. G. (1984). Achievement motivation: Conceptions of ability, subjective experience, task choice, and performance. *Psychological Review, 91*(3), 328–346.
> The foundational theoretical paper in Achievement Goal Theory. Nicholls distinguishes differentiated from undifferentiated conceptions of ability and derives the task-involvement/ego-involvement distinction from this analysis. Essential reading for understanding the philosophical and developmental foundations of the theory. Directly relevant to Phases II and IV of this report.

#### 48. [CITE] Ames, C. (1992). Classrooms: Goals, structures, and student motivation. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 84*(3), 261–271. *(Line 596)*

> [!cite] Ames, C. (1992). Classrooms: Goals, structures, and student motivation. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 84*(3), 261–271.
> The seminal paper establishing the motivational climate framework and the TARGET dimensions. Ames synthesises classroom observational research with goal theory to produce a practically actionable framework for educational design. A cornerstone reference for applications in instructional and classroom design. Relevant to Phases III and V.

#### 49. [CITE] Elliot, A. J., & McGregor, H. A. (2001). A 2×2 achievement goal framework. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 80*(3), 501–519. *(Line 599)*

> [!cite] Elliot, A. J., & McGregor, H. A. (2001). A 2×2 achievement goal framework. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 80*(3), 501–519.
> The paper that introduced and validated the 2×2 framework, distinguishing mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance goals. Two studies using confirmatory factor analysis demonstrate the empirical distinctiveness of the four goal types and their differential relationships to cognitive and affective outcomes. The most influential recent theoretical paper in the field. Relevant to Phase III throughout.

#### 50. [CITE] Dweck, C. S., & Leggett, E. L. (1988). A social-cognitive approach to motivation and personality. *Psychological Review, 95*(2), 256–273. *(Line 602)*

> [!cite] Dweck, C. S., & Leggett, E. L. (1988). A social-cognitive approach to motivation and personality. *Psychological Review, 95*(2), 256–273.
> The paper that introduced the entity/incremental theory of intelligence distinction and connected it to helpless vs. mastery-oriented response patterns. Provides the cognitive-developmental foundation that explains *why* individuals differ in goal orientation tendencies. Bridges the AGT literature with the mindset literature. Directly relevant to Phase IV.

#### 51. [CITE] Hulleman, C. S., Schrager, S. M., Bodmann, S. M., & Harackiewicz, J. M. (2010). A meta-analytic review of achievement goal measures: Different labels for the same constructs or different constructs with similar labels? *Psychological Bulletin, 136*(3), 422–449. *(Line 605)*

> [!cite] Hulleman, C. S., Schrager, S. M., Bodmann, S. M., & Harackiewicz, J. M. (2010). A meta-analytic review of achievement goal measures: Different labels for the same constructs or different constructs with similar labels? *Psychological Bulletin, 136*(3), 422–449.
> A critical meta-analysis demonstrating that measurement inconsistency — specifically, differences in how performance goal items are worded — significantly moderates the effects attributed to performance goals. An essential corrective to naive readings of the empirical literature and a key text for understanding the limitations discussed in Phase V.

#### 52. [CITE] Ames, C., & Archer, J. (1988). Achievement goals in the classroom: Students' learning strategies and motivation processes. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 80*(3), 260–267. *(Line 608)*

> [!cite] Ames, C., & Archer, J. (1988). Achievement goals in the classroom: Students' learning strategies and motivation processes. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 80*(3), 260–267.
> An influential classroom study documenting systematic differences in reported learning strategies between students in mastery-oriented versus performance-oriented classes. One of the clearest empirical demonstrations of the link between motivational climate and cognitive strategy use. Relevant to Phase IV and Phase V.

#### 53. [CITE] Elliot, A. J., Murayama, K., & Pekrun, R. (2011). A 3×2 achievement goal model. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 103*(3), 632–648. *(Line 611)*

> [!cite] Elliot, A. J., Murayama, K., & Pekrun, R. (2011). A 3×2 achievement goal model. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 103*(3), 632–648.
> Introduces the 3×2 model, which distinguishes task-based, self-based, and other-based standards of competence crossed with approach-avoidance valence to yield six goal types. Represents the current frontier of AGT theoretical development. Relevant as an expansion topic and as context for Phase III's discussion of theoretical evolution.

#### 54. [CITE] Murayama, K., & Elliot, A. J. (2012). The competition–performance relation: A meta-analytic review and test of the opposing processes model of competition and performance. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(6), 1035–1070. *(Line 614)*

> [!cite] Murayama, K., & Elliot, A. J. (2012). The competition–performance relation: A meta-analytic review and test of the opposing processes model of competition and performance. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(6), 1035–1070.
> A meta-analysis examining the relationship between competitive contexts, goal adoption, and performance. Relevant to the contested question of when performance-approach goals may be adaptive. Discussed in the limitations section of Phase V.

#### 55. [METHODOLOGY-AND-SOURCES] Research Grounding for This Report *(Line 621)*

> [!methodology-and-sources] Research Grounding for This Report
> This report synthesises approximately four decades of theoretical development and empirical research in Achievement Goal Theory. The theoretical narrative draws primarily on original theoretical papers (Nicholls, 1984; Ames, 1992; Elliot, 1997; Elliot & McGregor, 2001) rather than textbook summaries, ensuring fidelity to the primary intellectual contributions. Empirical claims are grounded in peer-reviewed research, with preference given to findings replicated across multiple studies or supported by meta-analytic evidence. Where the evidence is mixed or contested — most significantly regarding performance-approach goals — this is noted explicitly with reference to the relevant evidence streams rather than resolved artificially. The mechanisms discussed in Phase IV integrate findings from correlational classroom studies, experimental manipulations, and longitudinal investigations; their causal status varies, and this variation is implicitly marked in the language used ("is associated with" vs. "causes"). Developmental claims about the differentiated/undifferentiated ability distinction draw on Nicholls's original developmental work. Cultural generalisability claims are deliberately hedged, reflecting the WEIRD-sample limitation of the primary evidence base. The practical applications in Phase V are supported by the evidence base but presented with appropriate conditionality, given that the path from laboratory findings to classroom implementation involves contextual factors not fully captured in the research literature.

#### 56. [FURTHER-EXPLORATION] Deepening Your Practice *(Line 628)*

> [!further-exploration] Deepening Your Practice

#### 57. [TOPIC-IDEA] [[Self-Determination-Theory-—-Foundational-Report|Self-Determination Theory — Foundational Report]] *(Line 630)*

> [!topic-idea] [[Self-Determination-Theory-—-Foundational-Report|Self-Determination Theory — Foundational Report]]
> Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000) is the dominant motivational framework in contemporary educational psychology and represents the most important theoretical complement and counterpoint to AGT. Where AGT focuses on the *content* of goals (what standard of competence is operative), SDT focuses on the *regulatory style* of motivation (whether motivation is autonomous/intrinsic or controlled/extrinsic). A comparative treatment would clarify the significant areas of convergence (both frameworks identify conditions that support intrinsic motivation and deep learning) and important areas of distinction (SDT's autonomy construct maps onto mastery climate but is not identical to it; AGT's approach-avoidance dimension has no direct SDT parallel). Priority: **High** — SDT is encountered throughout the learning sciences literature and fluency in both frameworks is close to essential.

#### 58. [TOPIC-IDEA] [[Motivational Climate Design — Applied Guide]] *(Line 633)*

> [!topic-idea] [[Motivational Climate Design — Applied Guide]]
> A deep dive into the practical engineering of mastery-oriented learning environments, using Ames's TARGET framework as the primary organisational lens. This report would move from the abstract dimensions (Task, Authority, Recognition, Grouping, Evaluation, Time) to specific, evidence-based design decisions at each level — covering task design principles, feedback language, grouping structures, assessment practices, and the temporal architecture of learning sequences. Would draw on both AGT-derived research and complementary work from [[Universal Design for Learning]], [[Formative-Assessment|Formative Assessment]], and [[Project-Based Learning]]. Priority: **High** — directly actionable from this foundational treatment.

#### 59. [TOPIC-IDEA] [[Implicit Theories of Intelligence & Growth Mindset — Foundational Report]] *(Line 636)*

> [!topic-idea] [[Implicit Theories of Intelligence & Growth Mindset — Foundational Report]]
> Dweck's framework on entity vs. incremental theories of intelligence is the cognitive-developmental upstream cause of goal orientation differences. Understanding *why* people adopt different goal orientations requires understanding the implicit beliefs about the nature of ability that drive those orientations. This report would cover the experimental evidence base for mindset interventions, the theoretical relationship between mindset and AGT, and the important recent replication debates that have complicated the field's earlier enthusiasm for brief mindset interventions. Priority: **High** — directly linked to this report and already referenced in the PKB.

#### 60. [TOPIC-IDEA] [[Achievement-Emotions-—-Pekrun's-Control-Value-Theory|Achievement Emotions — Pekrun's Control-Value Theory]] *(Line 639)*

> [!topic-idea] [[Achievement-Emotions-—-Pekrun's-Control-Value-Theory|Achievement Emotions — Pekrun's Control-Value Theory]]
> Reinhard Pekrun's Control-Value Theory of achievement emotions provides a rich account of the affective consequences of achievement goal orientations, including enjoyment, boredom, anxiety, pride, shame, hopelessness, and relief. Because emotional experience is a major outcome variable in AGT research, and because emotions in turn influence cognitive processing and motivation, this framework deepens the AGT account significantly. The connection between performance-avoidance goals and test anxiety, and between mastery goals and epistemic enjoyment, are particularly well-developed in Pekrun's work. Priority: **Medium** — enriches AGT but is not necessary for applying it.

#### 61. [TOPIC-IDEA] [[Assessment-Design-&-Goal-Orientation|Assessment Design & Goal Orientation]] *(Line 642)*

> [!topic-idea] [[Assessment-Design-&-Goal-Orientation|Assessment Design & Goal Orientation]]
> Assessment is the most powerful climate-signalling dimension in most formal educational settings, and yet it is also the dimension most resistant to change — external pressures (standardised testing, grading systems, institutional accountability) heavily constrain what educators can do. This report would examine the evidence on how different assessment practices (formative vs. summative, criterion-referenced vs. norm-referenced, portfolio vs. examination, self-assessment vs. external evaluation) signal different goal orientations, with attention to both the design of individual assessments and the systemic constraints that shape what is possible. Priority: **High** — assessment design is a critical application domain.

#### 62. [TOPIC-IDEA] [[Epistemic Curiosity and the Intrinsic Pleasures of Inquiry]] *(Line 645)*

> [!topic-idea] [[Epistemic Curiosity and the Intrinsic Pleasures of Inquiry]]
> This expansion would explore the motivational states that are most fully aligned with mastery-approach orientation: curiosity, wonder, the intrinsic satisfactions of understanding, and what Csikszentmihalyi calls [[Flow]] in intellectual work. It would connect the AGT prediction that mastery goals support intrinsic motivation to the broader literature on curiosity-driven learning, inquiry-based pedagogy, and the conditions under which learners become genuinely self-directed. Priority: **Medium** — enriches and extends the mastery-orientation picture into positive motivational territory.
