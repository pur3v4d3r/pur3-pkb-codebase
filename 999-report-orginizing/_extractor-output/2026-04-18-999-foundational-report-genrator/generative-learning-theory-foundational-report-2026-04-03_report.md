#### 2. [KEY-CLAIM] The Generative Learning Principle *(Line 133)*

> [!key-claim] The Generative Learning Principle
> Learning is a process of active meaning construction in which the learner generates relationships between new information and existing knowledge, experience, and mental representations. Comprehension is not received but built. Understanding is not transmitted but constructed. This principle distinguishes generative learning from all passive models of knowledge acquisition.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Generative Learning Theory (GLT) — Wittrock's Original Formulation *(Line 156)*

> [!definition] Generative Learning Theory (GLT) — Wittrock's Original Formulation
> A theory of learning and comprehension proposing that meaningful understanding arises from the learner's generative cognitive activity: the active construction of relationships between new information and existing knowledge, experiences, and mental models. Learning is characterized not by the acquisition of information presented externally but by the learner's self-generated mental elaborations, organizational structures, and integrative connections. Coined by Merlin C. Wittrock (1974).

#### 4. [SECTION-SUMMARY] Section 1 Key Takeaways *(Line 163)*

> [!section-summary] Section 1 Key Takeaways
> 1. **Reaction to passivity**: GLT arose as a reaction to both behaviorist and early cognitive models that treated the learner as a passive recipient of information. Its central innovation was placing the learner's constructive cognitive activity at the center of the learning process.
> 2. **Intellectual lineage**: GLT synthesizes and extends Piaget's constructivism, Ausubel's meaningful learning theory, and Craik & Lockhart's levels of processing framework into a pedagogically actionable model.
> 3. **Core principle**: Learning is self-generated meaning construction, not information reception. The quality of learning depends primarily on the nature and quality of the cognitive activity the learner performs, not merely on exposure to content.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] The SOI Model — Three Core Processes *(Line 183)*

> [!definition] The SOI Model — Three Core Processes
> **Selection**: The learner actively attends to and selects relevant information from the available sensory input, filtering out irrelevant or redundant material. This is not passive perception but active attentional deployment guided by prior knowledge, goals, and comprehension monitoring.
> **Organization**: The learner arranges selected information into a coherent internal structure, forming a mental representation that captures the relationships between elements (temporal, causal, hierarchical, comparative). This produces a mental model or schema fragment rather than a list.
> **Integration**: The learner connects the organized representation to relevant prior knowledge — existing schemas, mental models, experiences, and concepts held in long-term memory — producing meaningful understanding by embedding new information within an existing cognitive network.

#### 7. [CLAUDE-INSIGHT] Organization as the Bridge Between Storage and Thought *(Line 206)*

> [!claude-insight] Organization as the Bridge Between Storage and Thought
> One of the most underappreciated implications of Wittrock's SOI model is that organization is not simply a memory convenience but the precondition for genuine thought. To think with knowledge — to reason, transfer, and apply — requires that knowledge be organizationally structured. Information stored as a list cannot be used to generate inferences, because inference requires awareness of relational structure. The learner who has organized material into a causal model can ask "what would happen if...?" in a way the learner with a propositional list cannot. This is why shallow surface-level learning so often fails to transfer: the knowledge exists but lacks the organizational structure that makes it usable as a tool for thinking.

#### 8. [EXAMPLE] Integration in Practice: Learning the Concept of Natural Selection *(Line 219)*

> [!example] Integration in Practice: Learning the Concept of Natural Selection
> For a learner with prior knowledge of animal breeding (intentional artificial selection of traits), integrating the concept of natural selection involves recognizing the structural parallel: in both cases, heritable variation exists in a population; in both cases, certain variants leave more offspring than others; in both cases, trait frequencies shift across generations. The learner who generates this analogy is performing active integration — using existing knowledge as a scaffold for organizing new conceptual material. For the learner without prior knowledge of animal breeding, the same information must be organized without this integrative scaffold, demanding greater organizational effort and producing a more isolated conceptual representation. The teaching implication: effective instruction works to activate and make vivid the analogical bridges between prior and new knowledge.

#### 11. [DEFINITION] The Generation Effect *(Line 251)*

> [!definition] The Generation Effect
> The empirical finding that people remember information better when they generate it themselves — even from minimal cues — compared to when they read the same information passively. First systematically documented by Slamecka and Graf (1978), the generation effect demonstrates that the active cognitive work of producing information (completing a word stem, filling in an antonym, generating a missing relation) produces more durable memory traces than passive reading, even when the generated material is identical to what would have been read. The effect is one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology and provides direct empirical support for the core claim of Generative Learning Theory.

#### 12. [EVIDENCE] The Testing Effect as Generative Learning in Action *(Line 270)*

> [!evidence] The Testing Effect as Generative Learning in Action
> [[Testing-Effect|The testing effect]] (also called the retrieval practice effect) is one of the most practically significant findings in educational psychology: the act of retrieving information from memory — through testing, self-quizzing, flashcards, or other retrieval practice formats — produces substantially greater learning than restudying the same material for equivalent time. Roediger and Karpicke's (2006) seminal demonstration showed that students who studied a text passage once and were tested three times retained significantly more material a week later than students who spent all their time studying. The testing effect is, from the perspective of GLT, an instance of generative processing: retrieval requires active reconstruction of information from memory, strengthening both the traces themselves and the retrieval routes to them. The learner who tests themselves is generating their own understanding — making the generative principle concrete.

#### 13. [METHODOLOGY-AND-SOURCES] Measurement Note on Self-Explanation Research *(Line 291)*

> [!methodology-and-sources] Measurement Note on Self-Explanation Research
> The self-explanation literature rests primarily on think-aloud protocols in which learners verbalize their thoughts during study. This methodology captures self-explanations when they are produced, but likely underestimates self-explanation that occurs silently without verbalization. Studies comparing prompted self-explanation (where learners are explicitly instructed to explain each step) with control conditions show reliable benefits, but effect sizes vary substantially by domain, material complexity, and learner expertise. Meta-analyses by Bisra et al. (2018) found robust average effect sizes (d ≈ 0.55), though with significant moderation by content type and instruction quality.

Deep learning favors note-making practices that compel reorganization, paraphrase, and connection rather than verbatim record.

#### 16. [DEFINITION] Generative Learning Strategy *(Line 322)*

> [!definition] Generative Learning Strategy
> A deliberate cognitive procedure that a learner employs to actively construct, transform, organize, or connect new information in ways that promote meaning-making and durable retention. Generative strategies are distinguished from organizational or mnemonic strategies by their emphasis on constructive connection to existing knowledge (integration) rather than surface-level reorganization. Effective generative strategies require the learner to perform cognitive work beyond what the text or presentation itself provides — generating inferences, elaborations, structural representations, or connections that are not explicit in the material.

> [!section-summary] Section 4 Key Takeaways
> 1. **Strategy taxonomy**: Generative learning strategies include summarization, concept mapping, elaborative questioning, self-explanation, drawing/diagramming, and analogical generation. Each engages all three SOI processes and produces learning benefits beyond passive exposure.

#### 19. [DEFINITION] The Generativity Paradox *(Line 396)*

> [!definition] The Generativity Paradox
> The apparent contradiction between Generative Learning Theory's prescription that active generation promotes learning and Cognitive Load Theory's finding that excessive cognitive demand impairs learning. The paradox is resolved by distinguishing between types of cognitive load: *germane* cognitive load (effort directly invested in schema construction, which is what GLT promotes) and *extraneous* cognitive load (effort wasted on processing irrelevant elements of the learning environment, which CLT advises minimizing). Generative activities that increase germane load facilitate learning; activities that increase extraneous load without contributing to schema construction impede learning. The challenge for instructional design is to maximize the former while minimizing the latter.

#### 20. [KEY-CLAIM] The Expertise Reversal Effect *(Line 419)*

> [!key-claim] The Expertise Reversal Effect
> One of the most important empirical demonstrations of the interaction between generative learning and prior knowledge is the [[Expertise-Reversal-Effect|expertise reversal effect]], identified by Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, and Sweller (2003). This effect describes how instructional methods that benefit novice learners may actually *impair* learning in more advanced learners, and vice versa. Detailed instructional guidance, worked examples, and scaffolded prompts help novices because they reduce extraneous load during knowledge acquisition. For experts, however, the same detailed guidance becomes redundant (the expert already knows the material) and thus creates extraneous load by requiring processing of already-known information. For generative strategies, the implication runs in the other direction: generative strategies that enhance learning for intermediate and advanced learners may impose excessive extraneous load on novices who lack the foundational schema to perform the generation meaningfully. Expertise reversal means there is no universal optimal strategy — only strategies that are optimal *for a particular learner at a particular stage of expertise*.

#### 21. [CLAUDE-INSIGHT] The Metacognitive Trap of Fluency *(Line 436)*

> [!claude-insight] The Metacognitive Trap of Fluency
> One of the most consequential practical implications of the research on desirable difficulties and generative learning is the systematic mismatch between subjective learning fluency and objective learning outcomes. Passive reading, re-reading, massed practice, and highlighting all feel productive because they produce a sense of familiarity and fluency — the material becomes easier to process with each repetition. But this fluency is largely a measure of processing *ease*, not knowledge *depth*. Generative strategies — self-testing, spaced retrieval, interleaved practice, self-explanation — often feel more difficult and produce less immediate fluency, but they engage the precise cognitive mechanisms that build durable and transferable representations. The learner who reliably chooses the study strategy that *feels* most productive is systematically selecting the strategy that produces the most forgettable learning. Metacognitive awareness of this trap — understanding that cognitive effort and short-term discomfort are signals of productive learning rather than failure — may be the most practically valuable insight that educational psychology has to offer.

#### 22. [SECTION-SUMMARY] Section 5 Key Takeaways *(Line 439)*

> [!section-summary] Section 5 Key Takeaways
> 1. **The generativity paradox resolves into a distinction of load types**: Generative activities increase germane cognitive load (schema-building effort), not total cognitive load indiscriminately. The key constraint is that germane load must remain within working memory capacity, which depends critically on learner prior knowledge.
> 2. **Expertise reversal**: Generative strategies that benefit intermediate and advanced learners may impose excessive cognitive demands on novices, who lack the foundational schemas to make generation productive. Instructional design must be calibrated to the learner's developmental stage.
> 3. **Productive failure and desirable difficulties**: Deliberately designed challenges — problem exploration before instruction, spaced and interleaved retrieval practice, reduced scaffolding — exploit generative learning mechanisms to produce superior long-term outcomes, even at the cost of reduced short-term fluency and increased subjective difficulty.
> 4. **Counterintuitive performance metrics**: What feels like productive studying (fluent re-reading) is often ineffective; what feels difficult (self-testing, interleaved practice) is often highly effective. Metacognitive re-calibration of study habits requires understanding this counterintuitive relationship.

#### 24. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) *(Line 466)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)
> Mayer's theoretical framework for understanding how people learn from words and pictures together. CTML predicts that presenting information in both verbal and visual forms — when the formats are designed to minimize extraneous load and support generative processing — produces superior learning to either format alone. The theory generates twelve instructional principles tested through controlled experiments, all derived from the core premise that well-designed multimedia supports the cognitive processes of selection, organization, and integration through which meaning is actively constructed.

#### 25. [EXAMPLE] The Signaling Principle in Practice *(Line 483)*

> [!example] The Signaling Principle in Practice
> Consider a multimedia lesson on the cardiovascular system. The unsignaled version presents text and diagrams without organizational cues. Learners must themselves determine which elements are most important, how they are organized, and what the central structural principles are — substantial generative work that may exceed working memory capacity for novice learners. The signaled version adds: bolded key terms when first introduced, a section heading outlining the main organizational structure, numbered steps in explanatory passages, and arrows in diagrams pointing to the most important elements. These signals do not add information — the same conceptual content is present in both versions. But they dramatically reduce the extraneous load of self-determining organizational structure, freeing working memory for the generative process of connecting the organized information to prior [[Prior-Knowledge|prior knowledge]] schemas. Studies by Mayer and colleagues consistently show that such signaling produces approximately 10-15% improvement in retention and transfer across a variety of content areas.

#### 26. [SECTION-SUMMARY] Section 6 Key Takeaways *(Line 502)*

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> 1. **CTML is GLT's multimedia extension**: Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning builds directly on Wittrock's SOI model, extending generative learning principles to dual-channel environments with explicit cognitive load management.
> 2. **Twelve principles as design specifications**: CTML's evidence-based principles (Coherence, Signaling, Contiguity, Modality, Redundancy, etc.) translate GLT's abstract prescriptions into concrete instructional design specifications, each with documented empirical support.
> 3. **Transfer advantage**: The primary learning advantage of generative multimedia learning appears on transfer measures, not retention of verbatim content. This reflects GLT's prediction that generative processing builds mental models (structural understanding) rather than verbatim records, and mental models are precisely what transfer requires.
> 4. **Generative drawing**: Learner-generated drawings produce superior learning to pre-provided drawings, directly paralleling the generation effect. The cognitive work of constructing a spatial representation, not the representation itself, is the active ingredient.

#### 28. [DEFINITION] The Judgment of Learning (JOL) Problem *(Line 537)*

> [!definition] The Judgment of Learning (JOL) Problem
> Judgments of Learning are the metacognitive assessments learners make about how well they have learned material — "I understand this," "I could explain this," "I would get this right on a test." Research consistently shows that JOLs made immediately after studying are systematically overconfident, particularly for material studied through passive reading. Re-reading a passage increases fluency, and learners confuse fluency with understanding. Generative strategies — particularly self-testing and self-explanation — tend to produce more accurate JOLs because they require the learner to actually retrieve or produce the material, not merely recognize it. Accurate JOLs are important because they determine whether learners continue studying, switch to more demanding strategies, or declare themselves done. Systematically inflated JOLs from passive study lead to premature termination of study and consequent knowledge gaps.

#### 31. [TENSION] Conceptual Tension: Process vs. Product *(Line 582)*

> [!tension] Conceptual Tension: Process vs. Product
> GLT claims that the *process* of generation drives learning, not the quality of the generated product. But in practice, research demonstrates that generative activities benefit learning most when the products generated are coherent, meaningful, and connected to accurate prior knowledge. Random generation, generation of misconceptions, and generation of irrelevant associations can actually *impede* learning by encoding incorrect or unhelpful connections. This suggests that "good generation" is somewhere between process and product — dependent on both the cognitive act of construction and the accuracy of the knowledge scaffolding that makes that construction meaningful. Precisely characterizing the interaction between process quality and product quality remains an open theoretical challenge.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Generative Learning Theory (GLT) *(Line 705)*

> [!definition] Generative Learning Theory (GLT)
> A constructivist account of learning developed by Merlin Wittrock (1974) that proposes learning is an active process of generating meaning by constructing connections between incoming information and existing knowledge. GLT holds that the learner does not passively record information but actively builds a mental model through selection, organization, and integration processes. See also: [[Constructivism]], [[Cognitive-Theory-of-Multimedia-Learning]], [[ICAP-Framework]], [[Information-Processing-Theory]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Selection (SOI Component) *(Line 708)*

> [!definition] Selection (SOI Component)
> The first process in Wittrock's Select-Organize-Integrate (SOI) model, referring to the allocation of attentional resources to particular elements of the learning environment. Selection is not passive filtering but an active, top-down constructive process in which the learner's prior knowledge and goals shape what is attended to. Expert learners select more efficiently because they can recognize the most conceptually important elements. See also: [[Attention]], [[Prior-Knowledge-Activation]], [[Selective-Attention]]

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Organization (SOI Component) *(Line 711)*

> [!definition] Organization (SOI Component)
> The second process in Wittrock's SOI model, referring to the learner's active construction of internal structures — hierarchies, sequences, causal networks, categories — that give selected information relational form. Organization transforms isolated elements into structured representations, providing the architecture into which integration can occur. Analogous to the chunking process in expertise development. See also: [[Chunking]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Mental-Models]], [[Concept-Maps]]

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Integration (SOI Component) *(Line 714)*

> [!definition] Integration (SOI Component)
> The third and highest-level process in Wittrock's SOI model, referring to the learner's connection of newly organized material to existing knowledge schemas in long-term memory. Integration is what distinguishes generative learning from mere organization: the new structure is not just internally coherent but is connected to the learner's broader knowledge network, providing the associative pathways through which the new material will be accessed, applied, and built upon. See also: [[Prior-Knowledge]], [[Spreading-Activation]], [[Meaningful-Learning-Theory]], [[Advance-Organizer]]

#### 40. [DEFINITION] The Generation Effect *(Line 717)*

> [!definition] The Generation Effect
> The empirical finding, first systematically documented by Slamecka and Graf (1978), that generating information from minimal cues produces better memory retention than reading the same information passively. The effect is robust across materials (words, facts, concepts), tasks (antonyms, rhymes, completions), and populations, providing direct empirical support for GLT's core claim that active construction produces more durable representations than passive encoding. See also: [[The-Generation-Effect]], [[Testing-Effect]], [[Retrieval-Practice]]

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Self-Explanation Effect *(Line 720)*

> [!definition] Self-Explanation Effect
> The consistent empirical finding that learners who actively generate explanations of presented material to themselves — particularly for worked examples and complex texts — achieve substantially better comprehension and transfer than those who study the same material without self-explaining. First systematically documented by Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann, and Glaser (1989). The effect reflects the simultaneous engagement of all three SOI processes: selection of relevant elements, organization into a causal or logical structure, and integration with prior principles. See also: [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[self-explanation]], [[Elaborative-Interrogation]]

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) *(Line 723)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)
> Mayer's theoretical framework for multimedia learning, built on Wittrock's SOI model and extended with Baddeley's dual-channel cognitive architecture. CTML holds that meaningful learning from words and pictures requires active selection, organization into separate verbal and pictorial models, and integration of those models with each other and with prior knowledge. The theory generates twelve empirically tested instructional design principles. See also: [[Cognitive-Theory-of-Multimedia-Learning]], [[Multimedia-Learning]], [[Dual-Coding-Theory]]

#### 43. [DEFINITION] ICAP Framework *(Line 726)*

> [!definition] ICAP Framework
> Chi and Wylie's (2014) framework organizing learning activities into four hierarchical levels — Interactive, Constructive, Active, Passive — based on the cognitive engagement they require. The framework proposes a learning hierarchy (I > C > A > P) grounded in the prediction that higher-level activities produce more complex, accurate, and integrated knowledge. ICAP provides a complementary taxonomy to GLT, specifying the behavioral activities that instantiate different degrees of generative cognitive engagement. See also: [[ICAP-Framework]], [[Constructivism]], [[Active-Learning]]

#### 44. [DEFINITION] Desirable Difficulties *(Line 729)*

> [!definition] Desirable Difficulties
> Robert Bjork's concept describing learning conditions that increase short-term difficulty while improving long-term retention and transfer. Desirable difficulties impose germane cognitive load — effort invested in the cognitive processes that build durable understanding — rather than extraneous load. Examples include spaced practice, interleaved practice, reduced feedback, and varied conditions of learning. See also: [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Spacing-Effect]], [[interleaving]]

#### 45. [DEFINITION] Productive Failure *(Line 732)*

> [!definition] Productive Failure
> Kapur's instructional approach in which learners attempt to solve complex problems *before* receiving formal instruction, exploiting the learning benefits of pre-instruction exploration. The "failure" of these initial attempts (most students cannot solve the problems correctly without instruction) is "productive" because it activates and differentiates prior knowledge, creates schema-preparation for subsequent instruction, and produces awareness of knowledge gaps that direct attention to critical elements. See also: [[Productive-Failure]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Prior-Knowledge-Activation]]

#### 46. [DEFINITION] Mathemagenic Activities *(Line 735)*

> [!definition] Mathemagenic Activities
> Coined by Rothkopf (1970), referring to learner behaviors that generate learning — literally "give birth to learning." Wittrock used this concept to ground GLT: the learner's own cognitive activities, not the information encountered, are the proximal cause of learning. Mathemagenic activities include generating questions, making predictions, creating summaries, constructing visual representations, and any other behaviors that require active construction of meaning. The concept foregrounds learner activity rather than instructional design as the primary cause of learning outcomes.

#### 47. [DEFINITION] Expertise Reversal Effect *(Line 738)*

> [!definition] Expertise Reversal Effect
> The finding by Kalyuga et al. (2003) that instructional methods beneficial for novices may impair learning in more advanced learners, and vice versa. For generative strategies, this implies that strategies highly effective for intermediate learners may impose excessive cognitive load on novices who lack the foundational knowledge to make generation productive. The effect is a challenge for universal instructional prescriptions and demands adaptive, expertise-calibrated instruction. See also: [[Expertise-Reversal-Effect]], [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]]

#### 48. [DEFINITION] Judgment of Learning (JOL) *(Line 741)*

> [!definition] Judgment of Learning (JOL)
> A metacognitive judgment made by a learner about how well they have encoded material — "I understand this," "I would recall this on a test," "I am confident about this claim." JOLs are systematically inflated by passive study strategies that produce fluency without genuine understanding. Generative strategies tend to produce more accurate JOLs because they require actual retrieval and production, revealing comprehension failures that re-reading masks. Accurate JOLs are essential for effective self-regulated learning. See also: [[Metacognition]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 53. [TENSION] Generation vs. Guidance: How Much Scaffolding is Optimal? *(Line 789)*

> [!tension] Generation vs. Guidance: How Much Scaffolding is Optimal?
> GLT prescribes generative activity, but cognitive load theory shows that unsupported generation by novices can impair learning. Yet Kapur's productive failure research shows that pre-instruction generation improves subsequent learning. These positions are not irreconcilable but they require careful specification: the question is not "generation or not" but "what type and degree of generation, for what type of learner, at what stage of expertise?" Resolving this tension requires a more fine-grained account of the interactions between generation, prior knowledge, and instructional support than current theory provides.

#### 54. [TENSION] Generative Learning in Social vs. Individual Contexts *(Line 792)*

> [!tension] Generative Learning in Social vs. Individual Contexts

Moreover, not all social interaction is learning-productive. #### 55. [OPEN-QUESTION] Does Generative Learning Theory Apply Across Cultures? *(Line 795)*

> [!open-question] Does Generative Learning Theory Apply Across Cultures?
> GLT was developed and primarily tested in Western, individualistic, academically oriented educational contexts. Whether the theory's prescriptions generalize across educational cultures that emphasize different relationships between teacher authority, student initiative, and knowledge construction — particularly collectivist cultures with strong transmission-model educational traditions — is an open empirical question. The cognitive mechanisms GLT describes (selection, organization, integration) are presumably universal features of human cognition; the instructional conditions that support or hinder them may be culturally variable.

#### 56. [DEBATE] AI and the Future of Generative Learning *(Line 798)*

> [!debate] AI and the Future of Generative Learning
> The proliferation of large language models generates a live debate within educational psychology: should AI tools be used to scaffold and support student generative processing (serving as dialogue partners, providing hints, presenting challenges), or does their availability fundamentally alter the educational ecosystem in ways that make traditional GLT prescriptions obsolete? Critics argue that in a world where AI can generate high-quality explanations, summaries, and analyses on demand, the question of whether students should generate these things themselves becomes a question about cognitive development and human flourishing, not just learning efficiency. Proponents of human generative learning argue that the cognitive processes generated by active learning — not the products — are what matter, and that AI tools that pre-empt those processes impoverish the learner regardless of output quality.

The pre-instruction struggle (usually including incorrect attempts) is "productive" because it activates, differentiates, and organizes prior knowledge relevant to the target concept, creating a "prepared mind" for instruction by establishing what needs to be explained. Exploits GLT's selection-organization-integration mechanism at the level of prior knowledge preparation.

#### 76. [FLASHCARD] Concept — Desirable Difficulties** | Q: What are desirable difficulties and why does subjective difficulty predict learning benefit? *(Line 889)*

> [!flashcard] Concept — Desirable Difficulties** | Q: What are desirable difficulties and why does subjective difficulty predict learning benefit?
> A: Bjork's concept: conditions that increase short-term effort and difficulty (spaced practice, interleaved practice, reduced feedback) improve long-term retention and transfer. Difficulty is "desirable" when it increases *germane* cognitive load — effort invested in schema construction — rather than extraneous load. Subjective difficulty often signals productive generative processing precisely because generation is cognitively demanding, and cognitive demand is the signature of schema-building work.

#### 77. [FLASHCARD] Process — CTML's Three Assumptions** | Q: What are the three foundational assumptions of Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning? *(Line 892)*

> [!flashcard] Process — CTML's Three Assumptions** | Q: What are the three foundational assumptions of Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning?
> A: (1) Dual Channel — humans have separate processing channels for verbal/auditory and visual/pictorial information; (2) Limited Capacity — each channel can process only a limited number of elements simultaneously; (3) Active Processing — meaningful learning requires active selection, organization into verbal and pictorial mental models, and integration of those models with each other and with prior knowledge. These map directly onto Wittrock's SOI model within Baddeley's architectural framework.

#### 78. [FLASHCARD] Application — Self-Explanation Strategy** | Q: How should self-explanation be deployed as a study strategy, and what conditions maximize its benefit? *(Line 895)*

> [!flashcard] Application — Self-Explanation Strategy** | Q: How should self-explanation be deployed as a study strategy, and what conditions maximize its benefit?
> A: Self-explain by pausing while studying worked examples or complex texts to verbalize (or write) *why* each step is valid, what principle it illustrates, and how it connects to prior knowledge. Maximize benefit by: (1) using for material with inferential gaps rather than fully explicit text; (2) building sufficient prior knowledge first (self-explanation requires something to integrate with); (3) comparing your explanation against the source to identify errors; (4) focusing on causal and structural relationships, not surface description.

#### 79. [FLASHCARD] Connection — GLT and Retrieval Practice** | Q: How does retrieval practice implement GLT's generative learning principle? *(Line 898)*

> [!flashcard] Connection — GLT and Retrieval Practice** | Q: How does retrieval practice implement GLT's generative learning principle?
> A: Retrieval practice (self-testing, spaced repetition, practice tests) is generatively superior to re-reading because retrieval requires the active reconstruction of information from memory — engaging the selection and integration processes that constitute generative encoding. Each retrieval attempt strengthens both the memory trace and the retrieval routes to it. GLT explains *why* the well-replicated testing effect works: retrieval is inherently a generative act, not a passive one.

#### 80. [FLASHCARD] Distinction — Germane vs. Extraneous Cognitive Load** | Q: How do germane and extraneous cognitive load relate to GLT's prescription for generative activity? *(Line 901)*

> [!flashcard] Distinction — Germane vs. Extraneous Cognitive Load** | Q: How do germane and extraneous cognitive load relate to GLT's prescription for generative activity?
> A: Extraneous load = effort wasted on processing irrelevant elements of the learning environment (bad design, split attention, redundancy). Germane load = effort directly invested in schema construction and automation. GLT promotes increasing germane load through generative strategies. CLT advises eliminating extraneous load to preserve working memory capacity for germane processing. The two frameworks complement when properly distinguished: both aim to maximize the proportion of available cognitive resources devoted to actual schema building.

#### 81. [FLASHCARD] Concept — ICAP Hierarchy and Constructive Activities** | Q: What distinguishes Constructive from Active activities in Chi's ICAP framework, and why does this distinction matter for GLT? *(Line 904)*

> [!flashcard] Concept — ICAP Hierarchy and Constructive Activities** | Q: What distinguishes Constructive from Active activities in Chi's ICAP framework, and why does this distinction matter for GLT?
> A: Active activities (highlighting, copying, underlining) engage the learner behaviorally but do not produce outputs that go beyond the presented information — they manipulate surface structure without genuinely transforming it. Constructive activities (summarizing, self-explaining, concept mapping, elaborating) require producing outputs that *add to* the presented information — generating inferences, connections, organizations, and elaborations not present in the source. This distinction matters for GLT because it identifies that the critical threshold for generative processing is the production of learner-generated content that exceeds the information encountered, not mere behavioral engagement with the material.
