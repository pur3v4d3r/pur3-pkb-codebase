#### 2. [DEFINITION] Intrinsic Motivation *(Line 268)*

> [!definition] Intrinsic Motivation
> The tendency to engage in activities for their inherent interest and enjoyment, without requiring separable external outcomes as justification. Intrinsically motivated behavior is characterized by curiosity, spontaneous engagement, persistence in the absence of surveillance, and positive affect. Critically, intrinsic motivation is NOT the same as enjoying the outcomes of an activity — a student who studies hard to receive praise is not intrinsically motivated, even if they enjoy the praise. What distinguishes intrinsic motivation is that the activity itself is the end, not merely a means to another end.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs *(Line 273)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs
> On SDT's account, basic psychological needs are innate psychological nutriments whose ongoing satisfaction is required for psychological health, growth, and the maintenance of intrinsic motivation and well-internalized extrinsic motivation. They are described as "basic" in the technical sense that their satisfaction (not merely their expression or pursuit) is hypothesized to be universally beneficial and their thwarting universally harmful — not as a matter of preference or culture but as a matter of psychological functioning. The theory identifies three: **autonomy**, **competence**, and **relatedness**.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Autonomy (in SDT) *(Line 278)*

> [!definition] Autonomy (in SDT)
> The need to experience one's actions as self-endorsed — to feel that one is acting from one's own values and choices rather than under external pressure or internal compulsion. Autonomy in SDT does NOT mean independence, self-sufficiency, or the absence of reliance on others. A student can be fully autonomous while following a teacher's structured assignment, if they understand and genuinely endorse the reasons for that assignment. Conversely, a student choosing their own learning path under intense social pressure to succeed is not autonomous. Autonomy is about the locus of perceived causality — whether the source of behavior feels internal or external.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Competence (in SDT) *(Line 281)*

> [!definition] Competence (in SDT)
> The need to experience oneself as effective in interaction with the environment — to feel capable, to master challenges, and to bring about desired outcomes. Competence in SDT is not an objective measure of ability but a psychological experience of effectance. This experience is sustained when challenges are optimally calibrated (sufficiently challenging to require effort, but achievable with effort), when feedback is informational and positive, and when progress is visible. Environments that are either too easy (no growth) or too difficult (repeated failure without scaffolding) undermine the competence need.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Relatedness (in SDT) *(Line 284)*

> [!definition] Relatedness (in SDT)
> The need to feel securely connected to others, to experience care and belonging, and to feel that one matters to the people around them. Relatedness in SDT does not require close friendship or intimacy — it can be satisfied by a classroom culture where students feel respected and valued by their teacher and peers. The critical functional requirement is felt security: that one's standing in the relational environment is not contingent on performance.

#### 7. [KEY-CLAIM] The Mutual Amplification Hypothesis *(Line 287)*

> [!key-claim] The Mutual Amplification Hypothesis
> The three needs are not independent silos. Satisfaction of one need tends to amplify the motivational yield of the others, while thwarting of one degrades the others' functioning. A student who feels autonomously engaged in a task is more able to experience competence-relevant feedback as informational rather than threatening. A student who feels related — cared for and secure — is better positioned to take the risks that competence development requires. Treating the needs as additive components misses the dynamic interdependence that field research increasingly confirms.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] The Regulatory Spectrum (OIT) *(Line 294)*

> [!definition] The Regulatory Spectrum (OIT)
> OIT proposes six regulatory styles arranged from least to most autonomous:
> 
> 1. **Amotivation** — absence of intentional regulation; the person does not act or acts without intention
> 2. **External Regulation** — behavior controlled by external rewards and punishments; fully controlled regulation
> 3. **Introjected Regulation** — regulation by internalized pressures: ego-involvement, guilt, shame, contingent self-worth; still controlled, but the controlling force is now internal
> 4. **Identified Regulation** — the person consciously values the goal and acts from that valuation; autonomous, though not yet integrated with broader self
> 5. **Integrated Regulation** — the identified goal has been integrated with the person's core values and identity; fully autonomous and extrinsic
> 6. **Intrinsic Motivation** — behavior performed for its inherent interest; fully autonomous and inherently rewarding

#### 10. [DEFINITION] Autonomy-Supportive Environment *(Line 313)*

> [!definition] Autonomy-Supportive Environment
> An environment that takes the perspective of the learner, acknowledges and works with the learner's feelings and perspective (even when those feelings involve resistance or disinterest), provides meaningful rationale for requirements, uses informational rather than controlling language, and minimizes external pressure while nurturing internal motivational resources. Autonomy support is NOT the absence of structure, expectations, or guidance. It is the way structure and guidance are delivered — with acknowledgment of the learner's perspective and genuine respect for their agency.

#### 11. [DEFINITION] Controlling Environment *(Line 316)*

> [!definition] Controlling Environment
> An environment that pressures learners toward specific outcomes through rewards, punishments, surveillance, deadlines presented as sources of pressure, praise that is evaluative rather than informational, conditional regard (where teacher warmth is contingent on performance), and language that communicates "you must," "you should," or "I will reward/punish you for." Controlling environments may produce compliance, but the compliance comes at the cost of autonomous motivation, deeper processing, and wellbeing.

#### 12. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Autonomy-Structure Conflation: The Most Costly Error in SDT's Educational Uptake *(Line 319)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Autonomy-Structure Conflation: The Most Costly Error in SDT's Educational Uptake
> The single most common implementation failure when educators learn about SDT is the equation of autonomy support with reduced structure, loose expectations, or student-directed free choice. This conflation is not only theoretically wrong — it is practically harmful. SDT's research strongly supports the importance of **structure** (clear expectations, scaffolded challenges, organized support) as a distinct and necessary dimension of need-supportive teaching. The theoretical claim is not that structure undermines autonomy, but that *how* structure is delivered — with or without acknowledgment of the learner's perspective and choice — determines whether it satisfies or thwarts the autonomy need. A highly structured, demanding learning environment can be deeply autonomy-supportive if the learner understands and endorses the reasons for the demands. A loosely structured, permissive environment can still thwart autonomy if the student feels directionless, incompetent, or relationally disconnected.

#### 15. [EVIDENCE] Deci et al. (1999) Meta-Analysis on Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation *(Line 345)*

> [!evidence] Deci et al. (1999) Meta-Analysis on Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation
> This landmark meta-analysis examined 128 studies and found that tangible, expected, and contingent rewards significantly undermine intrinsic motivation (d ≈ −0.68 for engagement, −0.61 for intrinsic motivation measures), while verbal rewards under certain conditions can enhance intrinsic motivation when delivered as genuine positive informational feedback about competence rather than as evaluation or control. The conditions under which reward effects are positive, negative, or neutral depend almost entirely on the functional significance of the reward: whether it communicates information about competence (informational function) or communicates external control (controlling function).

#### 16. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Asymmetry of Need Satisfaction and Need Thwarting *(Line 350)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Asymmetry of Need Satisfaction and Need Thwarting
> The evidence, taken carefully, points toward an asymmetry that SDT's original formulations underemphasized: need thwarting produces stronger and more persistent negative outcomes than need satisfaction produces positive outcomes. Studies using need frustration scales alongside need satisfaction scales — a methodological innovation introduced by Bartholomew, Ntoumanis, and colleagues in sport contexts and extended to education by Vansteenkiste and Soenens — consistently find that need frustration is a stronger and more unique predictor of ill-being, controlled motivation, and amotivation than need satisfaction is of well-being and autonomous motivation. This asymmetry parallels findings from the positive psychology literature on the greater weight of negative affect relative to positive affect in wellbeing outcomes, and has important implications for prioritization: preventing thwarting may matter more than maximizing satisfaction.

#### 17. [EVIDENCE] Jang (2008) Experimental Study on Rationale and Internalization *(Line 355)*

> [!evidence] Jang (2008) Experimental Study on Rationale and Internalization
> In a carefully designed experiment, Jang provided high school students with an uninteresting grammar activity under one of three conditions: no rationale, a controlling rationale ("you need to do this because it's in the curriculum"), or an autonomy-supportive rationale (acknowledging the activity's boring nature while providing genuine, informative reasons for its value). Students in the autonomy-supportive rationale condition showed significantly higher identified regulation, persistence, and learning performance than both other conditions — demonstrating that autonomous regulation of extrinsic tasks is achievable and that instructional delivery is the critical moderator.

#### 18. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Positive Feedback Paradox *(Line 360)*

> [!tension-identified] The Positive Feedback Paradox
> CET's predictions about feedback create a genuine tension that the empirical literature has not fully resolved. The theory predicts that positive feedback is motivationally enhancing when perceived as informational, but research on ego-involvement (Nicholls, 1984) and performance goal orientation (Dweck) suggests that some students — particularly those with fragile, contingent self-esteem — process positive feedback as ego-relevant rather than task-relevant, regardless of how it is delivered. The question of whether delivery can consistently override a student's pre-existing ego-involvement mode, or whether certain learners are so entrenched in ego-involvement that autonomy-supportive feedback simply doesn't reach them, is not resolved. Effective SDT applications need to engage with individual differences in ego-involvement rather than treating feedback effects as uniformly determined by delivery style.

#### 19. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What the Evidence Suggests About Relatedness: A More Nuanced Reading *(Line 365)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Evidence Suggests About Relatedness: A More Nuanced Reading
> The evidence suggests that relatedness functions primarily as an enabling condition rather than a direct driver of specific learning behaviors. When felt security is absent — when students experience conditional regard (teacher warmth contingent on performance), when they feel socially excluded, or when peer relationships are competitive and comparison-based — the autonomy and competence needs become harder to satisfy because the learner's attentional and motivational resources are partially consumed by social threat monitoring. In high-relatedness classrooms, not only is motivation higher on average, but the *variance* in motivation is lower: fewer students are operating from fear, ego-defense, or social anxiety. The implication is that relatedness is most important as a risk-mitigation variable — its absence is more damaging than its presence is enhancing.

#### 23. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Hidden Cost of Surveillance in Learning *(Line 392)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Hidden Cost of Surveillance in Learning
> One of the most practically significant mechanisms in SDT is the effect of surveillance on PLOC and learning quality. Studies by Deci and colleagues show that even passive surveillance — simply being observed — shifts PLOC toward external, reduces intrinsic motivation, and degrades performance on subsequent free-choice activities. This has a disturbing implication for digitally-mediated learning environments that track every mouse click, keystroke, and time-on-task: the monitoring infrastructure itself may be costing some of the motivational quality it is designed to measure. This is not an argument against all monitoring, but a call for careful distinction between monitoring that communicates control (you are being watched, you must perform) and monitoring that communicates support (data is available to help you learn more effectively). The functional significance of monitoring — whether learners interpret it as surveillance or as support — is what determines its motivational impact.

#### 24. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Optimal Challenge: SDT's Competence Mechanism and the Biology of Learning *(Line 401)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Optimal Challenge: SDT's Competence Mechanism and the Biology of Learning
> The SDT competence mechanism parallels what neuroscience describes as dopaminergic reward prediction error signaling. When a learner successfully handles a challenge that was at the edge of their capability, the brain registers a positive prediction error — the outcome was better than expected — and releases dopamine, strengthening the neural pathways associated with the behavior and creating a motivational pull toward similar challenges. Below-optimal challenge produces no prediction error signal (outcomes match or fall below expectations), while above-optimal challenge produces negative prediction error (repeated failure). SDT's behavioral claim that intrinsic motivation is maximized at optimal challenge is thus not merely a psychological observation — it reflects underlying neural architecture. This connection suggests that autonomy-supportive environments may be most effective precisely because they protect the learner's access to optimal challenge: when learners have some agency over task selection, they tend to self-regulate toward the challenge frontier, while controlled environments often impose challenges that are either too uniform or optimized for the average rather than the individual.

#### 25. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Competence Feedback and the Mastery-Performance Distinction *(Line 406)*

> [!analytical-insight] Competence Feedback and the Mastery-Performance Distinction
> A subtle but important consequence of the CET competence mechanism is that it predicts a systematic interaction between regulatory style and feedback type. Learners operating from controlled regulation tend to process all feedback through a performance frame — they want to know how they compare to others or how they are being evaluated. These learners are particularly susceptible to the controlling function of feedback, because their PLOC is already external and performance-oriented. Learners operating from autonomous regulation process feedback through a mastery frame — they want to know what it tells them about what to do next. For these learners, even evaluative feedback is more likely to be processed informationally. This interaction has a practical implication that is rarely acknowledged in SDT-informed instructional design: autonomy-supportive feedback is most powerful when combined with efforts to shift learners from performance to mastery framing — which requires, in turn, addressing the competence and autonomy needs together, not separately.

#### 26. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Relatedness as Risk Architecture *(Line 413)*

> [!analytical-insight] Relatedness as Risk Architecture
> Learning requires risk: asking a question that might reveal ignorance, attempting a difficult problem that might result in failure, offering an original idea that might be criticized, admitting confusion in a context where others may judge you for it. The willingness to take these risks is not a fixed personality trait — it is a dynamic function of the learner's felt security in the relational environment. When relatedness is well-satisfied, the learner's psychological resources are available for risk-taking because failure does not threaten their standing in the community. When relatedness is thwarted — through conditional regard, social comparison, exclusion, or indifference — the learner's resources are partially consumed by social threat monitoring, and the risk budget for intellectual engagement shrinks accordingly. This is why the "psychological safety" construct in organizational research (Edmondson, 1999) maps so closely onto SDT's relatedness need: psychological safety is essentially the organizational-context version of relatedness satisfaction.

#### 27. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Universality Claim and Cultural Moderation *(Line 418)*

> [!tension-identified] The Universality Claim and Cultural Moderation
> SDT asserts that the three basic needs are universal — that their satisfaction is beneficial and their thwarting harmful across all human beings regardless of cultural background. This universality claim has generated substantial debate, particularly in cross-cultural psychology. Research in East Asian educational contexts has sometimes found weaker (though rarely reversed) effects of autonomy support on motivation, and evidence from collectivist cultures suggests that relatedness may, in some contexts, function as a more powerful regulatory mechanism than autonomy — meaning that social belonging and conformity to in-group norms can partially substitute for autonomous regulation in maintaining academic engagement. SDT's proponents respond that the universality claim is not that need satisfaction always looks the same across cultures, but that the functional relationships hold universally: autonomy satisfaction always predicts better outcomes than autonomy thwarting, even if the behaviors that constitute "autonomy support" vary across cultural contexts. The tension is not fully resolved, and practitioners working in diverse cultural contexts should treat the universality claim with appropriate nuance rather than as a license to export Western autonomy-support frameworks without adaptation.

#### 28. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Paradox of Scaffolding Without Autonomy Support *(Line 427)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Paradox of Scaffolding Without Autonomy Support
> A particularly important implication of the needs' interaction involves the limits of scaffolding. Scaffolding — carefully structured support that reduces task complexity to the learner's current level and gradually withdraws — is a powerful pedagogical tool for satisfying the competence need. But in controlling instructional climates, scaffolding can paradoxically undermine motivation by communicating low confidence in the learner's capabilities, shifting PLOC toward the external (the structure is doing it, not me), and producing competence attributions ("I succeeded because the task was easy") rather than efficacy attributions ("I succeeded because I can do this"). The scaffold itself, if not delivered in an autonomy-supportive manner, can become a source of controlled regulation. This suggests that the motivational value of scaffolding depends critically on how it is framed: as "I'm giving you this support because you can't do this yet" versus "I'm giving you this support so you can experience success at the frontier of your current ability."

#### 31. [BEST-PRACTICE] Prioritize Need-Thwarting Prevention Over Need-Satisfaction Maximization *(Line 451)*

> [!best-practice] Prioritize Need-Thwarting Prevention Over Need-Satisfaction Maximization
> Given the evidence asymmetry between need thwarting and need satisfaction, the most impactful instructional decisions are those that *eliminate* active frustration of needs, not merely those that *add* need-satisfying elements. An autonomy-supportive feedback system sitting atop a classroom culture of conditional regard, social comparison, and surveillance is likely to produce minimal motivational yield. Audit for thwarting before adding satisfaction.

#### 32. [BEST-PRACTICE] Design for Optimal Challenge at the Individual Level, Not the Average *(Line 454)*

> [!best-practice] Design for Optimal Challenge at the Individual Level, Not the Average
> The competence mechanism requires individualized challenge calibration. Uniform assessments, single-path curricula, and whole-class pacing undermine competence experience for both above-average and below-average learners simultaneously. The practical implication is not that every student needs a unique curriculum, but that enough variability and choice must be built into the learning environment to allow students to operate near their own challenge frontier.

#### 33. [BEST-PRACTICE] Deliver Structure Autonomy-Supportively *(Line 457)*

> [!best-practice] Deliver Structure Autonomy-Supportively
> The most actionable single change available to most instructors is the delivery mode of structure, not the amount of structure. The same requirement, the same deadline, the same standard can be delivered in ways that acknowledge the learner's perspective (autonomy-supportive structure) or that simply impose the requirement (controlling structure). The critical elements: acknowledging that the requirement may feel burdensome, providing genuine rationale, and communicating genuine interest in the learner's perspective on it.

#### 34. [WARNING] Misconception: Rewards Always Undermine Motivation *(Line 462)*

> [!warning] Misconception: Rewards Always Undermine Motivation
> The popular appropriation of SDT's evidence on rewards frequently overgeneralizes to "rewards are bad." The actual claim is more nuanced: rewards undermine intrinsic motivation when they shift PLOC toward external, communicate control, and signal that the task is instrumental rather than valuable in itself. Unexpected rewards, verbal praise delivered informationally, and task-noncontingent rewards (given for participation rather than performance) do not carry these properties and do not show consistent demotivating effects. The practical guidance is not to eliminate rewards but to examine their functional significance: what does this reward communicate to the learner about who controls their engagement with this material?

#### 35. [WARNING] Misconception: Autonomy Support Means Student Choice *(Line 465)*

> [!warning] Misconception: Autonomy Support Means Student Choice
> As noted in Phase II, autonomy support is not the same as giving students choices about what to do. Autonomous regulation can be achieved in highly structured, teacher-directed environments if the learner genuinely understands and endorses the reasons for the direction. Conversely, environments offering extensive choice can still produce controlled regulation if choices are presented in ways that highlight performance consequences or social comparison. The locus of causality — not the structural source of the task — is what matters.

#### 36. [IMPORTANT] Limitation: The Causal Identification Problem *(Line 472)*

> [!important] Limitation: The Causal Identification Problem
> Most field evidence for SDT in education is correlational or quasi-experimental. Because autonomy, competence, and relatedness satisfaction tend to co-occur in good teaching relationships, and because good teaching tends to also involve better content, clearer explanations, and higher student ability — all confounded with need satisfaction — causal attribution is difficult. The experimental evidence (manipulating individual need satisfaction while holding others constant) is cleaner but more artificial. The practical guidance from SDT is well-grounded, but practitioners should maintain appropriate epistemic humility about which specific need is driving which specific outcome in any particular setting.

#### 37. [IMPORTANT] Limitation: Long-Term Effects and Ecological Validity *(Line 475)*

> [!important] Limitation: Long-Term Effects and Ecological Validity
> The majority of SDT intervention studies in education are short-term — weeks to a semester, rarely longer. Whether the motivational benefits of sustained autonomy-supportive environments compound over years of schooling, or whether effects plateau, is less well-documented. Longitudinal work by Vansteenkiste and colleagues offers encouraging evidence of cumulative effects, but this literature is younger and more methodologically varied.

#### 38. [IMPORTANT] Limitation: Cultural and Individual Boundary Conditions *(Line 478)*

> [!important] Limitation: Cultural and Individual Boundary Conditions
> As flagged in Phase IV, the universality claim requires cultural sensitivity in application. Additionally, SDT research has devoted less attention to individual differences in need strength and need prominence. Some learners may show higher sensitivity to competence thwarting than to autonomy thwarting, or vice versa — clinical populations, learners with anxiety disorders, and learners with histories of academic trauma may respond to need-support interventions quite differently than the population averages suggest.

#### 41. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Integrated Gateway Model: Autonomy as the Motivational Gatekeeper *(Line 510)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Integrated Gateway Model: Autonomy as the Motivational Gatekeeper
> Bringing the three need mechanisms together, I propose that the relationship among the needs is not symmetric but hierarchical in a specific functional sense: *autonomy satisfaction acts as a motivational gateway that determines whether competence and relatedness experiences can be fully processed as need-satisfying*. When autonomy is thwarted and PLOC is external, the learner processes competence feedback through a performance frame (ego-threat rather than information) and processes relational experiences through a social monitoring frame (status management rather than security). This is why controlling environments produce such broad motivational damage even when they provide rich competence scaffolding and warm teacher relationships: the scaffolding becomes a reminder of dependence, and the warmth becomes conditional regard in perception. Conversely, when autonomy is genuinely supported, competence and relatedness experiences become fully available as motivational fuel. The practical implication is that autonomy-support is not one of three equally important needs to satisfy — it is the prerequisite condition under which satisfying the other two needs becomes motivationally possible. Structure, scaffolding, and relational warmth are necessary but not sufficient; they must be delivered in an autonomy-affirming manner to achieve their full motivational yield.

#### 42. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] Need Satisfaction as Dynamic Equilibrium, Not Cumulative Score *(Line 519)*

> [!original-synthesis] Need Satisfaction as Dynamic Equilibrium, Not Cumulative Score
> A second synthetic insight: it is tempting to treat need satisfaction as a kind of psychological bank account — make enough deposits (autonomy-supportive moments, competence-building experiences, warm relational interactions) and the account balance grows, supporting motivation long-term. The evidence suggests a different model. Need satisfaction is more like a dynamic equilibrium than a cumulative score: it must be continuously maintained, because the effects of thwarting are rapid and can quickly override a history of satisfaction. A classroom that has been reliably autonomy-supportive for months can generate acute thwarting damage within a single high-stakes, controlling assessment event. The practical implication is that need support is not something to achieve and sustain in a background sense — it is an ongoing dynamic that requires attention in each instructional interaction, especially during the high-stakes moments that are most tempting to deliver in controlling rather than autonomy-supportive ways.

The interaction is theoretically important: a learner with a strong growth mindset may be better able to preserve autonomous competence-seeking even in mildly controlling environments, while a learner with a fixed mindset may be especially vulnerable to competence thwarting because failure confirms a fixed-ability narrative. Understanding this interaction requires integrating both frameworks.

SRL frameworks (Zimmerman, Pintrich) describe the cognitive-metacognitive processes learners deploy to manage their learning; SDT describes the motivational conditions under which those processes are deployed with full engagement versus defensiveness or disengagement. The connection is that autonomous regulation is essentially the motivational precondition for self-regulation: learners can only engage in genuine self-monitoring, self-evaluation, and adaptive strategy deployment when they are motivated by mastery rather than performance management. SRL without SDT-compatible environments produces self-regulation in service of controlled goals.
> 
> - **[[Psychological-Safety-in-Learning-Environments|Psychological Safety in Learning Environments]]** — Edmondson's psychological safety construct (developed in organizational contexts) maps directly onto SDT's relatedness mechanism and risk architecture analysis. Both frameworks converge on the same functional claim: when felt relational safety is absent, risk-taking (intellectual, creative, experimental) is suppressed. Cross-referencing these two literatures strengthens both: SDT provides the needs-based theoretical mechanism for why psychological safety matters, while the organizational literature provides implementation-level evidence about how team leaders and teachers can create safety through specific behaviors (listening, non-judgment, welcoming error as information).
> 
> - **[[Expectancy-Value-Theory|Expectancy-Value Theory]]** — Wigfield and Eccles' expectancy-value theory addresses motivation through learners' expectations of success and subjective task value. SDT and EVT are complementary in identifying why task value matters: in SDT terms, perceiving a task as valuable is the cognitive marker of identified regulation — the student has cognitively connected the task to their own goals and values. SDT goes further by identifying the *environmental conditions* that promote this value integration (autonomy support, rationale provision), while EVT goes further in specifying the content of subjective value (utility, interest, attainment, cost). Together they provide a fuller picture of academic motivation than either alone.
> 
> - **[[Gamification-in-Learning|Gamification in Learning]]** — Gamification is a theoretically interesting application domain for SDT because game mechanics can easily thwart or satisfy the three needs depending on implementation. Points, leaderboards, and badges are classic reward structures that CET predicts will undermine intrinsic motivation when they shift PLOC toward external — and the evidence from gamification research is consistent with this: competition-based leaderboards tend to undermine motivation for below-average performers and produce performance-goal orientation for all. Game mechanics that satisfy the competence need (optimal challenge, immediate feedback, mastery-based progression) and the autonomy need (choice, meaningful decisions, narrative agency) are more consistent with SDT's predictions about long-term engagement. This tension between popular gamification practice and SDT principles is a productive site for both research and design innovation.

#### 45. [DEFINITION] Amotivation *(Line 561)*

> [!definition] Amotivation
> The absence of intentional regulation — the state in which a person either does not engage in a behavior at all, or engages without any intention behind the action. In learning contexts, amotivation is typically the result of sustained need thwarting: the learner has given up any sense that engagement will satisfy their needs. Distinguished from disinterest (which can coexist with intrinsic motivation for other topics) by its global quality and its association with helplessness, passivity, and disengagement from academic life broadly.

#### 46. [DEFINITION] Autonomous Regulation *(Line 564)*

> [!definition] Autonomous Regulation
> The umbrella category in OIT encompassing identified regulation, integrated regulation, and intrinsic motivation — all forms of motivation in which the learner experiences the source of their engagement as arising from their own values and choices. Autonomous regulation is associated with deeper processing, greater persistence, higher wellbeing, and more positive academic emotions. The key functional marker is an internal perceived locus of causality.

#### 47. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET) *(Line 567)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)
> SDT's sub-theory addressing the effects of external events (rewards, feedback, deadlines, surveillance) on intrinsic motivation. CET proposes that any external event carries both an informational aspect (conveying something about competence) and a controlling aspect (conveying something about who is determining behavior). The relative salience of these two aspects determines whether the external event enhances (informational dominant) or undermines (controlling dominant) intrinsic motivation.

#### 48. [DEFINITION] Conditional Regard *(Line 570)*

> [!definition] Conditional Regard
> Teacher or parent warmth that is contingent on student performance or compliance — love and approval are available when the student succeeds but withheld when the student fails or resists. A documented form of relatedness thwarting that produces introjected regulation (students internalize the contingency — "I am valuable when I perform") rather than identified or integrated regulation. Associated with high anxiety, fragile self-esteem, and vulnerability to collapse of motivation under failure conditions.

#### 49. [DEFINITION] Controlled Regulation *(Line 573)*

> [!definition] Controlled Regulation
> The umbrella category in OIT encompassing external and introjected regulation — all forms of motivation in which the learner experiences the source of their engagement as external pressure or internal compulsion (guilt, shame, ego-threat). Controlled regulation is associated with surface processing, performance orientation, higher anxiety, and reduced wellbeing. The functional marker is an external or impersonal perceived locus of causality.

#### 50. [DEFINITION] Effectance *(Line 576)*

> [!definition] Effectance
> The subjective experience of being effective in interaction with the environment — the psychological state of genuine competence satisfaction. Effectance is not the same as objective performance: a student can perform well without experiencing effectance (if success feels attributable to luck, scaffolding, or low task difficulty) and can experience effectance from failure in the right challenge context (when effort was appropriate and feedback is informational). The SDT competence need is for effectance experience, not for objective achievement.

#### 51. [DEFINITION] Identified Regulation *(Line 579)*

> [!definition] Identified Regulation
> The regulatory style in which the learner consciously values the goal and engages with the activity because they judge it personally important, even if it is not intrinsically interesting. Identified regulation is classified as autonomous because the source of regulation is the learner's own evaluative judgment. A student who studies for a required course because they genuinely see its value for their goals — even if they find the specific material dry — is exhibiting identified regulation.

#### 52. [DEFINITION] Introjected Regulation *(Line 582)*

> [!definition] Introjected Regulation
> The regulatory style in which the learner is driven by internalized pressures: guilt, shame, ego-threat, or the need to maintain contingent self-esteem. Introjected regulation is classified as controlled even though the source of pressure is internal, because the learner is not freely choosing engagement — they are responding to internal compulsions that feel obligatory rather than chosen. Students who study primarily to avoid feeling like a failure are exhibiting introjected regulation.

#### 53. [DEFINITION] Need Thwarting (vs. Need Deprivation) *(Line 585)*

> [!definition] Need Thwarting (vs. Need Deprivation)
> Need thwarting refers to the *active frustration* of a psychological need — instructional behaviors, social conditions, or environmental features that specifically undermine need satisfaction. Distinguished from need deprivation (the passive absence of need satisfaction) by its causal specificity and its stronger association with ill-being, controlled motivation, and amotivation. Thwarting can occur even when overall need satisfaction is moderate, and its effects are not simply the mirror image of satisfaction effects.

#### 54. [DEFINITION] Organismic Integration Theory (OIT) *(Line 588)*

> [!definition] Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)
> The SDT sub-theory addressing the internalization and integration of extrinsic motivation — the process by which externally-imposed regulations can be progressively taken in and made one's own. OIT proposes the regulatory spectrum as a model of internalization progress, and identifies autonomy support, competence scaffolding, and relational security as the conditions that facilitate movement toward more autonomous regulatory styles.

#### 55. [DEFINITION] Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC) *(Line 591)*

> [!definition] Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC)
> The learner's subjective experience of the origin of their behavior — whether they feel that their engagement arises from their own choices and values (internal PLOC) or from external demands and pressures (external PLOC). PLOC is the central mechanism through which autonomy support affects motivational quality: autonomy-supportive environments shift PLOC toward internal, while controlling environments shift it toward external.
