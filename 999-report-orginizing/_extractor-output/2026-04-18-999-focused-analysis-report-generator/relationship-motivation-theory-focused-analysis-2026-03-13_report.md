#### 2. [DEFINITION] Autonomy Need (Relational Context) *(Line 209)*

> [!definition] Autonomy Need (Relational Context)
> Within close relationships, the need for autonomy is satisfied when a person experiences their relationship behavior — how they engage with, respond to, and invest in the partner — as volitional, self-endorsed, and congruent with their values and identity. It is NOT satisfied by independence, distance, or the absence of influence from the partner. A person who deeply values connection and freely chooses to reorganize their life around it is expressing their autonomy; a person who stays in a relationship because they fear what their partner will do if they leave is experiencing autonomy thwarting even if no external force prevents their departure.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Competence Need (Relational Context) *(Line 212)*

> [!definition] Competence Need (Relational Context)
> Within close relationships, the need for competence is satisfied when a person experiences themselves as capable of relating in the ways that matter to them — being an effective emotional support, navigating conflict constructively, expressing care in ways the partner receives, pursuing shared goals successfully. Relational competence need satisfaction differs from achievement-domain competence need satisfaction in that it is inherently co-constructed: one partner's sense of relational competence is directly shaped by the other's responsiveness, feedback, and acknowledgment.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Relatedness Need *(Line 215)*

> [!definition] Relatedness Need
> The need for relatedness is satisfied when a person experiences genuine mutual care, understanding, and belonging with another — feeling that they matter to the other person, that the other person understands who they actually are, and that the connection involves authentic mutual investment. Crucially, relatedness need satisfaction is *qualitative* rather than *quantitative*: it is not reduced by the number of relationships maintained but by the phenomenological quality of connection within any given relationship. Superficial contact with many people can leave the relatedness need unsatisfied; genuine understanding from a single person can fully satisfy it.

#### 5. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Asymmetry of Relatedness: Feeling Known vs. Feeling Liked *(Line 218)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Asymmetry of Relatedness: Feeling Known vs. Feeling Liked
> A non-obvious implication of RMT's definition of relatedness emerges when you trace its components carefully. Feeling understood, validated, and cared for — Reis, Clark, and Holmes's (2004) three components of [[Perceived-Partner-Responsiveness|perceived partner responsiveness]] — is not identical to feeling liked, approved of, or positively evaluated. A partner who consistently agrees with you, never challenges you, and reflects your self-presentation back to you without amendment is making you feel *liked*, but not necessarily *known*. RMT predicts that the relatedness need is more fully satisfied by accurate, responsive understanding (which may include disagreement and honest challenge) than by unvarying positive regard. This has an important implication: relationships oriented around unconditional approval may satisfy a desire for validation without actually satisfying the need for relatedness, producing relationships that feel good superficially but leave a persistent sense of not being truly known.

#### 6. [KEY-CLAIM] The Orthogonality of Motivation and Satisfaction *(Line 227)*

> [!key-claim] The Orthogonality of Motivation and Satisfaction
> A person's position on the autonomous-controlled motivation continuum is not the same as their level of relationship satisfaction. High autonomous motivation and high controlled motivation can both co-occur with high satisfaction — what differs is the well-being cost. Two people can report equally high satisfaction with their respective relationships while one maintains the relationship with autonomous motivation and the other with controlled motivation; the evidence consistently shows that the controlled-motivation person pays a larger well-being cost for equivalent relational investment, and is more vulnerable to identity dissolution when the relationship is threatened or ends.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Need Thwarting *(Line 239)*

> [!definition] Need Thwarting
> Need thwarting refers to partner behaviors that actively undermine the other person's basic psychological needs. Autonomy thwarting includes controlling behavior, psychological pressure, emotional coercion, surveillance, and conditional positive regard (withholding warmth unless the partner behaves as demanded). Competence thwarting includes chronic criticism, deprecation of the partner's abilities, and undermining their efforts in domains that matter to them. Relatedness thwarting includes emotional unavailability, dismissiveness, contempt, and communication patterns that leave the partner feeling fundamentally misunderstood or uncared-for.

#### 9. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Need Thwarting vs. Absence of Support Distinction: Theoretical Clarity vs. Measurement Complexity *(Line 242)*

> [!tension-identified] The Need Thwarting vs. Absence of Support Distinction: Theoretical Clarity vs. Measurement Complexity
> RMT's conceptual distinction between need thwarting and absence of support has strong theoretical grounding and clinical face validity — clinicians immediately recognize that a controlling, emotionally coercive partner is doing something categorically different from simply failing to provide autonomy support. But the measurement challenge is real: self-report instruments that assess need satisfaction and need frustration as separate factors tend to show moderate negative correlation, which is conceptually expected but makes the empirical independence of the two constructs difficult to demonstrate. The most rigorous evidence for their separability comes from studies showing that need thwarting predicts ill-being (psychopathology, somatic symptoms, identity disruption) over and above what need satisfaction alone predicts — a finding replicated across domains but requiring continued methodological attention.

#### 11. [EVIDENCE] Patrick, Knee, Canevello & Lonsbary (2007) — Need Fulfillment as Mediator *(Line 258)*

> [!evidence] Patrick, Knee, Canevello & Lonsbary (2007) — Need Fulfillment as Mediator
> Across two studies, Patrick and colleagues examined whether need fulfillment mediated the relationship between perceived partner responsiveness and both relationship quality and individual well-being. Perceived partner responsiveness — the degree to which one experiences the partner as understanding, validating, and caring — significantly predicted basic need satisfaction, which in turn predicted both relationship satisfaction and personal well-being. Importantly, need satisfaction mediated the responsiveness-well-being link over and above its effect on relationship satisfaction, suggesting that the relational pathway to well-being runs through need satisfaction as a distinct mechanism, not simply through relationship quality as a unitary construct. The competence and autonomy needs contributed to this mediation even within the relational context, not just relatedness alone — an early empirical signal that all three needs operate within the dyad.

#### 12. [EVIDENCE] Knee, Patrick & Lonsbary (2003) — Autonomous Motivation and Resilience *(Line 267)*

> [!evidence] Knee, Patrick & Lonsbary (2003) — Autonomous Motivation and Resilience
> Knee and colleagues examined how autonomous vs. controlled relationship motivation moderated responses to relational challenge. In two studies, participants completed daily diaries across several weeks, reporting on relationship conflicts, attributional responses to partner behavior, and relationship-relevant affect. Autonomously motivated participants showed a strikingly different profile of conflict response: they were more likely to attribute partner negative behavior to situational rather than dispositional causes, more likely to engage in constructive problem-solving following conflict, and less likely to report that conflicts threatened their sense of relational security. Controlled motivation participants showed the opposite pattern — conflicts were more likely to trigger dispositional attributions about the partner, withdrawal and self-protective distancing, and significant threat to relational security. This suggests that autonomous motivation is not simply a consequence of relationship quality but a moderator of how partners process and respond to the inevitable challenges that occur in any long-term relationship.

#### 13. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Direction of Causality Problem *(Line 270)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Direction of Causality Problem
> The primary methodological challenge in this literature is directional: does need satisfaction within a relationship *produce* autonomous motivation for maintaining it, or does autonomous motivation create the conditions for need satisfaction? Both causal paths are theoretically plausible, and most of the evidence is correlational or short-term longitudinal, making definitive causal inference difficult. The daily diary studies provide the strongest temporal evidence, showing that need satisfaction on a given day predicts autonomous motivation *on subsequent days* (supporting the bottom-up pathway), while experimental autonomy support manipulations show that artificially inducing autonomous motivation temporarily improves need satisfaction indicators (supporting the top-down pathway). The most defensible current conclusion is bidirectional causality with a positive feedback loop: autonomous motivation and need satisfaction mutually reinforce each other, which explains both the stability of healthy relationships and the self-amplifying character of relationship deterioration.

#### 17. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Conditional Positive Regard as a Relationship Pathogen with a Loving Face *(Line 298)*

> [!analytical-insight] Conditional Positive Regard as a Relationship Pathogen with a Loving Face
> The clinical and empirical literature on CPR reveals a phenomenon with an important structural feature: its costs accumulate slowly and are often invisible until they are severe. A person who has experienced CPR across multiple formative relationships — parental, romantic, mentoring — may have developed a pattern of self-monitoring and self-censorship so automatic that it is no longer experienced as thwarting; it has become the background assumption of what relationships are. This creates a diagnostic problem: people whose autonomy needs are most chronically thwarted by CPR may be least able to identify the thwarting, because the standard of autonomy-supporting relationship behavior has never been available to them for comparison. The RMT framework is therefore not just a theory of relationship quality but a potentially liberatory conceptual tool — it gives people a vocabulary for recognizing something that has been doing harm without a name.

#### 18. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Autonomy Support Creates Conditions for Genuine Disclosure, Not Just Permission *(Line 309)*

> [!analytical-insight] Autonomy Support Creates Conditions for Genuine Disclosure, Not Just Permission
> The relationship between autonomy support and self-disclosure runs through a mechanism that is not always made explicit in the research. Autonomy support doesn't increase disclosure because it removes external obstacles to sharing — it increases disclosure because it alters the internal calculus of what is safe to reveal. Under conditions of CPR, self-disclosure is risky: sharing a genuine but non-approved aspect of the self may trigger conditional withdrawal of warmth. Under autonomy-supportive conditions, this risk is reduced not because the partner has explicitly promised to accept anything, but because the pattern of responsive understanding has established an empirical track record of genuine care that is not contingent on self-presentation. This means that the deepest benefit of autonomy-supportive relationships — access to the other person's genuine inner life — is itself produced by the accumulation of autonomy-supportive behavior over time. The trust that enables genuine intimacy is not given; it is built through consistently responsive, non-contingent engagement.

#### 19. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Iterated Prisoner's Dilemmas → Relational Mutuality Dynamics *(Line 318)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Iterated Prisoner's Dilemmas → Relational Mutuality Dynamics
> The dynamics of mutual need support bear a striking structural resemblance to the well-studied [[Iterated-Prisoner's-Dilemma|Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma]] in game theory. In repeated interactions between two agents, cooperate-cooperate outcomes are more productive for both than defect-defect outcomes, but each individual has a short-term incentive to defect (take without giving). The stability of cooperation depends critically on reputation effects and the shadow of the future — the expectation that the interaction will continue and that past behavior will inform future responses. The RMT mutuality dynamic is the psychological analog: compassionate goals are equivalent to a cooperative strategy, and the expectation that genuine investment will be reciprocated stabilizes the cooperative equilibrium. What RMT adds to the game-theoretic framing is the motivational interior: partners with compassionate goals are not calculating their cooperation rationally — they are genuinely motivated to benefit the other, which produces cooperative behavior that is *robust to short-term provocation* in a way that purely strategic cooperation is not.

#### 20. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Autonomy-Intimacy Tension: Universal Developmental Challenge or Pathological Pattern? *(Line 325)*

> [!tension-identified] The Autonomy-Intimacy Tension: Universal Developmental Challenge or Pathological Pattern?
> RMT's position on the autonomy-relatedness tension is that genuine integration — simultaneously high autonomy need satisfaction and high relatedness need satisfaction within a single relationship — is achievable and represents the optimal relational configuration. But a meaningful tension in the literature concerns whether this integration is the norm in healthy adult relationships or an aspirational endpoint that many adults, particularly those with insecure attachment histories, rarely achieve. Attachment research suggests that securely attached individuals are better able to use close relationships as secure bases that support rather than threaten self-exploration — consistent with the integration hypothesis. But insecure attachment is not a pathological minority condition; the majority of adults show some degree of insecurity in close relationships. The empirical question is whether need integration is genuinely achievable for people with significant attachment insecurity, or whether attachment history creates a developmental ceiling on the autonomy-relatedness integration that RMT treats as the standard.

#### 21. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Resolution: Autonomy and Relatedness as Mutually Constitutive in Secure Relationships *(Line 328)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Resolution: Autonomy and Relatedness as Mutually Constitutive in Secure Relationships
> The apparent tension between autonomy and relatedness dissolves under close examination in well-functioning relationships, and the resolution reveals something important about the nature of each need. Autonomy need satisfaction does not require that the other person have no influence on you — it requires that your engagement with the relationship be self-endorsed. A person who deeply values honesty, care, and growth can experience influence from an autonomy-supportive partner as *autonomy-consistent* rather than autonomy-threatening, because accepting that influence is an expression of their own values. Similarly, relatedness need satisfaction does not require that you be identical to the partner or that the partner approve of everything you are — it requires that the partner understand and genuinely care for *who you actually are*. When both these conditions obtain, the partner who knows you deeply is the partner with whom you are most free to be yourself — autonomy and relatedness become mutually reinforcing rather than mutually limiting. The integration is not a compromise between two competing demands; it is the emergence of a qualitatively different relational experience in which each need supports the other.

#### 23. [BEST-PRACTICE] Assessing Motivational Quality, Not Just Relational Satisfaction *(Line 342)*

> [!best-practice] Assessing Motivational Quality, Not Just Relational Satisfaction
> For practitioners — therapists, coaches, organizational psychologists, instructional designers concerned with the relational climate of learning environments — the most practically important insight from RMT is to distinguish between the *product* of a relationship (how satisfied participants are) and the *process* (the motivational dynamics producing that satisfaction). High satisfaction with a low-autonomy, high-controlled-motivation relational dynamic is not a good outcome — it is a fragile equilibrium that predicts accumulating well-being costs and vulnerability to dissolution under stress. Assessing the degree to which participants experience their engagement as freely chosen, the degree to which the partner's behavior is experienced as responsive versus controlling, and the degree to which all three basic needs are supported rather than thwarted provides a richer diagnostic picture than satisfaction alone.

#### 24. [BEST-PRACTICE] The Teacher-Student Relationship as a Need-Support Context *(Line 349)*

> [!best-practice] The Teacher-Student Relationship as a Need-Support Context
> RMT suggests that a teacher's relational orientation — whether they primarily support or thwart students' basic needs in their relational behavior — is a substantial predictor of motivational quality in the learning context. A teacher who provides clear structure and high-quality instruction but does so through controlling language, conditional approval, and competence thwarting (chronic criticism, dismissiveness of student questions) creates a motivational climate that undermines the learning outcomes they are trying to produce. Conversely, a teacher who combines high instructional quality with genuine autonomy support, responsive understanding of student perspectives, and explicit support for student competence development creates conditions for self-regulated engagement. The distinction between structure and control — clear expectations communicated through rationale and support vs. clear expectations communicated through pressure and contingent approval — is the pedagogical application of the need support vs. need thwarting distinction at the heart of RMT.

#### 25. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Mentorship and Conditional Positive Regard in Learning Contexts *(Line 352)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Mentorship and Conditional Positive Regard in Learning Contexts
> The CPR mechanism analyzed in Phase IV is not confined to romantic relationships — it is equally operative in mentoring relationships, academic advising, and professional coaching. A mentor whose regard for the mentee is visibly contingent on the mentee's intellectual agreement, methodological conformity, or achievement level is exercising conditional positive regard in an educational context. The effects RMT predicts are consistent with what educational psychologists observe: mentees under CPR-characterized mentorship show greater intellectual dependency, reduced creative risk-taking, more anxiety about evaluation, and less genuine intellectual development than those whose mentors maintain warm regard independent of the mentee's performance on any given dimension. This is one of the most direct bridges between RMT's relational analysis and the PKB's focus on the conditions for effective learning.

#### 26. [WARNING] The Warmth Trap: High-Support, Low-Autonomy Relationships in Educational Contexts *(Line 355)*

> [!warning] The Warmth Trap: High-Support, Low-Autonomy Relationships in Educational Contexts
> A common pattern in educational relationships is what might be called the warmth trap: the teacher or mentor is highly warm, caring, and emotionally present, but their support is primarily *structuring* and *directive* rather than *autonomy-supportive*. They do a great deal for the learner, take charge of decisions, prevent challenges before they are encountered, and provide solutions before the learner has fully engaged with the problem. This pattern satisfies the relatedness need in one dimension (genuine warmth and care are present) while simultaneously thwarting the autonomy and competence needs (the learner does not experience themselves as self-directed or genuinely capable, because the support is too scaffolded to leave room for autonomous mastery). The learner who graduates from this relationship may have deep affection for the teacher and simultaneously find themselves poorly equipped for independent functioning — a direct product of the need profile the relationship has been continuously satisfying.

#### 27. [IMPORTANT] The Measurement Gap: Self-Report Dominance *(Line 360)*

> [!important] The Measurement Gap: Self-Report Dominance
> The vast majority of RMT evidence rests on self-report measures of need satisfaction, motivational quality, and partner behavior. While the within-person, daily diary, and longitudinal designs used in the best RMT studies mitigate some concerns about cross-sectional self-report data, a fundamental limitation remains: people's reports of their own motivational quality and their partner's need-supportive or need-thwarting behavior may be substantially influenced by their current relationship satisfaction, their mood, and their general attributional tendencies. Partner-report and behavioral observation studies are methodologically superior but far rarer. The field would benefit substantially from convergent validation using behavioral coding of actual interaction patterns.

#### 28. [IMPORTANT] Cultural Boundary Conditions of the Autonomy Need *(Line 363)*

> [!important] Cultural Boundary Conditions of the Autonomy Need
> The universality of the autonomy need — particularly its applicability within interdependent, collectively-oriented cultures — remains a point of genuine theoretical and empirical debate. Critics have argued that in cultures where relational duty, role-based obligation, and family loyalty are primary values, the RMT framework's emphasis on autonomous relationship motivation may be culturally parochial. SDT researchers respond that the relevant distinction is not between autonomous motivation and obligation per se, but between *identified and integrated* regulation of duty-based behavior (acting from duty because you genuinely endorse its value) and *introjected* regulation (acting from duty because of shame or guilt). A person who acts from a genuine, value-endorsed sense of relational responsibility is, on SDT's account, acting autonomously even if the content of their action looks different from individualistic self-expression. The empirical cross-cultural evidence partially supports this position, but the measurement of autonomous motivation in obligation-centered cultural contexts requires more cultural sensitivity than most instruments currently provide.

#### 30. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] Relationships as Motivational Ecosystems: The Bidirectional Resource Model *(Line 383)*

> [!original-synthesis] Relationships as Motivational Ecosystems: The Bidirectional Resource Model
> The ecological framing clarifies something the standard dyadic analysis misses. When RMT evidence shows that controlled motivation for relationship maintenance spills over into reduced autonomous functioning in work, personal goals, and health behaviors, it is revealing that a relationship is not a sealed system — it is a motivational context whose effects permeate the total psychological economy of each participant. A need-thwarting relationship does not merely reduce satisfaction within the relationship; it depletes the autonomous motivational resources available for self-directed functioning everywhere else. Conversely, a need-supporting relationship is not merely pleasant; it actively enriches the motivational resources each partner brings to the rest of their lives — producing a resource amplification effect whereby the relationship makes both participants more capable of pursuing their goals, engaging with challenges, and functioning autonomously outside it. This reframes the value of relationship quality from a hedonic register (relationships feel good or bad) to an ecosystemic one (relationships shape the total motivational environment in which both participants operate). The practical implication is consequential: improving the need-supportive quality of a close relationship is not merely a relational intervention — it is a global well-being intervention that affects the person's entire motivational functioning.

#### 31. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] RMT as the Motivational Infrastructure of Attachment Security *(Line 388)*

> [!original-synthesis] RMT as the Motivational Infrastructure of Attachment Security
> A second integrative synthesis concerns the relationship between RMT and [[Attachment-Theory|Attachment Theory]]. Attachment security is typically described as the outcome of a history of responsive caregiving — a child develops secure attachment by experiencing a caregiver who is available, sensitive, and responsive to distress. What this formulation leaves underspecified is *why* secure attachment produces the psychological benefits it does. RMT provides a mechanistic account: secure attachment is functionally equivalent to a history of sustained need support. The securely attached individual has a working model of relationships as contexts in which their autonomy is respected, their competence is affirmed, and their relatedness need is genuinely met — and this working model functions as a motivational scaffold for engaging with new relationships from a position of trust rather than vigilance. The attachment security effect on well-being is, on this account, largely a need satisfaction effect: security means reliable, history-backed expectation of need support. This integration has empirical support in La Guardia et al.'s finding that secure attachment predicts overall need fulfillment across relationships, and it generates the testable prediction that need satisfaction should mediate the relationship between attachment security and well-being outcomes — a prediction that has received initial but not comprehensive empirical attention.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Relationship Motivation Theory (RMT) *(Line 432)*

> [!definition] Relationship Motivation Theory (RMT)
> The seventh mini-theory of Self-Determination Theory, developed by Deci and Ryan, which examines how the basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness operate within close personal relationships and how the need-support or need-thwarting character of a relationship determines both its quality and its effects on participants' broader well-being. RMT does NOT reduce to the claim that good relationships are beneficial; it specifies the motivational mechanisms that distinguish beneficial from harmful relationships.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs (BPN) *(Line 435)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs (BPN)
> In SDT, the three universal psychological necessities — autonomy, competence, and relatedness — whose satisfaction produces psychological vitality and well-being and whose thwarting produces ill-being. Universal means cross-cultural, developmental, and domain-general, not merely widely preferred. The universality claim is the empirically contested aspect of BPN theory; the evidence for cross-cultural replication is substantial but not uncontested, particularly for the autonomy need in high-collectivism contexts.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Autonomous Relationship Motivation *(Line 438)*

> [!definition] Autonomous Relationship Motivation
> The motivational quality in which a person maintains a close relationship because they genuinely value it, because participation expresses their identity, or because the relationship is intrinsically rewarding. Distinguished from high commitment, which can co-occur with controlled motivation. Autonomous motivation predicts resilience in the face of relational challenge, positive responses to partner behavior, and lower psychological cost for equivalent relational investment.

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Controlled Relationship Motivation *(Line 441)*

> [!definition] Controlled Relationship Motivation
> The motivational quality in which a person maintains a close relationship due to external pressure, introjected guilt or obligation, fear of aloneness, or excessive sunk cost reasoning. Controlled motivation is NOT equivalent to dislike of the relationship — a person can be highly satisfied with a relationship while maintaining it for controlled reasons. The psychological cost of controlled motivation accrues through resource depletion and reduced autonomous functioning in other domains.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Need Support *(Line 444)*

> [!definition] Need Support
> Partner behavior that actively creates conditions for the other person's basic psychological needs to be satisfied. Autonomy support involves perspective-taking, acknowledging feelings, providing meaningful choice, and minimizing controlling language. Competence support involves providing optimal challenge, positive feedback, and scaffolding that enables genuine mastery. Relatedness support involves genuine attention, responsiveness, and investment in understanding the other person. Need support is the active form; its absence (neutrality) differs from need thwarting.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Need Thwarting *(Line 447)*

> [!definition] Need Thwarting
> Partner behavior that actively undermines the other person's basic psychological needs. Distinct from merely failing to provide need support — it involves positive actions (controlling demands, conditional approval, dismissiveness, criticism) that directly suppress autonomy, competence, or relatedness need satisfaction. Need thwarting is empirically separable from need support absence and predicts worse outcomes (particularly ill-being and psychopathology) over and above what need support alone predicts.

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Conditional Positive Regard (CPR) *(Line 450)*

> [!definition] Conditional Positive Regard (CPR)
> The relational pattern in which warmth, approval, and belonging are made contingent on the partner's compliance with desired behavior or self-presentation. CPR is the quintessential autonomy-thwarting mechanism in RMT; its long-term effect is the development of contingent self-esteem — a form of self-regard that fluctuates with performance on internalized conditions of worth, producing instability, anxiety, and self-monitoring at the expense of autonomous self-determination.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Perceived Partner Responsiveness (PPR) *(Line 453)*

> [!definition] Perceived Partner Responsiveness (PPR)
> Reis, Clark, and Holmes's (2004) construct capturing the phenomenological quality of connection within a close relationship: the experience of feeling understood (the partner accurately perceives who you are), validated (the partner affirms the legitimacy of your experience), and cared for (the partner is genuinely invested in your well-being). PPR is empirically the strongest relational predictor of relatedness need satisfaction and serves as the proximate mechanism through which need-supportive partner behavior translates into felt connection.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Contingent Self-Esteem *(Line 456)*

> [!definition] Contingent Self-Esteem
> A form of self-regard that fluctuates with the degree to which one is meeting internalized conditions of worth derived from conditional positive regard in important relationships. Contingent self-esteem is inherently unstable, fragile, and anxiety-producing — requiring continuous monitoring of performance on the relevant conditions. Distinguished from unconditional self-worth, which is not dependent on any particular performance or approval and is associated with more stable, autonomous functioning.

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Mutuality *(Line 459)*

> [!definition] Mutuality
> The bidirectional quality of need support within a close relationship — both partners simultaneously functioning as sources of support for the other's basic needs. RMT and Canevello and Crocker's research on compassionate vs. self-image goals suggest that dyadic mutuality (both partners oriented toward genuinely supporting the other) produces outcomes substantially better than additive models predict — suggesting that mutual need support is not simply twice the need support of one-directional support, but that the alignment of both partners' motivational orientations creates a qualitatively different relational ecology.

#### 44. [DEFINITION] Autonomy-Relatedness Integration *(Line 462)*

> [!definition] Autonomy-Relatedness Integration
> The relational state in which high autonomy need satisfaction and high relatedness need satisfaction are achieved simultaneously within a single close relationship — the partner knows you deeply and you feel fully self-directed in the relationship. RMT's theoretical contribution is to specify the conditions under which this integration is achieved: non-contingent genuine care, autonomy-supportive partner behavior, and a history of responsive understanding that makes disclosure safe without making it obligatory.

#### 45. [DEFINITION] Organismic Integration Theory (OIT) *(Line 465)*

> [!definition] Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)
> SDT's mini-theory explaining how extrinsic motivations are internalized across a continuum from external regulation to integration. When applied to relationship motivation, OIT's internalization continuum maps onto the autonomous-controlled motivation distinction: fully autonomous relationship motivation represents integrated regulation (the value of the relationship has been fully incorporated into the person's identity), while controlled relationship motivation represents introjected or externally regulated engagement.

> [!methodology-and-sources] Research Grounding for This Report
> This report draws primarily on the Self-Determination Theory literature on close relationships and basic psychological needs, including foundational SDT work by Deci and Ryan and the applied relationship research program developed through La Guardia, Knee, Patrick, Impett, Canevello, and their respective collaborators.
