#### 2. [DEFINITION] Organismic Metatheory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2017) *(Line 269)*

> [!definition] Organismic Metatheory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2017)
> The philosophical framework underlying SDT, which posits that human beings are inherently active organisms with an innate propensity toward psychological growth, integration, and the elaboration of a unified self. Unlike mechanistic models that treat behavior as the product of external forces acting on a passive organism, the organismic perspective treats the human organism as a naturally proactive agent whose developmental trajectory depends on the degree to which its basic psychological needs are satisfied by the social environment.
> 
> **Boundary:** The organismic metatheory is not a claim about biological determinism — it does not specify genetic programs for particular behaviors. Rather, it describes a general developmental directionality (toward integration and growth) that can be supported or derailed by environmental conditions.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This metatheoretical commitment is what makes SDT fundamentally different from reinforcement-based approaches to motivation. It is the ground from which all of SDT's specific predictions arise.
> 
> **See also:** [[Humanistic-Psychology]], [[Carl-Rogers]], [[Organismic-Valuing-Process]], [[Jean-Piaget]]

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Autonomy (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000) *(Line 282)*

> [!definition] Autonomy (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000)
> The experience of volition and self-endorsement in one's actions — the sense that one's behavior emanates from the self and is congruent with one's integrated values and interests. Autonomy in SDT does NOT mean independence, self-reliance, or freedom from influence. It means that one's actions are experienced as volitional rather than coerced, as self-endorsed rather than externally controlled.
> 
> **Boundary:** The distinction between autonomy-as-volition (SDT's meaning) and autonomy-as-independence (the colloquial meaning) is load-bearing for the entire theory. Confusing the two produces serious misunderstandings — particularly in cross-cultural contexts where cultures that value collectivism are wrongly assumed to de-emphasize autonomy.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Autonomy means "doing what you want" or "being free from constraints." In SDT, a person can autonomously choose to follow strict rules if they endorse those rules as congruent with their values.
> 
> **See also:** [[Autonomy-Need]], [[Perceived-Internal-Locus-of-Causality]], [[Autonomy-Support]], [[Self-Determination]]

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Competence (Deci & Ryan, 1985; White, 1959) *(Line 291)*

> [!definition] Competence (Deci & Ryan, 1985; White, 1959)
> The experience of [[Effectance]] and mastery — the sense that one can effectively interact with one's environment, produce desired outcomes, and develop new capacities. Competence need satisfaction arises from engaging optimally challenging tasks and receiving structure that supports skill development.
> 
> **Boundary:** Competence in SDT is not equivalent to objective ability or performance. It refers to the subjective experience of effectiveness. An objectively capable person whose environment provides no optimal challenge or positive feedback may have frustrated competence needs despite high ability.
> 
> **See also:** [[Competence-Need]], [[Perceived-Competence]], [[Self-Efficacy]], [[Mastery-Experience]]

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Baumeister & Leary, 1995) *(Line 298)*

> [!definition] Relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Baumeister & Leary, 1995)
> The experience of meaningful connection with others — the sense of belonging, of being cared for, and of caring for others. Relatedness involves both feeling connected and providing connection; it encompasses [[Mutuality]] rather than mere proximity.
> 
> **Boundary:** Relatedness does not require constant social interaction. It can be satisfied by meaningful relationships with a small number of people. The need is for quality of connection, not quantity.
> 
> **See also:** [[Relatedness-Need]], [[Perceived-Partner-Responsiveness]], [[Relationship-Motivation-Theory]], [[Attachment-Theory]]

#### 19. [DEFINITION] Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000, 2017) *(Line 534)*

> [!definition] Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000, 2017)
> A macro-theory of human motivation, personality development, and well-being proposing that human beings possess inherent growth tendencies whose actualization depends on the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence, and relatedness — by the social environment. SDT is organized as six interrelated mini-theories sharing a common organismic metatheoretical foundation.
> 
> **Boundary:** SDT is a theory of motivational quality and need satisfaction, not a general theory of performance, personality, or cognition. It addresses why people are motivated, not how they perform once motivated.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The encompassing framework whose architecture this entire report describes and evaluates.
> 
> **See also:** [[SDT]], [[Edward-Deci]], [[Richard-Ryan]], [[Basic-Psychological-Needs-Theory]], [[Organismic-Meta-Theory]]

#### 20. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs (Deci & Ryan, 2000) *(Line 543)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs (Deci & Ryan, 2000)
> Innate, organismic requirements for autonomy (volition and self-endorsement), competence (effectance and mastery), and relatedness (meaningful connection with others), whose satisfaction is essential for psychological growth, integrity, and well-being and whose frustration produces predictable patterns of ill-being — regardless of whether the individual consciously values or recognizes them.
> 
> **Boundary:** "Basic" means essential for optimal functioning, not that these needs are the only psychological needs. SDT does not deny the existence of other desires and preferences, but claims that only these three function as universal nutriments.
> 
> **Research Status:** The universality claim is supported by evidence from 100+ countries, though the [[WEIRD-Sample-Bias]] in the evidence base is acknowledged.
> 
> **See also:** [[Basic-Psychological-Needs]], [[Autonomy-Need]], [[Competence-Need]], [[Relatedness-Need]], [[Need-Satisfaction]]

#### 21. [DEFINITION] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008) *(Line 552)*

> [!definition] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008)
> Motivation experienced with a sense of volition, choice, and self-endorsement. Encompasses intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, and integrated regulation. Autonomous motivation is associated with deeper learning, greater persistence, enhanced well-being, and more adaptive cognitive and behavioral strategies compared to controlled motivation.
> 
> **Boundary:** Autonomous motivation does not require that behavior be performed for fun or pleasure (that would be intrinsic motivation specifically). It requires only that the behavior is experienced as volitional and self-endorsed — consistent with the person's values and integrated self-structure.
> 
> **See also:** [[Autonomous-Motivation]], [[Intrinsic-Motivation]], [[Identified-Regulation]], [[Integrated-Regulation]], [[Autonomous-vs.-Controlled-Motivation]]

#### 22. [DEFINITION] Controlled Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008) *(Line 559)*

> [!definition] Controlled Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2008)
> Motivation experienced under pressure — either from external contingencies (rewards, punishments, deadlines) or internal pressures (guilt, anxiety, [[Contingent-Self-Worth]], ego involvement). Encompasses external regulation and introjected regulation. Controlled motivation produces compliance and short-term performance but undermines deep learning, persistence, and well-being.
> 
> **Boundary:** Controlled motivation is not "bad" in all circumstances — it can produce necessary behavior in the short term. The problem is that controlled motivation is fragile, produces poorer-quality engagement, and erodes with time unless internalization occurs.
> 
> **See also:** [[Controlled-Motivation]], [[External-Regulation]], [[Introjected-Regulation]], [[Ego-Involvement]], [[Conditional-Regard]]

#### 23. [DEFINITION] Internalization (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000) *(Line 566)*

> [!definition] Internalization (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000)
> The process through which externally regulated behaviors, values, or practices are progressively transformed from externally imposed demands into self-endorsed regulations integrated into the person's value system and identity. Internalization is the mechanism by which extrinsic motivation becomes increasingly autonomous along the [[Internalization-Continuum|regulatory continuum]].
> 
> **Boundary:** Internalization is not mere compliance or behavioral conformity. It involves a genuine transformation in the psychological basis of regulation — from "I have to" through "I should" through "I value this" to "this is part of who I am."
> 
> **Historical Development:** The concept draws on psychoanalytic and developmental theory but was reconceptualized by SDT as a natural, need-driven developmental process rather than a defensive mechanism.
> 
> **See also:** [[Internalization]], [[Internalization-Continuum]], [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]], [[Organismic-Integration-Tendency]]

#### 24. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Evaluation Theory (Deci, 1975; Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 575)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Evaluation Theory (Deci, 1975; Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The SDT mini-theory explaining how external events (rewards, feedback, surveillance, deadlines) affect intrinsic motivation through two cognitive mediators: perceived locus of causality and perceived competence. The [[Functional-Significance]] of events — whether they are experienced primarily as controlling or informational — determines their motivational impact.
> 
> **Boundary:** CET applies specifically to intrinsic motivation — the motivation to engage in activities for their inherent interest and enjoyment. It does not directly address extrinsic motivation, which is the province of OIT.
> 
> **See also:** [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]], [[Functional-Significance]], [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]], [[Undermining-Effect]], [[Informational-Feedback]]

#### 25. [DEFINITION] Organismic Integration Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000) *(Line 582)*

> [!definition] Organismic Integration Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000)
> The SDT mini-theory specifying the forms of extrinsic motivation arrayed along the internalization continuum from external regulation through introjected, identified, and integrated regulation, and identifying the social-contextual conditions (autonomy support, competence structure, relational warmth) that facilitate or impede movement along this continuum.
> 
> **Boundary:** OIT addresses only extrinsic motivation — behavior performed for instrumental reasons. Intrinsic motivation (behavior performed for inherent interest) is not on the continuum; it is categorically different.
> 
> **See also:** [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]], [[Internalization-Continuum]], [[SDT's-Regulatory-Continuum]], [[External-Regulation]], [[Introjected-Regulation]], [[Identified-Regulation]], [[Integrated-Regulation]]

#### 26. [DEFINITION] Functional Significance (Deci, 1975; Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 589)*

> [!definition] Functional Significance (Deci, 1975; Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The perceived meaning or salience of an external event with respect to its controlling versus informational aspects. The same objective event (e.g., positive performance feedback) can be experienced as primarily controlling (pressuring future behavior) or primarily informational (enhancing perceived competence), depending on contextual cues, delivery style, and the recipient's motivational orientation.
> 
> **Boundary:** Functional significance is a psychological, not an objective, property of events. It depends on the individual's perception, not on the event's objective characteristics.
> 
> **Operational Indicator:** Controlling functional significance is signaled by conditional language, surveillance, evaluation pressure, and external locus of causality. Informational functional significance is signaled by competence-relevant information, absence of performance pressure, and internal locus of causality.
> 
> **See also:** [[Functional-Significance]], [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]], [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]], [[Informational-Feedback]]

#### 27. [DEFINITION] Autonomy Support (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Reeve, 2006) *(Line 598)*

> [!definition] Autonomy Support (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Reeve, 2006)
> A set of interpersonal behaviors and contextual conditions that support the basic psychological need for autonomy by providing meaningful choice, offering genuine rationales, acknowledging the learner's perspective and feelings, minimizing controlling language, and supporting initiative. Autonomy support is the most extensively researched contextual variable in SDT and the most robust predictor of autonomous motivation.
> 
> **Boundary:** Autonomy support is NOT permissiveness, laissez-faire teaching, or the absence of structure. It is a specific interpersonal style compatible with — and often enhanced by — clear expectations and organizational structure.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** "Autonomy support means letting students do whatever they want." In SDT, autonomy support is fully compatible with requirements and structure, provided they are accompanied by rationale, perspective-taking, and non-controlling language.
> 
> **See also:** [[Autonomy-Support]], [[Autonomy-Supportive-Environment]], [[Autonomy-Supportive-Teaching-and-Learning-Environments]], [[Controlling-Environment]]

#### 28. [DEFINITION] Need Frustration (Vansteenkiste & Ryan, 2013) *(Line 607)*

> [!definition] Need Frustration (Vansteenkiste & Ryan, 2013)
> The active thwarting of basic psychological needs — the experience of coercion and pressure (autonomy frustration), incompetence and failure (competence frustration), or exclusion and rejection (relatedness frustration). Need frustration is empirically and conceptually distinct from the mere absence of need satisfaction (need deprivation) and uniquely predicts ill-being, psychopathology, and defensive functioning.
> 
> **Boundary:** Need frustration requires active thwarting, not mere absence. A learning environment that provides no choices (need deprivation) differs motivationally from one that actively coerces and pressures (need frustration).
> 
> **Research Status:** The satisfaction–frustration distinction has been validated through factor analysis and demonstrates unique predictive power for negative outcomes beyond what low need satisfaction accounts for.
> 
> **See also:** [[Need-Frustration]], [[Need-Thwarting]], [[Need-Satisfaction]], [[Satisfaction-Frustration-Asymmetry]]

#### 29. [DEFINITION] Goal Contents (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996) *(Line 616)*

> [!definition] Goal Contents (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996)
> The substantive aspirations that people pursue, classified as either intrinsic (personal growth, community contribution, meaningful relationships, health) or extrinsic (wealth, fame, attractive appearance, social power). [[Goal-Contents-Theory]] proposes that intrinsic goal contents are directly linked to basic need satisfaction, while extrinsic goal contents function as need substitutes that fail to deliver genuine well-being even when achieved.
> 
> **Boundary:** Goal content theory addresses *what* people pursue, not *why* they pursue it. A person can pursue an intrinsic goal (e.g., personal growth) for controlled reasons (e.g., to please a parent) — in which case both the content and the regulatory style matter for outcomes.
> 
> **See also:** [[Goal-Contents-Theory]], [[Intrinsic-Goal-Content]], [[Extrinsic-Goal-Content]], [[Aspiration-Index]], [[Tim-Kasser]]

#### 30. [DEFINITION] Causality Orientations (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 623)*

> [!definition] Causality Orientations (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> Relatively stable individual differences in the way people orient to motivational cues in their environments. The three orientations — autonomy orientation (seeking volition and interest), control orientation (attending to rewards and external demands), and impersonal orientation (tendency toward amotivation) — represent dispositional tendencies that moderate the effects of social contexts on motivation and well-being.
> 
> **Boundary:** Causality orientations are dimensions, not types — individuals possess all three orientations to varying degrees. The relative strength of each orientation predicts motivational functioning across contexts.
> 
> **See also:** [[Causality-Orientations-Theory]], [[Autonomy-Orientation]], [[Control-Orientation]], [[Impersonal-Orientation]]

#### 39. [TENSION] Autonomy as Volition vs. Autonomy as Independence *(Line 694)*

> [!tension] Autonomy as Volition vs. Autonomy as Independence
> **Position A (SDT mainstream):** Autonomy means volition — the experience of self-endorsement and willing engagement — and is empirically distinguishable from independence. A person can be autonomously interdependent (e.g., freely choosing to cooperate, defer, or follow a leader).

> **Why it matters:** The resolution of this tension determines whether SDT can genuinely claim universal applicability or remains a culturally embedded framework.

#### 40. [TENSION] Need Satisfaction vs. Need Frustration: Symmetric or Asymmetric? *(Line 705)*

> [!tension] Need Satisfaction vs. Need Frustration: Symmetric or Asymmetric?
> **Position A (Recent formalization — Vansteenkiste & Ryan):** Need satisfaction and need frustration are asymmetric constructs — satisfaction predicts well-being, frustration uniquely predicts ill-being, and the absence of frustration does not equal satisfaction. They constitute distinct pathways with different predictive targets.
> 
> **Position B (Earlier SDT and some critics):** The distinction adds complexity without proportional explanatory value. Need satisfaction operates on a single continuum from frustration through deprivation to satisfaction, and the asymmetry claim is empirically overstated.
> 
> **Current state of evidence:** Factor analytic studies support the two-factor model, and frustration uniquely predicts negative outcomes. The asymmetry is likely real but its practical magnitude varies across studies.
> 
> **Why it matters:** If the asymmetry is genuine, environments must both remove thwarting conditions AND actively provide supportive conditions — a fundamentally different design principle than a single-continuum model implies.
>

#### 61. [PROTOCOL] Autonomy-Supportive Teaching Protocol (SDT-Informed) *(Line 864)*

> [!protocol] Autonomy-Supportive Teaching Protocol (SDT-Informed)
> **Step 1:** Identify required activities and regulations that cannot be optional.
> **Step 2:** For each requirement, formulate a genuine rationale explaining *why* it matters for the learner's growth — not merely compliance reasons.
> **Step 3:** Where possible, offer meaningful choices within constraints — choices about method, sequence, pacing, or focus, even when the content itself is required.
> **Step 4:** Acknowledge the learner's perspective explicitly, especially when making demands: "I understand this may feel tedious, and here is why I believe it matters for what you're trying to achieve."
> **Step 5:** Replace controlling language ("You must," "You should," "You have to") with autonomy-supportive language ("You might consider," "One effective approach is," "This matters because").
> **Step 6:** Support initiative and self-direction — ask learners what questions they have, rather than only telling them what they need to know.
> **Step 7:** Monitor and adjust: observe whether learners are engaging with genuine interest or mere compliance, and adjust practices accordingly.

> **Source:** Phase V — Autonomy-Supportive Teaching
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Distinction

#### 74. [FLASHCARD] Untitled *(Line 987)*

> [!flashcard] Untitled
> **Q:** What are the three causality orientations in SDT's COT?
> **A:** (1) Autonomy orientation — tendency to seek and respond to autonomy cues, regulate autonomously. (2) Control orientation — tendency to attend to rewards, demands, and regulate through external/introjected pressure. (3) Impersonal orientation — tendency toward amotivation, helplessness, and perceived incompetence. These are dimensional, not categorical.
> **Source:** Phase IV — Causality Orientations
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Definition
> **Tags:** SDT, COT, individual-differences

#### 75. [FLASHCARD] Untitled *(Line 995)*

> [!flashcard] Untitled
> **Q:** How does SDT relate to Self-Regulated Learning (SRL)?
> **A:** SDT and SRL are complementary, not competing. SDT explains *why* learners self-regulate (motivational energetics — autonomous motivation drives deeper SRL engagement). SRL models explain *how* they self-regulate (cognitive-metacognitive processes). Autonomous motivation predicts more effective self-regulatory behavior than controlled motivation.
> **Source:** Phase V — Implications for Self-Regulation
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection
> **Tags:** SDT, SRL, integration, metacognition

#### 76. [FLASHCARD] Untitled *(Line 1003)*

> [!flashcard] Untitled
> **Q:** What is the main criticism of SDT's universality claim?
> **A:** Critics argue that the emphasis on autonomy privileges Western individualistic values and may not apply to collectivist cultures. SDT responds that autonomy means volition (not independence) and that volition is valued across cultures. Cross-cultural evidence largely supports universality, though behavioral expressions of need satisfaction vary. The WEIRD sample bias remains a limitation.
> **Source:** Phase V — Limitations
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Application
> **Tags:** SDT, universality, cross-cultural, criticism

#### 77. [FLASHCARD] Untitled *(Line 1011)*

> [!flashcard] Untitled
> **Q:** What is the organismic metatheory underlying SDT?
> **A:** The philosophical position that human beings are inherently active organisms with natural tendencies toward psychological growth, integration, and elaboration of a unified self. Unlike mechanistic models, the organismic perspective treats humans as proactive agents whose development depends on environmental satisfaction of basic psychological needs.
> **Source:** Phase II — The Organismic Metatheoretical Commitment
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Definition
> **Tags:** SDT, metatheory, organismic, philosophy
