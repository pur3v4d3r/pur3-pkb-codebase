#### 3. [DEFINITION] Reading Comprehension (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978; Kintsch, 1998) *(Line 179)*

> [!definition] Reading Comprehension (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978; Kintsch, 1998)
> Reading comprehension is the process of constructing a coherent mental representation of a text's meaning that goes beyond the literal linguistic content. It requires the integration of information extracted from the text with the reader's existing knowledge, motivational resources, and metacognitive capacities. Comprehension is not a passive reception of meaning but an active, constructive process in which the reader continuously builds, updates, and revises a multi-level internal model of what the text is about.
> 
> **Boundary:** Reading comprehension is NOT equivalent to word recognition, accurate oral reading, or the ability to recall surface-level statements. A reader may reproduce sentences verbatim while failing to comprehend the text at the level of a coherent, integrated representation.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This definition establishes that comprehension is a multi-level construction problem, not a single capacity. All subsequent sections address different components and constraints of this construction process.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[language-comprehension]], [[Simple-View-of-Reading]], [[Walter-Kintsch]]

#### 4. [KEY-CLAIM] The Simple View of Reading and Its Insufficiency for Academic Texts *(Line 198)*

> [!key-claim] The Simple View of Reading and Its Insufficiency for Academic Texts
> Gough and Tunmer (1986) proposed that reading comprehension (RC) = Decoding (D) × Language Comprehension (LC). This multiplicative model captures the intuition that both components are necessary: if either approaches zero, overall comprehension collapses. The model has strong empirical support for explaining reading across early developmental stages.
> 
> However, applied to academic text comprehension, the [[Simple-View-of-Reading]] requires substantial elaboration. "Language comprehension" in academic contexts is not a unitary capacity but a complex of vocabulary knowledge, syntactic processing ability, world/domain knowledge, inferential reasoning, and metacognitive monitoring. The Simple View names the vehicle without engineering the engine.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Phonological Awareness *(Line 238)*

> [!definition] Phonological Awareness
> Phonological awareness is the explicit, conscious sensitivity to the sound structure of spoken language, including the ability to identify and manipulate phonemes, syllables, and onset-rime units. It is recognised as the strongest early predictor of reading acquisition and is the primary mechanism targeted in effective early literacy interventions.

#### 9. [DEFINITION] Reading Fluency (Wolf & Katzir-Cohen, 2001; LaBerge & Samuels, 1974) *(Line 253)*

> [!definition] Reading Fluency (Wolf & Katzir-Cohen, 2001; LaBerge & Samuels, 1974)
> Reading fluency is the ability to read text accurately, at an appropriate rate, and with prosodic expression, in a manner that permits comprehension. Fluency is the integrated product of automatised word recognition and efficient phonological processing. It is the mediating variable between the word-level skills (decoding, vocabulary) and the text-level comprehension processes.
> 
> **Boundary:** Fluency is NOT simply reading speed. High reading rate in the absence of comprehension is not fluency but racing. Fluency is defined by the *integration* of accuracy, rate, and comprehension-supportive prosody.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Fluency is the construct that connects the decoding foundation to the comprehension architecture. Its development is the lever by which word-level automaticity liberates cognitive resources for higher-order processing.
> 
> **See also:** [[Reading-Fluency]], [[reading-fluency-automaticity-focused-analysis-2026-03-20]], [[Silent-Reading-Fluency]], [[Verbal-Efficiency-Theory]], [[Fluency.md]]

#### 10. [DEFINITION] Lexical Automaticity *(Line 270)*

> [!definition] Lexical Automaticity
> Lexical automaticity is the ability to retrieve the meaning of a written word immediately upon visual recognition, without deliberate attention allocation. Automatic lexical access is characterised by speed (typically 200–250 ms in skilled readers), reliability (consistent access regardless of context), and obligatoriness (the process cannot be suppressed by effort).
> 
> **Boundary:** Lexical automaticity applies to words in the reader's established mental lexicon. For novel, low-frequency, or domain-specific vocabulary — which academic texts deploy heavily — lexical access is not automatic but effortful, placing a persistent tax on [[Working-Memory-Capacity|working memory]] throughout the reading episode.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Academic texts are characterised by a high proportion of vocabulary items below the threshold of lexical automaticity for most readers, making every sentence a partially effortful decoding problem even for those who read everyday text fluently.
> 
> **See also:** [[Vocabulary-Acquisition-for-Reading-Automaticity]], [[Lexical-Automaticity]], [[Schema-Automation-in-Reading]], [[Fluency-Assessment-and-the-Accuracy-Automaticity-Gap]]

#### 11. [WARNING] The Matthew Effect — Cumulative Advantage and Compounding Disadvantage *(Line 283)*

> [!warning] The Matthew Effect — Cumulative Advantage and Compounding Disadvantage
> The [[Matthew-Effect-in-Reading]] (Stanovich, 1986) captures one of the most consequential dynamics in reading development. Named after the biblical observation that "the rich get richer," the Matthew Effect in reading describes the cumulative process by which strong early readers read more, thereby expanding vocabulary and world knowledge, thereby comprehending better, thereby reading more — while weak early readers avoid reading, thereby failing to expand vocabulary and knowledge, thereby comprehending less well, thereby avoiding reading further.
> 
> The Matthew Effect is particularly vicious in academic contexts because academic text exposure is central to the acquisition of domain knowledge, which is itself central to academic comprehension. The gap between strong and weak academic readers tends to widen, not narrow, across the course of education, unless targeted intervention disrupts the cycle.
> 
> **See also:** [[Matthew-Effect-in-Reading]], [[Matthew-Effect-Interventions-and-the-Closing-of-Early-Gaps]], [[Matthew-Effect]]

#### 12. [EXAMPLE] The Matthew Effect in a University Cohort *(Line 292)*

> [!example] The Matthew Effect in a University Cohort
> Consider two students entering a biology degree programme. Student A has read extensively about science during secondary school — popular science books, science journalism, Wikipedia-level biology — accumulating broad domain vocabulary and conceptual schema. Student B has read minimally, despite equivalent intelligence, arriving with standard curriculum biology exposure only. In the first semester, Student A reads a cell biology textbook with 70% of its vocabulary already in their mental lexicon; Student B encounters 50% unfamiliar vocabulary per page. Student A's comprehension proceeds fluently; Student B's comprehension is effortful, slow, and less complete. Student A learns from reading; Student B reads without learning. By year two, the knowledge gap has widened substantially, and the difference is mistaken for a difference in intelligence or ability.

#### 16. [DEFINITION] Vocabulary Knowledge Depth (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2002) *(Line 316)*

> [!definition] Vocabulary Knowledge Depth (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2002)
> Vocabulary knowledge exists on a spectrum: from no knowledge of a word, through partial and contextual knowledge, to *rich, decontextualised knowledge* — the ability to use a word flexibly and precisely across varied contexts. Reading comprehension research distinguishes vocabulary *breadth* (how many words a reader knows at some level) from vocabulary *depth* (how thoroughly each word is known). Academic comprehension requires deep vocabulary knowledge of discipline-specific terms, not merely recognitional familiarity.
> 
> **Boundary:** Word frequency data from corpora consistently show that 5,000–8,000 word families are sufficient for comprehension of everyday spoken conversation, and 95% coverage of newspaper text requires approximately 5,000–8,000 families. Academic text comprehension requires an additional tier of 2,000–5,000+ specialised academic word families beyond this base.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The vocabulary demands of academic texts are categorically different from everyday vocabulary demands, making general vocabulary acquisition strategies insufficient without a specific academic vocabulary development component.
> 
> **See also:** [[Vocabulary-Acquisition-for-Reading-Automaticity]], [[Lexical-Automaticity]], [[Matthew-Effect-in-Reading]], [[language-comprehension]]

#### 17. [KEY-CLAIM] Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition Cannot Compensate for Academic Vocabulary Deficits *(Line 339)*

> [!key-claim] Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition Cannot Compensate for Academic Vocabulary Deficits
> The standard mechanism for vocabulary acquisition in everyday contexts is *incidental* acquisition through reading and conversation: words are learned implicitly through repeated contextually-supported encounters. Research by Nagy and his colleagues demonstrates that incidental acquisition from reading requires approximately 10–15 encounters with a word before it reaches the depth of knowledge required for reliable comprehension support.
> 
> For Tier 3 domain-specific vocabulary, incidental acquisition through general reading is essentially impossible — these words do not appear outside disciplinary texts. For Tier 2 academic vocabulary, incidental acquisition is possible but slow, requiring sustained academic reading volume that many learners do not accumulate. *Explicit vocabulary instruction* — direct teaching of word meaning, morphological analysis, and contextual usage — is therefore necessary for closing academic vocabulary gaps that incidental exposure cannot bridge.

#### 18. [CLAUDE-INSIGHT] Why Academic Vocabulary Cannot Be Taught in Isolation *(Line 346)*

> [!claude-insight] Why Academic Vocabulary Cannot Be Taught in Isolation
> There is a seductive efficiency in vocabulary instruction as a standalone activity — memorising word lists, flashcard drilling, definitions practice. The research reveals this approach as fundamentally inadequate for academic reading purposes. Vocabulary depth — the rich, flexible, generative word knowledge that supports comprehension — is not acquired from definitions but from *encounters within meaningful contexts*. Words that appear in academic texts carry disciplinary meaning that cannot be captured by dictionary definitions. The phrase "the mechanism underlying this effect" carries different weight in chemistry, cognitive psychology, and legal theory; knowing that "mechanism" means "a system of interconnected parts" does not convey its epistemic role in argument structure.
> 
> The implication is that vocabulary development for academic reading must be *embedded within content learning*: reading academic texts in the domain, with explicitly taught vocabulary support, is simultaneously vocabulary instruction AND reading comprehension instruction AND domain knowledge acquisition. These three processes cannot be efficiently separated.

#### 22. [DEFINITION] Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980) *(Line 374)*

> [!definition] Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980)
> A schema is a cognitive structure — an organised, generic representation of knowledge about a type of object, situation, event, or procedure — stored in long-term memory. Schemas function as interpretive frames that guide the encoding and retrieval of new information by providing default assumptions, expected slots to be filled, and inferences about unstated relationships. During reading, schemas are activated by textual cues and serve as the background against which textual information is interpreted and integrated.
> 
> **Boundary:** Schemas are NOT fixed templates or rigid scripts. Rumelhart (1980) characterised schemas as "data structures for representing the generic concepts stored in memory" — they are probabilistic, prototype-like structures that generate expectations while remaining flexible. A schema can be violated, partially instantiated, or restructured through new experience.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Schema activation is the primary mechanism through which prior knowledge is recruited during reading. The quality and specificity of the schemas a reader brings to an academic text largely determines how much inference generation, situation model integration, and deep comprehension will be achievable.
> 
> **See also:** [[Schema-Theory]], [[Schema-Theory-and-Learning]], [[schema-theory-and-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-06]], [[Prior-Knowledge]], [[Schema-Automation-in-Reading]]

#### 23. [KEY-CLAIM] Prior Knowledge Is the Most Powerful Predictor of New Learning from Academic Text *(Line 389)*

> [!key-claim] Prior Knowledge Is the Most Powerful Predictor of New Learning from Academic Text
> Research across three decades in educational psychology, cognitive science, and reading science consistently identifies prior knowledge as the single strongest predictor of how much a reader will learn from an academic text. This finding has been replicated across domains, text types, learner populations, and methodologies.
> 
> The mechanism is comprehensible from the schema theory account: prior knowledge activates schemas that enable inference generation, fill in ellipses and presuppositions in the text, support coherence monitoring, and integrate new information into rich pre-existing networks. Without prior knowledge, the reader is attempting to build a mental representation from scratch — an extremely effortful, error-prone process that consistently yields fragile, poorly integrated learning.

#### 24. [WARNING] Prior Knowledge as a Comprehension Obstacle: Misconception-Based Interference *(Line 408)*

> [!warning] Prior Knowledge as a Comprehension Obstacle: Misconception-Based Interference
> [[Conceptual-Change-Theory]] research by Vosniadou, Chi, and others demonstrates that readers with strongly entrenched but incorrect prior knowledge often *assimilate* correct new information into their incorrect schemas rather than *accommodating* their schemas to fit the new information. The result is predictable: the reader thinks they have understood the academic text, but their "understanding" is a distorted version that preserves their misconceptions. They have constructed a plausible situation model — but one incompatible with the text's actual content.
> 
> This is particularly problematic in science education (where naive physics conflicts with quantum mechanics and relativity), history education (where simplified national narratives conflict with complex historical accounts), and psychology/social science (where commonsense folk theories conflict with empirical findings). The academic reader who brings confident but incorrect prior knowledge may comprehend *less accurately* than the reader who brings no relevant prior knowledge at all.
> 
> **See also:** [[Conceptual-Change-Theory]], [[Conceptual-Change-Theory-and-Schema-Restructuring]], [[Schema-Accommodation]], [[Assimilation-and-Accommodation]]

#### 25. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Knowledge-Comprehension Interface as a Two-Way Dependency *(Line 419)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Knowledge-Comprehension Interface as a Two-Way Dependency
> Schema theory reveals a crucial bidirectional dependency that is often overlooked in both reading instruction and academic study guidance: comprehension depends on prior knowledge (schemas), AND the acquisition of new knowledge from text depends on successful comprehension. This means that the knowledge gap and the comprehension gap are not sequential problems (first fix knowledge, then comprehend) but simultaneous and mutually reinforcing ones.
> 
> This bidirectionality has important implications for academic reading instruction. It means that isolated comprehension strategy instruction cannot succeed without concurrent knowledge-building, AND that knowledge-building cannot succeed without comprehension support. The inference that follows: effective academic reading development must be *integrated* — simultaneously addressing vocabulary, prior knowledge activation, comprehension strategy deployment, and metacognitive monitoring — rather than treating these as sequential or separable components.
> 
> This integration is precisely what the best empirically-validated reading programs (e.g., Reciprocal Teaching, Content-Area Literacy Instruction, the Text-Based Collaborative Dialogue literature) achieve.

#### 28. [DEFINITION] Inference Generation in Reading (Graesser, Singer & Trabasso, 1994) *(Line 444)*

> [!definition] Inference Generation in Reading (Graesser, Singer & Trabasso, 1994)
> An inference is cognitive content that is activated during reading but is not explicitly stated in the text. Inference generation is the process by which readers go beyond the literal text to construct implicit meaning. Graesser, Singer, and Trabasso's constructionist theory distinguishes between two primary inference classes: *coherence inferences* (generated automatically to maintain local and global coherence of the text) and *elaborative inferences* (generated selectively to enrich the situation model with relevant world knowledge).
> 
> **Boundary:** Not all inferences are equally likely or automatic. Coherence inferences — particularly causal bridging inferences needed to connect adjacent text segments — are generated automatically; elaborative inferences are generation-selective, meaning they are activated only when the reader has the relevant knowledge, when the inference is sufficiently constrained by text cues, and when working memory and attention resources are available. The distinction matters practically because comprehension breakdowns often involve missing coherence inferences rather than absent elaborative inferences.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Academic texts are inference-dense: academic authors routinely presuppose theoretical, methodological, and terminological knowledge that disciplines assume readers possess. Developing academic readers frequently fail not because of decoding problems but because they cannot generate the coherence inferences that academic texts require.
> 
> **See also:** [[Elaborative-Inference]], [[Inferential-Bridging]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Situation-Models-in-Text-Comprehension]], [[Walter-Kintsch]]

#### 29. [EXAMPLE] Bridging Inference Failure in Academic Reading *(Line 471)*

> [!example] Bridging Inference Failure in Academic Reading
> A developing reader encounters: "Critics argued that the methodology confounded self-selection with treatment effects. Subsequent meta-analyses revealed effect sizes substantially below initial estimates." A skilled academic reader immediately generates the implicit bridge: the confounding undermined causal inference, inflating effect sizes, which meta-analysis corrected by aggregating better-controlled studies. A developing reader without the methodological schema for confounding and selection bias reads two facts without a connecting argument — the passage yields a textbase but not a situation model.
> 
> **Teaching implication:** Explicit instruction in the typical inferential structures of academic genres — what kinds of inferences different academic text types routinely require — is as important as vocabulary instruction and schema activation.

#### 30. [CLAUDE-INSIGHT] Academic Inference as a Disciplinary Skill *(Line 478)*

> [!claude-insight] Academic Inference as a Disciplinary Skill
> Research on disciplinary reading practices (Moje, Stockdill, Kim & Kim, 2011; Prior, 2005) reveals something important: inference generation in academic texts is not a single generic skill but a family of *discipline-specific* inference types. A historian reading a primary source activates schemas for source bias, historical context, and evidential standards that yield inferences a literary scholar never generates from the same text. A biologist reading an experimental report generates methodological inferences (What controls were used? What alternative explanations remain?) that a psychologist with the same general academic vocabulary would not generate unless they knew the biological subdiscipline.
> 
> This has a disquieting practical implication: "academic reading skill" as taught in generalist academic skills courses transfers only weakly to disciplinary reading because the schemas that drive inference generation are domain-specific. Deep academic reading in any discipline requires exposure to sufficient disciplinary content to build the inference-generating schemas the discipline employs. Reading skills can be taught; reading schemas must be accumulated through experience.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Monitoring in Reading (Flavell, 1979; Brown, 1987) *(Line 513)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Monitoring in Reading (Flavell, 1979; Brown, 1987)
> Metacognitive monitoring in the context of reading is the ongoing process by which a reader evaluates their own comprehension state — tracking whether they are understanding, identifying specific loci of confusion or failure, and assessing the quality of their developing situation model against standards of coherence and accuracy. It encompasses both *metacognitive knowledge* (knowing that comprehension can fail, knowing what strategies are available to repair it, knowing how reading tasks differ) and *metacognitive regulation* (deploying, adjusting, and evaluating comprehension strategies in practice).
> 
> **Boundary:** Metacognitive monitoring is NOT simply feeling like you understand something — research consistently demonstrates that subjective confidence is poorly calibrated with actual comprehension, particularly in novice readers. "Feeling of knowing" (FOK) is a metacognitive judgement that is famously susceptible to illusions of knowing: readers can feel satisfied with their comprehension while their situation model is fragmentary or distorted. Genuine metacognitive monitoring requires active interrogation of comprehension — asking specific questions and checking understanding against specific criteria.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The gap between illusion of knowing and actual comprehension is the primary long-run obstacle to academic reading development. Readers who don't know they're not understanding don't deploy repair strategies. Metacognitive monitoring training is therefore not optional enrichment — it is the prerequisite for all other comprehension strategies becoming effective.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognition]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring]], [[Fix-Up-Strategy]], [[metacognition-in-learning-focused-analysis-2026-03-20]]

#### 36. [WARNING] The Illusion of Knowing: Why Metacognitive Monitoring Needs Training *(Line 538)*

> [!warning] The Illusion of Knowing: Why Metacognitive Monitoring Needs Training
> Research by Thiede, Griffin, Wiley, and Anderson (2010) demonstrates that most learners show remarkably poor comprehension calibration — they substantially overestimate their understanding of academic texts, particularly after a single reading. This "illusion of knowing" is not simply a motivational failure: it reflects genuine metacognitive limitations in knowing what you don't know.
> 
> Particularly problematic for academic reading is what Bjork (1994) calls "fluency illusion": texts that are quickly processed, stylistically familiar, or consistent with existing beliefs generate a subjective sense of understanding that can be entirely decoupled from actual situation model quality. A conspiracy theory that confirms a reader's existing beliefs will generate high subjective comprehension; a rigorous academic argument that challenges those beliefs will generate low subjective comprehension — even if the academic argument is actually more clearly written.
> 
> **See also:** [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]]

#### 37. [CLAUDE-INSIGHT] Academic Reading as a Self-Regulated Practice, Not a Performance Skill *(Line 571)*

> [!claude-insight] Academic Reading as a Self-Regulated Practice, Not a Performance Skill
> The single most consequential misconception about academic reading I encounter in the literature and in practice is the implicit framing of reading as a *performance* — an activity to be completed, producing a product (information extracted) — rather than as a *cyclical self-regulating practice* in the Zimmerman sense.
> 
> This matters because performance framing produces passive behaviour when comprehension fails: the reader experiences confusion as a signal that the task is too hard, or that they lack ability, rather than as a signal that a specific comprehension repair is needed. Self-regulated practice framing produces active behaviour: confusion is epistemically informative, pointing to a specific gap to be filled.
> 
> The research on growth mindset ([[Growth-Mindset]], Dweck, 2006) and academic buoyancy ([[Academic-Buoyancy]], Martin, 2014) converges on this point: learners who interpret comprehension difficulty as normal, expected, and addressable persist with academic texts and develop; learners who interpret difficulty as evidence of inability disengage. The metacognitive training literature makes the same point from a different direction: metacognitive monitoring only becomes useful when it is coupled with fix-up strategy repertoire AND with the attribution belief that fixing is possible.
> 
> **See also:** [[Self-Regulated-Learning]], [[Academic-Buoyancy]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]], [[Growth-Mindset]]

#### 41. [EXAMPLE] Reciprocal Teaching in an Academic Text Context *(Line 609)*

> [!example] Reciprocal Teaching in an Academic Text Context
> A group of university students reads a challenging economics paper. The "teacher" for that passage asks a prediction: "Based on the section heading *Adverse Selection in Insurance Markets*, what do you expect the argument to claim?" Students generate predictions drawing on prior knowledge schemas. During reading, strategy roles rotate: the questioner asks "What assumption does the author make about information symmetry?" The clarifier notes: "I don't know what 'moral hazard' means in this context." The summariser synthesises: "So the argument is that when buyers know more than sellers, markets can fail — this is what the author means by market failure."
>

#### 42. [CLAUDE-INSIGHT] The Cumulative Advantage Problem in Academic Reading Instruction *(Line 632)*

> [!claude-insight] The Cumulative Advantage Problem in Academic Reading Instruction
> There is a deep tension in academic reading instruction between short-term comprehension support and long-term development. Short-term supports — glossaries, text scaffolding, pre-reading discussions, accessible paraphrases — improve immediate comprehension for developing readers. But they may also reduce the *comprehension effort* that drives vocabulary acquisition, inference generation skill, and schema development. 
> 
> The most effective long-term academic reading development requires what Bjork (1994) calls [[Desirable-Difficulties]]: productive difficulty that requires learners to actively deploy and consolidate comprehension processes, rather than relying on support systems that do cognitive work for them. This creates an instructional dilemma: reducing demand supports access and motivation; maintaining demand drives development. The resolution lies in *calibrated scaffolding* — providing support at the level of immediate demand while systematically withdrawing support as competence develops, requiring learners to internalise the processes being scaffolded.

#### 49. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Vocabulary-Schema-Inference Triad: An Integrated Comprehension Framework *(Line 705)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Vocabulary-Schema-Inference Triad: An Integrated Comprehension Framework
> This report argues for the primacy of three interlocking components in academic reading comprehension — vocabulary knowledge, schema/prior knowledge, and inference generation — that together constitute what can be called the *Vocabulary-Schema-Inference (VSI) Triad*. These three components are not additive but *multiplicative* in their interaction: deficiency in any one of the three cascades into failure across the other two.
> 
> **The cascade logic:**
> - Without sufficient vocabulary, the semantic content of text nodes cannot be activated in the CI model's construction phase, blocking the integration phase from yielding a coherent situation model.
> - Without activated schemas, inferential bridges between explicit text propositions cannot be constructed — the textbase yields a sequence of propositions rather than a causally-structured situation model.
> - Without inference generation (both automatic coherence inferences and strategic elaborative inferences), even adequate vocabulary and schema activation fail to produce situation model integration — the reader has the building materials but not the architectural plan.
> 
> The triad is the theoretical core of the argument that academic reading comprehension cannot be effectively developed through component-isolated instruction. Intervening on vocabulary alone without building schemas leaves inference generation under-resourced; intervening on inference strategies without vocabulary support leaves the construction phase starved for content; building schemas without vocabulary development or inference strategy instruction leaves the integration phase disconnected from strategic regulation.
> 
> **Instructional implication:** Academic reading development programs should be designed with the VSI triad as the explicit target — systematically and concurrently addressing all three components, with metacognitive monitoring providing the regulatory overlay that ensures each component is being effectively deployed during real reading.

#### 51. [DEFINITION] Construction-Integration (CI) Model (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978; Kintsch, 1988) *(Line 736)*

> [!definition] Construction-Integration (CI) Model (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978; Kintsch, 1988)
> A computational and psychological theory of text comprehension that describes comprehension as a two-phase iterative process: a *construction phase* (automatic spreading activation generates a rich but noisy propositional network from each text proposition plus associated long-term memory content) and an *integration phase* (constraint satisfaction suppresses contextually inconsistent activations and strengthens consistent ones, yielding a coherent propositional network). The CI model operates across three representational levels: the surface structure (verbatim wording), the textbase (propositional content), and the situation model (mental representation of the denoted state of affairs).
> 
> **Boundary:** The CI model is a process model of online comprehension, not a developmental model. It describes what happens during reading rather than how comprehension skill develops over time. It is also primarily a single-author, single-text model; comprehension of multi-document, multi-source, or interactive discourse requires extensions of the CI framework.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The CI model is the theoretical engine of this report — it explains how vocabulary, prior knowledge, and inference generation combine in real-time to produce comprehension outcomes, and it predicts where comprehension will fail when any component is insufficient.
> 
> **See also:** [[Walter-Kintsch]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Models-in-Text-Comprehension]]

#### 52. [DEFINITION] Situation Model (Johnson-Laird, 1983; van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 745)*

> [!definition] Situation Model (Johnson-Laird, 1983; van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> The situation model (also called "mental model" in Johnson-Laird's framework, or "discourse model" in some traditions) is the deepest representational level of text comprehension: a mental representation of the state of affairs described by the text rather than of the text itself. Situation models are dynamic, multi-dimensional data structures encoding spatial, temporal, causal, intentional, and entity-identity information about the narrated or described world. A reader who has constructed a situation model can answer questions about unstated implications, draw analogies to other domains, and detect text-world inconsistencies — tasks that textbase-level comprehension cannot support.
> 
> **Boundary:** "Constructing a situation model" is not the same as "understanding a text" — situation models can be inaccurate, incomplete, or schema-distorted. The quality of a situation model depends on the vocabulary, prior knowledge schemas, and inference generation processes the reader brings to the text.
> 
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Situation-Models-in-Text-Comprehension]], [[Rolf-Zwaan]], [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch]]

#### 53. [DEFINITION] Phonological Awareness (Liberman & Shankweiler, 1977; Adams, 1990) *(Line 752)*

> [!definition] Phonological Awareness (Liberman & Shankweiler, 1977; Adams, 1990)
> Phonological awareness is explicit, conscious sensitivity to the sound structure of spoken language — the capacity to attend to and manipulate phonological units including syllables, onset-rime patterns, and individual phonemes. It is the primary cognitive prerequisite for decoding alphabetic written language. Phonological awareness allows a reader to connect grapheme patterns (letters and letter combinations) to phoneme sequences, enabling pronunciation of unfamiliar printed words. The alphabetic principle — that letters systematically represent phonemes — is inaccessible to a reader without phonological awareness.
> 
> **Boundary:** Phonological awareness is a necessary but not sufficient condition for reading fluency. It enables decoding but does not guarantee the lexical automaticity required for fluent reading; nor does it directly contribute to comprehension above the word level.
> 
> **See also:** [[Phonological-Awareness-and-Fluency-Development]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Decoding]], [[Phonological-Loop]]

#### 54. [DEFINITION] Lexical Automaticity (Logan, 1988; LaBerge & Samuels, 1974) *(Line 759)*

> [!definition] Lexical Automaticity (Logan, 1988; LaBerge & Samuels, 1974)
> Lexical automaticity is the state of word recognition in which a familiar written word is identified rapidly, effortlessly, and without attentional resources through direct retrieval from memory rather than through active decoding. Logan's instance theory characterises automatisation as the accumulation of specific memory traces for each word encounter; automaticity is achieved when retrieval from this episodic memory is faster and more reliable than algorithmic decoding. Lexical automaticity frees working memory resources previously consumed by decoding for higher-order comprehension processes — inference generation, coherence monitoring, and situation model construction.
> 
> **Boundary:** Lexical automaticity applies to individual word recognition. It does not guarantee comprehension — a reader may automatically recognise all words in a sentence while failing to integrate them into a coherent textbase or situation model, due to syntactic complexity or missing schemas.
> 
> **See also:** [[Lexical-Automaticity]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Verbal-Efficiency-Theory]], [[Simple-View-of-Reading]]

#### 55. [DEFINITION] Comprehension Monitoring (Brown, 1978, 1987) *(Line 766)*

> [!definition] Comprehension Monitoring (Brown, 1978, 1987)
> Comprehension monitoring is the metacognitive capacity to evaluate the quality of one's own understanding during reading — detecting coherence failures, vocabulary gaps, referential discontinuities, and structural ambiguities as they occur. It is part of the broader metacognitive regulation system: monitoring detects failure; regulation deploys repair. Brown distinguished comprehension monitoring from the "cognitive" reading processes it oversees: it is a metacognitive, executive function rather than a comprehension operation per se.
> 
> **Boundary:** Comprehension monitoring does not automatically lead to comprehension repair. Readers may monitor accurately but lack fix-up strategies; or may have strategies but misapply them. The detection-repair link requires both metacognitive skill and strategic knowledge.
> 
> **See also:** [[Comprehension-Monitoring]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring-Training-Protocols]], [[Fix-Up-Strategy]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Ann-Brown]]

#### 56. [DEFINITION] Schema Accommodation vs. Assimilation (Piaget; Rumelhart & Norman, 1981) *(Line 773)*

> [!definition] Schema Accommodation vs. Assimilation (Piaget; Rumelhart & Norman, 1981)
> Assimilation is the incorporation of new information into an existing schema without changing the schema's structure — the new information is interpreted through the existing frame. Accommodation is the modification of an existing schema, or the creation of a new one, in response to information that cannot be assimilated into current structures without distortion. In academic reading, assimilation is the default process and produces efficient comprehension when schemas are accurate; accommodation is the more cognitively demanding process required when texts challenge or contradict existing schemas. Conceptual change learning — reading academic texts that revise current understanding — requires accommodation.
> 
> **Boundary:** Accommodation does not always occur when it should. Readers with strong prior schemas routinely assimilate incompatible new information into existing structures (the misconception-based interference described in Section 4), producing apparent understanding that is actually distortion.
> 
> **See also:** [[Schema-Theory]], [[Conceptual-Change-Theory]], [[Prior-Knowledge]], [[Assimilation-and-Accommodation]]

#### 57. [DEFINITION] Verbal Efficiency Theory (Perfetti, 1985, 1988) *(Line 780)*

> [!definition] Verbal Efficiency Theory (Perfetti, 1985, 1988)
> The Verbal Efficiency Theory predicts that individual differences in reading comprehension are substantially explained by the efficiency (speed and accuracy) of local word recognition and lexical processing. Readers for whom word recognition consumes excessive working memory resources — due to low automaticity of decoding or lexical access — have fewer cognitive resources available for higher-order comprehension processes. The theory predicts a *processing bottleneck* at the word level for less efficient readers that constrains comprehension quality even when the reader possesses the relevant schemas and vocabulary knowledge for the text's content.
> 
> **Boundary:** Verbal efficiency effects are most pronounced with developing and emerging readers. With skilled adult readers, individual differences in comprehension are better explained by higher-order cognitive variables (inference generation, schema richness, working memory capacity) rather than by word-level efficiency, which is typically high and non-differentiating in adults.
> 
> **See also:** [[Verbal-Efficiency-Theory]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]]

#### 58. [DEFINITION] Tier 2 Academic Vocabulary (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2002) *(Line 787)*

> [!definition] Tier 2 Academic Vocabulary (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2002)
> Tier 2 vocabulary consists of high-frequency, general academic words that appear across disciplines and academic registers but rarely in conversational speech — examples include *analyse, synthesis, mechanism, paradigm, infer, criterion, implicit, hypothesis, juxtapose, ambiguous*. Tier 2 words are the connective tissue of academic argument, carrying logical, rhetorical, and epistemic relationships between ideas. Developing academic readers typically lack both the breadth and depth of Tier 2 vocabulary required for full academic text comprehension, making Tier 2 instruction the highest-priority vocabulary intervention for academic reading development beyond basic literacy.
> 
> **Boundary:** Tier 2 vocabulary is not simply "difficult vocabulary" — it is vocabulary defined by its distribution across academic registers rather than by its complexity. Some Tier 2 words are morphologically simple (*claim, evidence, suggest*); some domain-specific Tier 3 words are more complex. The register and distribution criterion, not lexical complexity, defines the tier.
>

#### 59. [DEFINITION] Self-Explanation Effect (Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher, 1994) *(Line 794)*

> [!definition] Self-Explanation Effect (Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher, 1994)
> The self-explanation effect is the reliable finding that learners who generate explicit, verbal explanations of text content to themselves during or after reading demonstrate substantially better comprehension and retention than learners who re-read the same text or receive equivalent study time without generating explanations. The mechanism involves forced situation model construction: self-explanation requires the reader to identify inferential gaps, generate bridging inferences, and integrate prior knowledge with textual content in ways that passive re-reading does not require. Self-explanation makes comprehension invisible visible.
> 
> **Boundary:** Self-explanation is effortful and time-intensive compared to re-reading. It is most valuable for conceptually challenging texts where comprehension of deep structure matters; its advantages over passive re-reading are smaller for simple factual texts where surface comprehension is sufficient.
> 
> **See also:** [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[self-explanation]], [[Elaborative-Interrogation]], [[Retrieval-Practice]]

#### 64. [TENSION] Tension 1: Comprehension = Understanding vs. Comprehension = Knowing What to Do *(Line 845)*

> [!tension] Tension 1: Comprehension = Understanding vs. Comprehension = Knowing What to Do
> **Position A (mentalist):** Comprehension is the construction of an accurate mental representation — a situation model that corresponds to the text's intended meaning. Success is measured by accuracy of representation. *Strongest advocates:* Kintsch, van Dijk, Johnson-Laird.
> 
> **Position B (pragmatist/functional):** Comprehension is demonstrated through what a reader can DO with a text — solve problems using its content, argue against it, apply it, integrate it with other texts. Representation accuracy is necessary but not sufficient. *Strongest advocates:* Graesser (situation awareness), Perfetti (comprehension quality), Bransford (transfer-appropriate processing).
> 
> **Current state:** The distinction matters for assessment: representational tests (summary, recall, multiple-choice) and transfer tests (application, argumentation) are somewhat independent, suggesting the two accounts capture different aspects of a complex achievement.
> 
> **This report's stance:** Both are required. Situation model construction is necessary for functional comprehension, but not sufficient; effective academic reading requires the additional capacity to deploy situation model content purposefully in disciplinary reasoning.

#### 65. [TENSION] Tension 2: Strategy Instruction vs. Knowledge Building as the Primary Lever *(Line 854)*

> [!tension] Tension 2: Strategy Instruction vs. Knowledge Building as the Primary Lever
> **Position A (strategy-focused):** Academic reading comprehension improves most through explicit instruction in comprehension strategies — text structure awareness, inference generation prompts, self-explanation, reciprocal teaching roles. Skill is teachable independently of content.
> 
> **Position B (knowledge-focused):** Academic reading comprehension improves most through systematic domain knowledge building — rich content curricula, wide academic reading in domains, vocabulary-embedded content learning. Skills without knowledge have nothing to operate on.

> **Current state:** A genuine tension with significant instructional stakes. Willingham (2006), Hirsch (2016), and Wexler (2019) make strong empirical cases for knowledge primacy; strategy instruction advocates can point to substantial intervention effects from explicit strategy training.

#### 83. [PROTOCOL] Protocol 1: Pre-Reading Activation and Goal-Setting (Adapted from SRL Forethought Phase) *(Line 1021)*

> [!protocol] Protocol 1: Pre-Reading Activation and Goal-Setting (Adapted from SRL Forethought Phase)
> **When to use:** Before beginning any challenging academic text — journal articles, textbook chapters, primary literature, academic argument essays.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Preview structure** (2 minutes): Read title, abstract/summary, headings, and conclusion first. Form an initial prediction about the text's central argument.
> 2. **Background knowledge audit** (1 minute): Ask: What do I already know about this topic? What are my existing schemas? Are any of them likely to be challenged? Write 2–3 bullet points.
> 3. **Vocabulary scan** (2 minutes): Skim text for unfamiliar vocabulary, especially Tier 2 academic words and domain-specific Tier 3 terms. Look up essential terms before reading.
> 4. **Goal-setting** (1 minute): Write one specific reading goal ("I want to understand how X explains Y" or "I want to identify the evidence for the core claim"). This goal becomes the anchor for comprehension monitoring.
> 
> **Outcome:** Activated schemas, pre-built vocabulary, set expectations → improved comprehension quality in reading phase.
> **Time:** ~6 minutes total. Returns substantially more than it costs in comprehension quality and retention.

#### 84. [PROTOCOL] Protocol 2: Active Reading with Self-Explanation (Adapted from Reciprocal Teaching + Chi et al.) *(Line 1033)*

> [!protocol] Protocol 2: Active Reading with Self-Explanation (Adapted from Reciprocal Teaching + Chi et al.)
> **When to use:** During dense, conceptually challenging passages in academic texts where surface reading is insufficient.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Read one paragraph or coherent section.**
> 2. **Cover or look away from text.**
> 3. **Self-explain:** State in your own words what the passage argued — what was the claim, what was the evidence, what inferences connect them. Be specific.
> 4. **Gap detection:** Identify anything you couldn't explain clearly. This is a genuine comprehension gap — mark it explicitly.
> 5. **Targeted re-reading:** Return to the text only to address identified gaps — look for the answer to your specific question, not for general re-familiarisation.
> 6. **Inference check:** Articulate the most important inference the passage requires that is NOT stated explicitly.
> 
> **Outcome:** Forces situation model construction; surfaces comprehension gaps the fluency illusion masks; builds the bridging inference habits of skilled readers.

#### 85. [PROTOCOL] Protocol 3: Post-Reading Situation Model Reconstruction *(Line 1046)*

> [!protocol] Protocol 3: Post-Reading Situation Model Reconstruction
> **When to use:** Immediately after completing an academic text (article, chapter, argument).
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Close the text completely.**
> 2. **Reconstruct:** Without notes, write or speak the text's central argument, the primary evidence, the key inferences, and any unresolved questions it raised for you. Target 3–5 minutes.
> 3. **Evaluate against goals:** Compare your reconstruction to the comprehension goal you set in the pre-reading protocol. Did you achieve it? What's missing?
> 4. **Gap list:** Write a bullet list of specific things you couldn't reconstruct — these are the comprehension gaps.
> 5. **Selective re-reading:** Address only the listed gaps; do not re-read the full text.
> 6. **Integrate:** Write one or two sentences connecting the text's argument to two or three things you already knew — this is the schema integration step that converts the situation model into long-term schematic knowledge.
> 
> **Outcome:** Spaced retrieval of situation model content; schema integration; metacognitive calibration.

> **A:** The Vocabulary-Schema-Inference Triad claims that the three primary components of academic reading comprehension interact *multiplicatively* rather than additively: deficiency in any one cascades into failure across the others. This implies that isolated vocabulary instruction, isolated schema-building, or isolated inference strategy instruction each produces narrow, non-transferring gains — effective academic reading instruction must address all three components concurrently and with metacognitive monitoring as the regulatory overlay.

#### 95. [FLASHCARD] Untitled *(Line 1126)*

> [!flashcard] Untitled
> **Q:** Name and describe the five dimensions of Zwaan's Event-Indexing Model.
> **A:** Readers continuously track: (1) *Time* — temporal ordering and sequence of events; (2) *Space* — spatial location and relationship of entities; (3) *Causality* — cause-effect and reason-consequence chains; (4) *Intentionality* — goals, motivations, and actions of agents; (5) *Entity identity* — stable tracking of who/what across mentions and pronoun chains. Academic texts make differential demands on these dimensions depending on genre and discipline.
> **Source:** § Section 5 / Inference Generation and Situation Model

### 1. Textbase-vs-Situation-Model-Distinction

> [!definition] Textbase-vs-Situation-Model-Distinction
> The textbase represents propositional content explicitly stated in the text; the situation model represents the integrated mental representation of the domain or scenario the text describes, incorporating prior knowledge and inference. Deep learning requires situation model construction; mere recall can be supported by textbase alone.

*Source: Line 192 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*

### 2. Principle-of-cognitive-resource-allocation-in-reading

> [!definition] Principle-of-cognitive-resource-allocation-in-reading
> Reading comprehension is a resource-limited process. Cognitive resources devoted to lower-level word processing (decoding, lexical access) are unavailable for higher-level integration. Skilled comprehension requires that word-level processing reach a level of automaticity sufficient to free working memory for situation model construction. Instruction that strengthens fluency is therefore preparation for comprehension, not an alternative to it.

*Source: Line 266 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*

### 3. Academic-Vocabulary-Tier-2-Bottleneck

> [!definition] Academic-Vocabulary-Tier-2-Bottleneck
> Tier 2 general academic vocabulary — words like "synthesise," "mechanism," "criterion," "implicit," "inference" — constitutes the primary vocabulary obstacle in academic reading for developing readers. These words are common in academic text but rare in everyday speech, meaning learners lack the incidental exposure through conversation that builds Tier 1 knowledge. Explicit instruction in high-frequency academic vocabulary (e.g., from the Academic Word List) has strong evidence support for improving academic reading comprehension.

*Source: Line 333 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*

### 4. Prior-Knowledge-as-Comprehension-Multiplier

> [!definition] Prior-Knowledge-as-Comprehension-Multiplier
> Prior domain knowledge does not merely add to comprehension; it multiplies it. Readers with relevant background knowledge make more accurate inferences, build richer situation models, notice more coherence breaks, and remember more from the same text than readers without background knowledge. The practical implication is that "building background knowledge" is not preparatory to learning from academic text — it IS learning from academic text, making it the highest-leverage long-term investment a learner can make.

*Source: Line 396 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*

### 5. CI-Model-Two-Phase-Comprehension

> [!definition] CI-Model-Two-Phase-Comprehension
> The Construction-Integration model explains how comprehension produces both coherent understanding AND creative misreading: in the construction phase, an irrelevant but strongly associated prior-knowledge schema may be activated; if that schema is sufficiently activated and prior knowledge is insufficient to suppress it in the integration phase, the faulty schema can persist into the situation model, producing plausible-but-incorrect comprehension. This is the mechanism behind expert readers' active monitoring practice: they continuously check whether their activated schemas are being confirmed or disconfirmed by incoming text.

*Source: Line 461 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*

### 6. Self-Explanation-as-Comprehension-Tool

> [!definition] Self-Explanation-as-Comprehension-Tool
> Self-explanation is not a supplementary study technique — it is a fundamental comprehension mechanism that makes visible what silent, passive reading leaves invisible: comprehension gaps that the fluency illusion masks. A simple operational test: attempt to explain in your own words, without the text, what an academic paragraph argued. If the explanation is impossible, comprehension has not occurred.

*Source: Line 559 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*

### 7. Vocabulary-Instruction-Principles

> [!definition] Vocabulary-Instruction-Principles
> The most effective academic vocabulary instruction combines: (1) rich instruction for a targeted set of high-value academic words; (2) incidental encounters through wide reading; (3) morphological awareness training for generative acquisition; (4) semantic network building that connects new words to prior vocabulary; and (5) production opportunities that require using words in academically authentic contexts.

*Source: Line 624 in `developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08.md`*
