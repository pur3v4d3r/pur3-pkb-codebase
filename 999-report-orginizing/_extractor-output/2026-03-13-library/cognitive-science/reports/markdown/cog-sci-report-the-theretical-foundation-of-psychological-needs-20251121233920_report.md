#### 2. [ABSTRACT] Untitled *(Line 29)*

> [!abstract] Untitled
> The three basic psychological needs identified by [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]] are **autonomy**, **competence**, and **relatedness**. These needs represent fundamental psychological nutrients that, when satisfied, foster intrinsic motivation, psychological growth, integration, and flourishing. Conversely, when these needs are frustrated or thwarted, individuals experience controlled regulation, diminished motivation, psychological distress, and eventual disengagement from activities. Developed through decades of empirical research by [[Edward-Deci|Edward Deci]] and [[Richard-Ryan|Richard Ryan]], these needs have been validated across cultures, contexts, and developmental stages, establishing them as universal requirements for optimal human functioning. Understanding these needs provides the foundation for designing external support systems—including [[Personal-Knowledge-Base|Personal Knowledge Base]] architectures—that work with rather than against human psychological architecture.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Untitled *(Line 34)*

> [!definition] Untitled
> **Basic Psychological Needs** are innate, universal requirements for psychological growth, integrity, and well-being that specify the necessary conditions for intrinsic motivation and healthy development. Within [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]], three fundamental needs have been identified: **autonomy** (experiencing volition and self-direction), **competence** (experiencing effectiveness and mastery), and **relatedness** (experiencing meaningful connection with others). These needs are not learned desires or culturally conditioned values but rather evolved psychological nutrients that all humans require to thrive.

#### 4. [CORE-PRINCIPLE] Untitled *(Line 41)*

> [!core-principle] Untitled
> The satisfaction of basic psychological needs provides the **motivational energy** for psychological growth and integration. Unlike physical needs that create deficits when unmet, psychological needs function as facilitating conditions—their satisfaction enables flourishing, while their frustration creates vulnerability to maladjustment and controlled forms of motivation. This asymmetry has profound implications for designing systems meant to sustain long-term engagement, as merely removing obstacles is insufficient; active support for need satisfaction is required.

#### 5. [KEY-CLAIM] Untitled *(Line 46)*

> [!key-claim] Untitled
> **Autonomy** concerns the need to experience one's actions as emanating from the self rather than being imposed by external forces. It is satisfied when individuals feel they are acting with volition, authenticity, and psychological freedom, and it is frustrated when they experience pressure, coercion, or being controlled to behave in particular ways against their authentic inclinations.

#### 6. [EXAMPLE] Untitled *(Line 55)*

> [!example] Untitled
> In workplace studies, autonomy emerged as crucial for middle managers in Norwegian universities, aligning with principles of the Nordic work-life model, where internal motivation and support from immediate leadership enhanced middle managers' performance. Conversely, in educational research, psychologically controlling teachers who adopt covert behaviors such as inducing guilt, limiting verbal expression, and exhibiting disapproval can hinder satisfaction of psychological needs, encouraging an external locus of motivation rather than intrinsic motivation, producing a learning context characterized by control, obligation, and coercion. These contrasting examples illustrate how autonomy support creates thriving environments while autonomy frustration generates psychological distress and disengagement.

#### 7. [KEY-CLAIM] Untitled *(Line 60)*

> [!key-claim] Untitled
> **Competence** concerns the need to experience oneself as effective in producing desired outcomes and mastering optimal challenges. It is satisfied through successful interaction with the environment, appropriate challenge, and effectance-relevant feedback that confirms one's capabilities. Frustration of this need occurs when individuals experience failure, helplessness, or when feedback emphasizes inadequacy rather than providing information about how to improve.

#### 8. [EVIDENCE] Untitled *(Line 69)*

> [!evidence] Untitled
> The connection between competence need satisfaction and sustained engagement has been demonstrated across educational contexts. In a study of 1,549 students from 10 secondary schools in Singapore, structural equation modeling showed that students' psychological needs were positively related to autonomous motivation, which in turn led to higher enjoyment, value, and lower pressure, though interestingly, while autonomy and relatedness contributed negatively to controlled motivation, competence positively predicted controlled motivation. This nuanced finding suggests that competence experiences can drive both autonomous and controlled forms of motivation depending on the broader motivational climate—competence feedback in a controlling context may increase pressure to perform rather than fostering intrinsic interest.

#### 9. [KEY-CLAIM] Untitled *(Line 74)*

> [!key-claim] Untitled
> **Relatedness** concerns the need to feel connected to others, to care and be cared for, and to experience a sense of belonging to a community or relationship. It is satisfied through warm, supportive social interactions and meaningful relationships, and frustrated through social exclusion, alienation, coldness, or rejection. While autonomy and competence are more directly tied to intrinsic motivation, relatedness provides the secure base and emotional context that enables individuals to pursue growth-oriented activities.

And just as chronic malnutrition stunts physical development, chronic need frustration impairs psychological growth and integration.

#### 11. [PRINCIPLE-POINT] Untitled *(Line 88)*

> [!principle-point] Untitled
> Contemporary [[Basic Psychological Need Theory]] distinguishes between need satisfaction and need frustration as separate constructs rather than opposite ends of a single continuum. Need satisfaction facilitates well-being, autonomous motivation, and growth, while need frustration actively produces ill-being, controlled motivation, and maladaptive outcomes. This asymmetry means that preventing need frustration is necessary but insufficient—active support for need satisfaction is required for flourishing.

#### 12. [KEY-CLAIM] Untitled *(Line 99)*

While the specific manifestations and relative salience of needs may vary across cultural contexts, empirical research has demonstrated that need satisfaction predicts well-being and autonomous motivation across diverse populations, challenging cultural relativist accounts that suggest fundamental differences in human motivational architecture.
