#### 2. [STEEL-MAN] The Strongest Version of the Reading-Primary Vocabulary Hypothesis *(Line 292)*

> [!steel-man] The Strongest Version of the Reading-Primary Vocabulary Hypothesis
> The case for extensive reading as the foundation of vocabulary acquisition is genuinely compelling and rests on multiple converging lines of evidence. First, the *scale argument*: competent adult readers of English possess working vocabularies of 15,000–50,000+ word families, and no formal instruction program could plausibly cover more than a few hundred words per year — meaning the vast majority of vocabulary must be acquired through other channels, of which reading is the most linguistically rich. Second, the *contextualisation argument*: words learned in rich textual context are learned with semantic depth, collocational networks, and register information that decontextualised word-list instruction cannot provide. When you encounter *laconic* in a narrative where a character responds to a long question with a single-word answer, you learn not just the definition but the texture, the register, the appropriate deployment conditions. Third, the *motivation and reading interaction argument*: avid readers report intrinsic motivation for reading, and the sustained attention this motivation enables may produce superior encoding to the effortful but often joyless work of deliberate vocabulary study. Fourth, the *empirical correlation argument*: reading volume is among the strongest predictors of vocabulary size across virtually all ages and languages studied, a relationship robust enough that some researchers (Stanovich, 2000; Nation, 2001) treat it as one of the best-documented findings in educational psychology.
> 
> **Why this version matters**: If the reading-primary hypothesis is correct in its strongest form, it recommends a clear pedagogy — prioritise extensive, motivated reading over decontextualised word drills and vocabulary tests. It also offers a parsimonious account of vocabulary development that requires no separate theory of word learning: the mechanism is comprehensible input, repeated in quantity, and vocabulary is the natural residue.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition *(Line 309)*

> [!definition] Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition
> The process by which word meanings are acquired as a by-product of comprehension-focused reading or listening, without deliberate intention to learn the words encountered. Distinguished from *intentional vocabulary learning*, which involves explicit study (word cards, memorization, dictionary use) directed at acquiring specific words. The received view holds that incidental acquisition from reading is the dominant vocabulary learning mechanism for mature readers; the dialectical challenge concerns the efficiency and scope of this mechanism.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance**: The entire debate about reading-based vocabulary learning turns on whether incidental acquisition is efficient enough, at sufficient scale, to account for the vocabulary sizes proficient readers achieve — and whether it builds the kind of word knowledge that supports skilled reading and writing.
> 
> **Operational Indicators**: A word acquired incidentally would be recognisable in context but might not be retrievable on demand; incidental acquisition is typically assessed by pre/post-reading vocabulary tests comparing control and reading-exposure conditions.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage**: The distinction was systematised in SLA research by Schmidt (1990) through the *noticing hypothesis*; Nation (2001) provides the most comprehensive treatment of incidental vs. intentional learning in his framework.
> 
> **Cross-References**: [[Incidental-Learning]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Elaborative-Inference]]

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Text Coverage and the Vocabulary Threshold Hypothesis *(Line 320)*

> [!definition] Text Coverage and the Vocabulary Threshold Hypothesis
> The proportion of running words in a text that a reader already knows. Paul Nation's research established that readers require approximately 95–98% vocabulary coverage to comprehend a text without external support (dictionary, glossing), and approximately 98%+ for comfortable independent reading that enables incidental acquisition of new words. This figure has been empirically supported by multiple studies (Laufer, 1989; Nation, 2001; Hu & Nation, 2000). For typical English fiction, 98% coverage corresponds to knowing approximately 8,000–9,000 word families; for academic text, substantially more.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance**: This is the keystone finding that conditionalises the reading-primary hypothesis — reading can build vocabulary *only if the reader already knows enough words to comprehend the text*. This creates a threshold paradox with major implications explored in Phase III.
> 
> **Operational Indicators**: Measured by calculating the percentage of tokens (running words) in a text covered by a given frequency band of vocabulary.
> 
> **Cross-References**: [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Verbal-Efficiency-Theory]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

#### 5. [DEFINITION] The Incremental Knowledge Model (Multi-Dimensional Word Knowledge) *(Line 329)*

> [!definition] The Incremental Knowledge Model (Multi-Dimensional Word Knowledge)
> A model of vocabulary knowledge according to which knowing a word is not binary (known or unknown) but involves gradual acquisition across multiple independent dimensions: (1) spoken/written form, (2) word parts and morphology, (3) syntagmatic relationships (collocations, typical sentence positions), (4) paradigmatic relationships (synonyms, antonyms, hyponyms), (5) semantic range and polysemy, (6) register and contextual appropriateness, (7) frequency and availability for production. Nation (2001) identifies nine aspects of word knowledge across three dimensions of form, meaning, and use, each of which can be known receptively or productively. Word knowledge is thus better understood as a *probability distribution* of knowledge states than as a toggle switch.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance**: This model is central to the philosophical challenge in Phase III: the question is not just *how many* words reading builds but *what kind* of word knowledge it builds. The argument will be that reading predominantly develops shallow receptive knowledge while leaving productive depth, morphological awareness, and collocational precision underdeveloped.
> 
> **Cross-References**: [[Semantic-Memory]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Elaborative-Inference]]

#### 6. [DEFINITION] The Matthew Effect in Reading *(Line 336)*

> [!definition] The Matthew Effect in Reading
> Stanovich's (1986) application of the sociological "Matthew Effect" (from Matthew 25:29 — "to him who has, more will be given") to reading development. Readers who start with larger vocabularies read more fluently, which produces more enjoyable reading experiences, which produces more reading, which produces more vocabulary — while readers who start with smaller vocabularies find reading effortful, read less, acquire less vocabulary, and fall further behind. The result is a *divergent trajectory*: vocabulary-rich and vocabulary-poor learners do not converge on the same vocabulary ceiling through reading; they diverge from it. This is one of the most robust findings in reading research.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance**: The Matthew Effect is the critical lens challenge: it reveals that the reading-primary pathway is not equally accessible to all learners, and that reading-based vocabulary acquisition systematically compounds advantage rather than distributing it equally.
> 
> **Cross-References**: [[Stanovich]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Incidental-Learning]]

#### 11. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What the Acquisition Rate Research Actually Shows *(Line 376)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Acquisition Rate Research Actually Shows
> The acquisition rate literature, taken as a whole, suggests that reading is a powerful mechanism for *consolidating* knowledge of words that are already partially known and that appear frequently enough to generate multiple exposures — but a weak mechanism for acquiring genuinely new, low-frequency words from single or rare encounters. Nation (2006) calculated that to acquire vocabulary at the rate that adult vocabulary size implies, readers would need to accumulate 10–20 meaningful encounters with a word over time. For high-frequency words in common genres, this is achievable through extensive reading. For Tier 2 academic vocabulary and Tier 3 technical vocabulary, the frequency of textual encounters may be too low to drive acquisition through reading alone without deliberate supplementation.

#### 12. [EVIDENCE] The Nation-Laufer Threshold Paradox *(Line 381)*

> [!evidence] The Nation-Laufer Threshold Paradox
> The 95–98% vocabulary coverage threshold (Nation, 2001; Laufer, 1989; Hu & Nation, 2000) creates a structural paradox for the reading-primary hypothesis. Reading can support incidental vocabulary acquisition only when the reader already understands most of the text — but understanding most of the text already requires a vocabulary size that many learners have not yet achieved. Below the threshold, new vocabulary encounters occur too densely and with too little context to enable reliable acquisition; above the threshold, the words encountered are often already known at some level. The sweet spot — enough unknown words to grow, enough known words to comprehend — is narrower than the thesis implies, and getting into it requires prior vocabulary investment that reading alone cannot provide.

#### 13. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Reading Naturally Directs Attention to Unknown Words *(Line 390)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Reading Naturally Directs Attention to Unknown Words
> **The assumption**: When a reader encounters an unfamiliar word in text, reading automatically triggers a processing event that results in some degree of vocabulary acquisition. The word is noticed, contextual inference is attempted, meaning is constructed, and encoding occurs.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: This assumption is so intuitive — surely we notice words we don't know — that it is rarely made explicit in the reading-for-vocabulary literature. It underlies the acquisition rate estimates (e.g., 5–15% per encounter) as their baseline.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: Skilled readers routinely *skip* unknown words without conscious processing. The strategies that make reading fluent — top-down inference, semantic prediction, tolerance of lexical uncertainty — also systematically prevent the effortful processing that vocabulary acquisition requires. Proficient readers trained to read for comprehension do not naturally slow down at unknown words; they fill in semantically plausible values and continue. This means the actual rate of *attended* word encounters may be much lower than the acquisition rate literature assumes.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption partially fails. Research on reading strategies (Nassaji, 2004) confirms that readers frequently do not stop to process unknown words and that comprehension-oriented reading is systematically less conducive to vocabulary acquisition than attention-oriented reading. The assumption survives only in conditions where readers are explicitly instructed to attend to vocabulary — which transforms incidental into intentional learning.

#### 14. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] Fluency and Acquisition: The Processing Trade-Off *(Line 401)*

> [!tension-identified] Fluency and Acquisition: The Processing Trade-Off
> There is a genuine tension at the heart of the reading-for-vocabulary relationship: the cognitive disposition that makes reading productive for comprehension — fluency, automaticity, tolerance of local ambiguity — is largely incompatible with the cognitive disposition that makes reading productive for vocabulary acquisition — attention to unfamiliar words, effortful inference, deeper encoding. These two dispositions pull in opposite directions. Reading optimised for comprehension routinely sacrifices word-level processing; reading optimised for vocabulary acquisition routinely sacrifices reading speed and passage-level comprehension. The received view treats reading as equally efficient for both purposes, but this is a theoretical claim that does not survive careful examination of the cognitive demands involved. The optimal vocabulary-building reading posture is more effortful and slower than the received view acknowledges.

#### 15. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Context Quality Problem *(Line 410)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Context Quality Problem
> The contextual inference mechanism works best when words are central to the passage's meaning, when contexts are informationally rich, and when the reader has sufficient background knowledge to interpret the context correctly. It works poorly when words are peripheral to the main idea, when contexts are sparse or misleading, and when vocabulary gaps produce cascading comprehension failures that undermine the inferential process itself. For high-frequency, contextually central words, the reading-primary hypothesis describes something real. For low-frequency, contextually peripheral words — precisely the vocabulary that matters most for expansion — contextual inference is unreliable, and the received view's confidence in it is unwarranted.

#### 16. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Reading as a Compounding Investment That Requires Initial Capital *(Line 417)*

> [!analytical-insight] Reading as a Compounding Investment That Requires Initial Capital
> The Matthew Effect reveals that reading-based vocabulary acquisition operates like a compounding interest account that requires a minimum initial deposit. Those who meet the vocabulary threshold for independent reading can compound their vocabulary through reading; those below the threshold cannot. This means the reading-primary hypothesis is implicitly recommending a strategy that works well for those who need it least (vocabulary-rich, fluent readers) and poorly for those who need it most (vocabulary-poor, struggling readers). Treating extensive reading as the primary vocabulary-building strategy for vocabulary-poor learners is not merely inefficient — it is structurally mis-targeted, because these learners are below the threshold that makes the strategy work.

#### 19. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Vocabulary-Reading Correlation Reflects Reading Causing Vocabulary Growth *(Line 447)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Vocabulary-Reading Correlation Reflects Reading Causing Vocabulary Growth
> **The assumption**: The strong positive correlation between reading amount and vocabulary size reflects reading as the cause, building vocabulary as the effect.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: The temporal logic seems obvious: you read first, then you learn words. The directionality is assumed to run from input to acquisition.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If vocabulary size partly *enables* reading (as the threshold research confirms it does), then the correlation is partly explained by a reverse path: those with large vocabularies read more because reading is more pleasurable and less effortful for them. The correlation reflects a self-reinforcing system, not a unidirectional cause-effect chain.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption fails in its unidirectional form. The evidence for bidirectional causality is strong (Cunningham & Stanovich, 1997; Stanovich, 1986). The practical implication: recommending extensive reading as the *primary* vocabulary-building strategy for low-vocabulary readers is analogous to telling someone who can't afford gym equipment to get fit by going to the gym — the strategy works only once the relevant precondition is already in place.

#### 20. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: What Counts as "Knowing a Word" Is Unitary *(Line 460)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: What Counts as "Knowing a Word" Is Unitary
> **The assumption**: The reading-primary hypothesis treats vocabulary acquisition as essentially the accumulation of more word-meaning pairs, where reading provides encounters that build these pairs. Word knowledge is treated as roughly binary or at most as varying along a single dimension (strength of association).
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: Measurement practices reinforce this assumption. Most vocabulary tests (including large-scale assessments) measure something close to receptive vocabulary — recognition of word meanings. When studies report strong correlations between reading and vocabulary, they are typically measuring this unidimensional receptive knowledge.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If word knowledge is multi-dimensional — if knowing a word fully means knowing its forms, meanings, collocations, register, morphological family, and syntactic behaviour — then reading builds only part of word knowledge. Specifically, reading efficiently builds receptive vocabulary (breadth) but inefficiently builds productive depth, collocational precision, register sensitivity, and morphological awareness. A learner who has read widely has large receptive vocabulary but may have a substantially smaller productive vocabulary — a gap that reading alone is structurally poorly positioned to close.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption clearly fails. Productive vocabulary is systematically smaller than receptive vocabulary for all learners; the gap is larger for words acquired primarily through incidental reading than for words that have been used, practised, and actively processed. Reading builds an impressive but shallow semantic knowledge base; deep, productive, flexible word knowledge requires active processing that reading rarely demands.

#### 21. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Received View's Systematic Measurement Bias *(Line 475)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Received View's Systematic Measurement Bias
> The reading-primary hypothesis was developed and tested primarily with measures of *receptive* vocabulary breadth — the number of words a reader can recognise and approximately define. These are precisely the dimensions of word knowledge that reading builds most efficiently. Had the research tradition used measures of productive vocabulary depth, collocational accuracy, or register sensitivity as its primary outcome measures, the reading-primary hypothesis would have found much weaker support in the empirical literature. The hypothesis is, in part, a consequence of what the field chose to measure — and what it chose to measure was shaped, in part, by what was easiest to operationalise. This is not a defect unique to vocabulary research, but it is a defect with significant practical consequences.

#### 22. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Context in Naturally Occurring Text Is Adequately Informative for Vocabulary Inference *(Line 478)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Context in Naturally Occurring Text Is Adequately Informative for Vocabulary Inference
> **The assumption**: When a reader encounters an unknown word in natural text, the surrounding context provides enough semantic, syntactic, and pragmatic information to support a reasonable inference about the word's meaning.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: Intuition strongly supports this assumption — we all have experiences of figuring out a word from context. Success cases are memorable; failure cases (where we inferred wrongly, or inferred nothing and moved on) are not.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: Research by Beck et al. (1983) and Schatz and Baldwin (1986) found that a substantial proportion of natural text contexts are misleading or uninformative for vocabulary inference. The implication: the mechanism by which reading is supposed to build vocabulary (contextual inference) operates less reliably and at lower accuracy than the received view requires.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption partially fails. Naturally occurring contexts vary enormously in informativeness, and the average informativeness across large text samples is considerably lower than the received view implies. Reliable contextual inference requires a level of prior vocabulary and background knowledge that the threshold hypothesis already conditionalises; below the threshold, contextual inference becomes unreliable and even misleading.

#### 25. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model of Vocabulary Acquisition *(Line 514)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model of Vocabulary Acquisition
> The dialectical process produces an original synthesis that cannot be articulated from within the reading-primary hypothesis: vocabulary acquisition is most efficiently conceptualised as a *dual-channel* process, with the optimal channel determined by vocabulary frequency and knowledge dimension. **Channel A: Deliberate Instruction** handles high-frequency vocabulary (bands 1–3,000 word families) and mid-frequency academic vocabulary (bands 3,000–9,000), using frequency-ranked word lists, spaced retrieval practice, and morphological pattern instruction to build the vocabulary threshold that enables independent reading. **Channel B: Extensive Reading** handles consolidation of partially known vocabulary, collocational enrichment, register sensitivity development, and exposure to low-frequency vocabulary in contextually rich authentic text — but only once Channel A has established the threshold.
> 
> The crucial innovation over the received view is that the two channels are *sequentially dependent* in the direction opposite to what the reading-primary hypothesis implies. The received view treats reading as primary and instruction as supplementary. The reconstructed model treats threshold instruction as the prerequisite that *unlocks* reading's acquisition potential — reading is the more important long-term mechanism but is conditionally dependent on prior deliberate learning. The relationship is not "instruction vs. reading" but "instruction → threshold → reading → consolidation → richer reading → further consolidation." This is a recursively generative architecture, but it cannot be entered from the reading end for learners who are below the threshold.
> 
> An additional dimension is metacognitive: Channel B is not passive. Extensive reading builds vocabulary efficiently only when the reader maintains *active word-noticing* — a metacognitive disposition toward flagging unfamiliar words, attempting inference, and following up with deliberate study or consultation. This is not automatic reading; it is strategically monitored reading, and developing this disposition requires metacognitive instruction that the reading-primary hypothesis does not include in its prescriptions.

#### 26. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Conditions of Applicability Problem *(Line 525)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Conditions of Applicability Problem
> The reading-primary hypothesis is an example of a theoretical claim that is true under certain conditions and false under others, but that has been stated as if it were unconditional. This is a recurrent pattern in educational research, where findings from specific populations (often relatively advantaged, literate, adult learners) are generalised to pedagogical prescriptions applied to populations that do not meet the original conditions. The dialectical process has revealed the specific conditions under which the reading-primary hypothesis holds — threshold adequacy, active word-noticing, reading in comprehensibility range — and this is as valuable as identifying where the hypothesis fails. A conditioned version of the hypothesis is more useful, not less, than the unconditioned version: it tells practitioners when to emphasise reading and when to prioritise direct instruction.

#### 27. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Active Reading Disposition as Metacognitive Infrastructure *(Line 528)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Active Reading Disposition as Metacognitive Infrastructure
> The reconstructed understanding points to an insight that neither the reading-primary hypothesis nor its alternatives fully articulate: the most important variable in reading-based vocabulary acquisition may not be the *amount* of reading but the *stance* toward unfamiliar words during reading. A reader who maintains a metacognitive disposition of active word-noticing — who treats unfamiliar words as data rather than obstacles, who pauses at semantic uncertainty rather than tolerating it, who uses the [[Encoding-Specificity-Principle]] to encode new words in the contexts where they were first encountered — will acquire substantially more vocabulary per hour of reading than a fluent but attention-inattentive reader who covers three times as many pages. This disposition is neither natural nor automatic; it is a learnable skill that requires metacognitive scaffolding, explicit instruction, and deliberate practice. The implication is that vocabulary-building reading programmes should spend as much time developing this metacognitive stance as they spend recommending text difficulty or reading volume.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition (Nation, 2001; Schmidt, 1990) *(Line 627)*

> [!definition] Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition (Nation, 2001; Schmidt, 1990)
> **Definition:** The acquisition of word meaning as a by-product of comprehension-focused reading or listening, without deliberate intention to learn the specific words encountered. Distinguished from intentional vocabulary learning by the absence of explicit study goals directed at word learning. Incidental acquisition does not mean unconscious acquisition — Schmidt's noticing hypothesis requires that some degree of attention to the word form is necessary — but the primary cognitive goal is comprehension rather than vocabulary learning.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The central mechanism of the thesis under examination. The dialectical process established that incidental acquisition is real but conditional — conditionalised on threshold, active noticing, and text coverage — rather than automatic and universal as the received view implies.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Measured in research by pre/post-testing on vocabulary items encountered in reading texts, with no explicit instruction on those items between tests.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** The distinction was sharpened in SLA research by Rod Ellis (1994) and operationalised empirically by Krashen (1982), Nation (2001), and Schmitt (2008, 2014).
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Incidental-Learning]], [[Elaborative-Inference]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring]]

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Text Coverage / Vocabulary Threshold (Laufer, 1989; Nation, 2001; Hu & Nation, 2000) *(Line 638)*

> [!definition] Text Coverage / Vocabulary Threshold (Laufer, 1989; Nation, 2001; Hu & Nation, 2000)
> **Definition:** The proportion of running words in a text that a reader already knows. The *vocabulary threshold* (or coverage threshold) is the level of text coverage — approximately 95–98% — below which comprehension breaks down sufficiently to prevent the contextual inference on which incidental vocabulary acquisition depends. At 95% coverage, approximately 1 in 20 words is unknown; at 98%, 1 in 50. For English fiction, 98% coverage corresponds to approximately 8,000–9,000 word families; for academic text, substantially more.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The single most important empirical finding in the dialectical challenge — the threshold creates a paradox for the reading-primary hypothesis and forces the conditioned reconstruction.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Calculated by identifying all word tokens in a text and determining what proportion fall within a given frequency band (using resources like Nation's BNC/COCA word lists).
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]]

#### 38. [DEFINITION] The Matthew Effect in Reading (Stanovich, 1986) *(Line 647)*

> [!definition] The Matthew Effect in Reading (Stanovich, 1986)
> **Definition:** The self-reinforcing divergence in reading and vocabulary development whereby those who begin with larger vocabularies read more fluently, which produces more pleasurable reading experiences, which produces more reading, which produces more vocabulary — while those with smaller vocabularies face a reverse spiral. Named after the biblical passage in which those who already have receive more. Empirically demonstrated through longitudinal reading research showing diverging vocabulary trajectories between high-vocabulary and low-vocabulary learners.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The critical lens challenge — the Matthew Effect reveals that reading-based vocabulary acquisition is a structurally inequitable mechanism that compounds pre-existing advantage rather than distributing vocabulary growth equally.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Diverging growth trajectories in longitudinal vocabulary studies; disproportionate reading volume in high-vocabulary learners; lower per-page vocabulary acquisition in low-vocabulary readers despite equivalent reading time.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Stanovich]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Incidental-Learning]]

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Incremental Knowledge Model (Nation, 2001; Schmitt, 2014) *(Line 656)*

> [!definition] Incremental Knowledge Model (Nation, 2001; Schmitt, 2014)
> **Definition:** A model of vocabulary knowledge according to which knowing a word involves gradual acquisition across multiple independent dimensions: spoken/written form, word parts, syntagmatic relationships (collocations), paradigmatic relationships (synonyms/antonyms), semantic range and polysemy, register and contextual appropriateness, and availability for production. Each dimension can be known receptively (recognition in context) or productively (availability for use). Word knowledge is not binary (known/unknown) but a probability distribution across dimensions and modalities.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Provides the philosophical lens challenge — reading efficiently builds receptive knowledge but systematically underbuilds productive depth, collocational precision, and register sensitivity. The model reveals that the reading-vocabulary correlation, based primarily on receptive vocabulary measures, overstates reading's contribution to full word knowledge.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Semantic-Memory]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Elaborative-Rehearsal]]

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Receptive vs. Productive Vocabulary Knowledge (Nation, 2001) *(Line 663)*

> [!definition] Receptive vs. Productive Vocabulary Knowledge (Nation, 2001)
> **Definition:** A fundamental distinction in vocabulary knowledge. *Receptive* (or passive) vocabulary comprises words a reader can recognise and approximately understand when encountered in context; *productive* (or active) vocabulary comprises words a speaker/writer can accurately deploy in their own output. For adult proficient readers, productive vocabulary is typically 50–70% of receptive vocabulary in size (and substantially narrower in depth). The gap exists because contextual reading provides reception-building encounters without production-building practice.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Reading-primary vocabulary instruction builds primarily receptive vocabulary. The reconstructed model specifies that productive vocabulary requires deliberate practice, generative use, and active processing beyond what reading provides.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Shallow-Processing]], [[Elaborative-Rehearsal]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]]

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Active Word-Noticing Disposition (adapted from Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis, 1990) *(Line 670)*

> [!definition] Active Word-Noticing Disposition (adapted from Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis, 1990)
> **Definition:** A metacognitive stance toward reading in which the reader deliberately maintains attention to unfamiliar lexical items, attempts semantic inference from context, flags items for follow-up study, and monitors for vocabulary gaps rather than tolerating them as comprehension noise. Distinguished from passive reading by the deliberate allocation of attentional resources to word-level processing alongside passage-level comprehension. Not natural or automatic; a learnable disposition that requires explicit metacognitive development.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Identified in the reconstructive synthesis as the key variable mediating reading's vocabulary acquisition efficiency. The same reading volume produces substantially different vocabulary outcomes depending on whether the active word-noticing disposition is present.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Comprehension-Monitoring]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model (Original Synthesis — this report) *(Line 677)*

> [!definition] Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model (Original Synthesis — this report)
> **Definition:** A reconstructed model of vocabulary acquisition that assigns different acquisition strategies to different vocabulary frequency bands and knowledge dimensions. *Channel A (Deliberate Instruction)* handles high-to-mid-frequency vocabulary (approximately the 1–9,000 word family range) using spaced retrieval practice, frequency-ranked word lists, and morphological instruction to establish the vocabulary threshold. *Channel B (Extensive Reading with Active Noticing)* handles consolidation, collocational enrichment, and exposure to contextually embedded low-frequency vocabulary — but only after Channel A has established the threshold. The two channels are sequentially dependent, with Channel A as the prerequisite for Channel B's efficiency.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The original contribution of this dialectical re-examination — a reconstructed model that preserves what is correct in the reading-primary hypothesis while specifying the conditions under which it holds and the complementary mechanism required when those conditions are not met.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Spaced-Repetition]], [[Retrieval-Practice]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Beck's Three-Tier Vocabulary Framework (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2002, 2013) *(Line 684)*

> [!definition] Beck's Three-Tier Vocabulary Framework (Beck, McKeown & Kucan, 2002, 2013)
> **Definition:** A classification of English vocabulary into three pedagogically relevant tiers. *Tier 1* comprises basic, everyday vocabulary acquired through everyday conversation and experience (e.g., *happy*, *walk*, *table*). *Tier 2* comprises high-frequency academic and literary vocabulary that appears across multiple domains of formal discourse but is rarely used in everyday conversation (e.g., *pernicious*, *infer*, *consequence*) — this is the vocabulary most critical for academic literacy and most underserved by reading alone. *Tier 3* comprises low-frequency, domain-specific technical vocabulary (e.g., *photosynthesis*, *amortization*).
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The three-tier distinction maps onto the frequency-indexed dual-channel model: Tier 1 is acquired through normal language exposure and early education; Tier 2 is the target of Channel A deliberate instruction; Tier 3 is domain reading with glossing and deliberate study.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Incidental-Learning]], [[Semantic-Memory]], [[Situated-Learning]]

#### 44. [DEFINITION] Krashen's Input Hypothesis (Krashen, 1982, 1985) *(Line 691)*

> [!definition] Krashen's Input Hypothesis (Krashen, 1982, 1985)
> **Definition:** The theoretical claim that language acquisition — including vocabulary acquisition — occurs through exposure to *comprehensible input* slightly beyond the learner's current level (*i+1*). On this view, explicit instruction plays a minor role; the primary acquisition mechanism is the processing of large quantities of meaningful, comprehensible input. Applied to vocabulary, the hypothesis predicts that extensive reading is the primary and most efficient vocabulary acquisition mechanism.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The proximate theoretical source of the received view under examination. The dialectical process established that the hypothesis captures something real (reading is the dominant mechanism for mature, above-threshold learners) but overstates its sufficiency and understates the threshold conditions required for it to operate.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Schema-Theory]], [[Incidental-Learning]], [[Self-Regulated-Learning]]

#### 45. [DEFINITION] Passive Exposure Fallacy (Original Synthesis — this report) *(Line 698)*

> [!definition] Passive Exposure Fallacy (Original Synthesis — this report)
> **Definition:** The error of treating exposure to information as equivalent to acquisition of it. The Passive Exposure Fallacy operates when a learning prescription recommends increased exposure volume (more reading, more practice time, more hours) without specifying the active processing requirements that make exposure educationally productive. It is a *fallacy* because it conflates a necessary condition (some exposure is required) with a sufficient condition (enough exposure is sufficient). Active, generative processing is the rate-limiting step in vocabulary acquisition; exposure without active processing yields diminishing returns.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The abstract principle generalised from the vocabulary dialectic with the widest applicability across domains.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Shallow-Processing]], [[Desirable-Difficulties]], [[Transfer-of-Learning]]

#### 46. [DEFINITION] The Threshold-Dependency Principle (Original Synthesis — this report) *(Line 705)*

> [!definition] The Threshold-Dependency Principle (Original Synthesis — this report)

Below the threshold, the primary mechanism is inefficient and a different acquisition strategy (typically more deliberate and instructional) is required; above it, the primary mechanism becomes self-reinforcing. #### 52. [TENSION] Incidental vs. Intentional Learning as Primary Mechanism *(Line 757)*

> [!tension] Incidental vs. Intentional Learning as Primary Mechanism
> **Position A (Thesis):** Incidental acquisition from reading is the primary mechanism for mature vocabulary development; intentional study builds initial representations but reading enriches and consolidates them over time. The sheer scale of mature vocabulary (15,000–50,000+ word families) requires a mechanism that operates without deliberate effort.
> 
> **Position B (Challenge):** Intentional vocabulary learning, organised by frequency and supported by spaced retrieval practice, is more efficient per unit time for acquiring new vocabulary; reading is most valuable as a consolidation mechanism for words already partially known. The scale argument misidentifies the source of large vocabularies — they grow from reading, but reading works because prior deliberate learning has established the threshold.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** Evidence supports a conditional version of both positions: incidental acquisition dominates for above-threshold learners reading in their comprehensibility range; intentional learning is more efficient for below-threshold learners and for productive/deep knowledge development. The relative efficiency depends critically on frequency band and knowledge dimension.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** Determines how reading programs and vocabulary interventions should be structured — whether reading should be the centrepiece with instruction as support, or instruction as the foundation with reading as the amplifier.
> 
> **This Report's Resolution:** The Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model resolves the tension by assigning each mechanism to the domain where it is most efficient: deliberate study for high-to-mid-frequency vocabulary below the threshold, reading for consolidation and enrichment above it.

#### 53. [TENSION] Breadth vs. Depth in Vocabulary Development *(Line 768)*

> [!tension] Breadth vs. Depth in Vocabulary Development
> **Position A:** Building large receptive vocabularies (breadth) should precede or accompany building deep productive knowledge of individual words; encountering many words in context establishes the semantic network within which deep knowledge of individual words becomes possible.
> 
> **Position B:** Developing deep, accurate, productive knowledge of a smaller core vocabulary (depth) is more valuable for academic and professional literacy than shallow recognition of a large number of words; deep knowledge of one word enables more productive language use than shallow knowledge of five.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** Evidence suggests breadth and depth are correlated but distinct, and that the optimal balance depends on learner goals and context. For reading comprehension, breadth (meeting the threshold) is the priority; for academic writing and argument, depth of productive vocabulary is the limiting factor.
> 
> **This Report's Resolution:** The Incremental Knowledge Model reveals that breadth and depth are not alternatives but sequential targets: breadth to reach the threshold (Channel A), then reading to consolidate and deepen (Channel B), then active use in writing and discussion to develop productive depth.

#### 54. [TENSION] The Sufficiency vs. Necessity of Reading *(Line 777)*

> [!tension] The Sufficiency vs. Necessity of Reading
> **Position A:** Reading is both necessary and sufficient for robust vocabulary acquisition by mature, literate learners; the correlational evidence shows that vocabulary size is primarily a function of reading volume, and this relationship is causal.
> 
> **Position B:** Reading is necessary but not sufficient; it is the dominant mechanism for above-threshold learners but requires deliberate supplementation at the vocabulary threshold, for productive depth development, and for learners who are below the coverage threshold.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The necessity claim survives scrutiny; the sufficiency claim does not survive the threshold paradox, the multi-dimensionality challenge, or the Matthew Effect analysis.
> 
> **This Report's Resolution:** The reconstructed model preserves reading's necessity while specifying the conditions under which it is sufficient and the mechanisms required when those conditions are not met.

#### 55. [OPEN-QUESTION] What is the Optimal Text Coverage for Vocabulary Growth? *(Line 786)*

> [!open-question] What is the Optimal Text Coverage for Vocabulary Growth?
> Nation's 95% and 98% coverage thresholds are widely cited but contested. Some researchers argue that 90% coverage may be sufficient when readers are trained in active word-noticing strategies; others suggest that 98% underestimates the coverage required for reliable incidental acquisition of specific vocabulary items. The optimal coverage level may also interact with text genre, learner vocabulary size, and reading purpose in ways that current research has not fully specified.

#### 56. [DEBATE] Can the Active Word-Noticing Disposition Be Reliably Taught at Scale? *(Line 789)*

> [!debate] Can the Active Word-Noticing Disposition Be Reliably Taught at Scale?
> The reconstructed model assigns the active word-noticing disposition a central role in mediating reading's vocabulary acquisition efficiency. #### 72. [PROTOCOL] The Frequency-Indexed Vocabulary Development Protocol *(Line 914)*

> [!protocol] The Frequency-Indexed Vocabulary Development Protocol
> **Context:** For any adult learner seeking to deliberately and efficiently expand their vocabulary — whether in L1 academic English, a second language, or a domain-specific technical vocabulary. Reflects the reconstructed Frequency-Indexed Dual-Channel Model.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Assess your current vocabulary threshold.** Use Nation's Vocabulary Levels Test (freely available online) to determine your approximate vocabulary size by frequency band. Identify which frequency band you are currently in and estimate your text coverage in the types of text you read regularly.
> 2. **If below 8,000 word families (Channel A Priority):** Engage in deliberate study of high-frequency and mid-frequency academic vocabulary using frequency-ranked word lists (Nation's word lists, Academic Word List). Use spaced repetition software ([[Spaced-Repetition-Systems]]) for retention. Target 15–30 minutes of deliberate study per day. Continue until text coverage in your target genre reaches approximately 95–98%.
> 3. **If above 8,000 word families (Channel B Priority, with A supplement):** Engage in extensive reading with active word-noticing. Carry a notebook or digital note-taking tool; when you encounter an unfamiliar word, note it, infer the meaning from context, then verify and add to a review deck. Target 30–45 minutes of active-noticing reading per session.
> 4. **Develop productive vocabulary.** For words you want in your productive vocabulary (available for use in your own writing), deliberate study alone is insufficient. Practice using them: in writing, in explanation to others, in paraphrase. The [[Generation-Effect]] ensures that words you generate (as output) are better remembered than words you passively process.
> 5. **Review your word-noticing stance metacognitively.** After each reading session, spend 2 minutes asking: Did I maintain the word-noticing stance or did I slip into passive reading? How many unfamiliar words did I encounter vs. how many did I actively process? Adjust the next session accordingly.
> 
> **Success Criteria:** Increasing vocabulary test scores across sessions; decreasing look-up frequency per page over time; growing productive vocabulary evidenced by richer and more precise language in your own writing.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** (1) Treating deliberate study as a substitute for reading rather than a prerequisite for it; (2) treating passive reading as a substitute for productive practice when deep word knowledge is the goal; (3) abandoning the active word-noticing stance during reading sessions as reading speed increases — this is the Passive Exposure Fallacy in practice.

> [!flashcard] Seed 09
> **Q:** What is the difference between receptive and productive vocabulary knowledge, and why does reading primarily build the former?
> **A:** Receptive vocabulary comprises words a reader can recognise and approximately understand in context; productive vocabulary comprises words a speaker/writer can accurately deploy in their own output. Productive vocabulary is typically 50–70% of receptive vocabulary in size and substantially shallower in depth. Reading builds receptive knowledge because the cognitive task is comprehension (processing incoming words for meaning), not production (generating words from memory). Productive knowledge requires active use, generation, and retrieval practice — cognitive operations that reading rarely demands.
