#### 2. [STEEL-MAN] The Strongest Version of the Bottom-Up Received View *(Line 305)*

> [!steel-man] The Strongest Version of the Bottom-Up Received View
> The most compelling version of the bottom-up account begins with an undeniable empirical finding: in early reading development, the single strongest predictor of comprehension is **[[Decoding|decoding skill]]**. Readers who cannot efficiently convert print to phonological representation cannot comprehend, because their [[Working-Memory|working memory]] is fully consumed by the mechanics of word recognition. When those mechanics become automatic — when [[Lexical-Automaticity|lexical automaticity]] is achieved — working memory capacity is freed for higher-level processing, and comprehension improves almost as a consequence. The [[Simple-View-of-Reading|Simple View of Reading]] (Gough & Tunmer, 1986) formalizes this: Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Linguistic Comprehension, where both components are necessary and neither alone is sufficient.
> 
> Furthermore, most linguistic comprehension draws on processes that operate automatically and implicitly: syntactic parsing, semantic integration, anaphora resolution. A fluent reader integrating "The dog bit the man. He went to the hospital." does not consciously reason about who "He" refers to — this referential resolution happens automatically, within milliseconds, below the threshold of conscious awareness. If this much happens automatically, the view goes, why should we expect the larger-scale assembly of meaning to be fundamentally different?
> 
> **Why this version matters**: If this view were correct, the primary leverage point for improving comprehension would always be at the decoding and vocabulary level. Improving someone's word recognition would reliably improve their comprehension, and strategic training would be secondary. For early readers and those with genuine decoding deficits, this prediction is accurate and highly useful.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] [[Situation-Model|Situation Model]] (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 322)*

> [!definition] [[Situation-Model|Situation Model]] (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> **Definition:** A situation model is the mental representation of the situation or state of affairs that a text describes — not the text itself, but the *world* the text is about. It is constructed by integrating the propositional content of the text base with the reader's prior knowledge, goals, and inferences. A situation model is the difference between knowing what the text *says* and understanding what it *means* in relation to a coherent state of affairs. It is not stored as a linguistic structure but as an integrated, multimodal representation that can include spatial, causal, temporal, and intentional dimensions.
> 
> **Boundary conditions**: A situation model is NOT the same as the text base (the propositions explicitly stated), nor is it simply a summary of the text. It includes inferred information that the text does not state, background knowledge that the text assumes, and the reader's own elaborations. Two readers can process the same text base yet construct dramatically different situation models depending on their prior knowledge and strategic engagement.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] [[Propositional-Representation|Propositional Text Base]] (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978) *(Line 327)*

> [!definition] [[Propositional-Representation|Propositional Text Base]] (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978)
> **Definition:** The propositional text base is the explicit semantic content of a text — a network of propositions (subject-predicate structures) corresponding to the meanings stated by individual sentences and their immediate connections. It represents what the text explicitly says, minus the words themselves. Processing the text base is necessary for situation model construction but not sufficient for it: a reader can represent the propositional content of a passage while failing to construct a coherent situation model if they lack the prior knowledge or generate insufficient inferences to integrate the text base into a world-representation.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] The [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|Three-Level Model of Text Representation]] (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 330)*

> [!definition] The [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|Three-Level Model of Text Representation]] (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> **Definition:** Text comprehension produces three levels of representation, each dependent on but not reducible to the one below it. The **surface code** (exact words and syntax) fades rapidly. The **propositional text base** (explicit semantic content) persists longer and supports recognition and verbatim recall. The **situation model** (mental representation of the described world) persists longest, supports inference, transfer, and application, and constitutes the gold standard of comprehension. Crucially, the levels are dissociable: readers can succeed at one while failing at the next.

#### 12. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Decoding-Comprehension Correlation Tension *(Line 374)*

> [!tension-identified] The Decoding-Comprehension Correlation Tension
> The empirical literature presents an apparent tension: at early reading stages, decoding is by far the strongest predictor of comprehension; at later stages, that predictive power shifts to vocabulary, inference skill, and prior knowledge. The received view explains early-stage findings well but fails late-stage findings. What this suggests is not that the received view is simply wrong — it is that it is *developmentally bounded*. It describes the binding constraint of early reading accurately, and then fails to update when decoding automaticity no longer constrains the ceiling on comprehension. The danger is applying the early-stage account to late-stage reading challenges, which is precisely what many self-taught adult readers do. They work on vocabulary and careful sentence parsing, missing the inference-generation and model-maintenance operations that are now the actual binding constraint.

#### 13. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Linguistic Compositionality Scales to Discourse Level *(Line 381)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Linguistic Compositionality Scales to Discourse Level
> **The assumption**: Text-level comprehension works on the same compositional principle as sentence-level comprehension — understand each sentence, combine according to discourse structure, and the text-level meaning follows.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: Compositionality is so fundamental to linguistic understanding at the sentence level that it is natural to assume it extends to higher levels of text structure. The assumption rarely surfaces explicitly because it seems like the obvious continuation of how language works.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If discourse-level comprehension is not compositional — if text meaning requires pragmatic inference, background knowledge integration, and goal-directed interpretation that go beyond combining sentence meanings — then the entire "understand the sentences and the big picture follows" prescription collapses. Comprehension would require operations categorically different from and additional to sentence-level parsing.
> 
> **Assessment**: The assumption fails at the discourse level. Grice's theory of conversational implicature, discourse coherence research, and the inference generation literature all confirm that text meaning depends on pragmatic inference that goes beyond what compositionality provides. The most famous demonstration: "I have a car" and "I have a car, but I won't drive you" mean very different things even though the added clause contains no logical negation of the first. Discourse meaning is not the sum of sentence meanings — it is a pragmatic construction that depends on context, background knowledge, and inferential processes that the text does not specify.

#### 14. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Text Self-Sufficiency *(Line 392)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Text Self-Sufficiency
> **The assumption**: The text contains its meaning. A reader who processes the text's explicit content faithfully will extract that meaning. Background knowledge may help, but it is not constitutive.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: Written language is specifically designed to be relatively context-independent compared to spoken language — this is one of its defining features. It is therefore reasonable to expect that the text contains everything necessary for its own understanding. But this is a design aspiration, not an achievement.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If background knowledge is not merely helpful but *constitutive* — if situation models literally cannot be constructed without prior knowledge that the text assumes rather than states — then knowledge-poor readers face a categorically different reading challenge than the received view suggests. Their problem is not insufficient attention to sentences; it is insufficient prior knowledge to execute the inferential operations the text demands.
> 
> **Assessment**: The assumption fails. Research on readability and text comprehension consistently demonstrates that prior knowledge about the topic is one of the strongest predictors of situation model quality — often stronger than verbal ability or vocabulary. Readers with topic-relevant schemas can construct coherent situation models from relatively impoverished texts; readers without those schemas fail on rich texts. This is not because schemas help — it is because schemas are necessary for the inferential operations that bridge the propositional text base to a situation model.

#### 15. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What the Fluency Illusion Research Shows *(Line 407)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Fluency Illusion Research Shows
> Studies on judgment of learning (Nelson & Narens, 1990; Koriat, 1997) demonstrate that readers' confidence in their comprehension is primarily predicted by processing fluency — how easily and smoothly the text was processed — rather than by the actual coherence of the situation model they constructed. This means that readers who construct poor situation models but process the text fluently will systematically rate their own comprehension as high. They will not spontaneously re-read, generate questions, or engage in monitoring strategies precisely because the fluency signal tells them everything is fine. The received view, by implying that fluent sentence-level processing leads to comprehension, reinforces and validates this monitoring failure. It provides a theoretical justification for the fluency illusion rather than a corrective against it.

#### 16. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Careful Reading Paradox *(Line 412)*

> [!tension-identified] The Careful Reading Paradox
> There is a genuine paradox at the heart of the received view's practical guidance: the most common advice derived from it — read slowly and carefully, making sure you understand each sentence before moving on — may actually impair situation model construction in some circumstances. Research on reading strategies suggests that readers who parse each sentence in isolation, seeking local clarity before moving forward, sometimes lose the thread of global structure precisely because working memory is consumed by local verification. The situation model, which requires holding and updating a global representation, degrades when attention is too narrowly focused on local processing. Skilled readers who construct good situation models characteristically do *less* sentence-level re-reading and *more* global-level monitoring — a strategy profile that the received view would never recommend.

#### 17. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What the Prior Knowledge Research Shows *(Line 421)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Prior Knowledge Research Shows
> Studies consistently demonstrate that prior knowledge about a text's topic predicts situation model quality independently of, and often more strongly than, verbal ability, vocabulary, and reading fluency. Recht and Leslie (1988), in a landmark study, showed that low-verbal students with high baseball knowledge comprehended a baseball passage significantly better than high-verbal students with low baseball knowledge. The situation model — a coherent representation of *what is happening in the described world* — simply cannot be constructed by a reader who lacks the conceptual structures to interpret the events the text describes. This is not a motivational or strategic problem; it is a structural one. Prior knowledge is constitutive of situation model construction, not merely helpful for it.

#### 21. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Situation Model Construction is Unitary *(Line 454)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Situation Model Construction is Unitary
> **The assumption**: Situation model construction is a single process — either you "get" the big picture or you don't. It is treated as a binary outcome of sufficient sentence-level processing.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: The received view makes no distinctions within "comprehension" beyond successful or unsuccessful sentence decoding. The idea that situation model construction has multiple, separately monitored dimensions is invisible from within the received view's framework.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If situation model construction is multidimensional — if different readers (or the same reader with different texts) fail on different dimensions — then "you didn't understand it" is not a useful diagnosis. Different dimension failures require different remediation. A reader who fails on causal dimension tracking needs to focus on asking "why?" during reading; one who fails on entity tracking needs to focus on referential monitoring; one who fails on intentional tracking needs to focus on character motivation.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption definitively fails. The Event Indexing Model is well-supported empirically: reading time studies, text recall studies, and inference verification studies all confirm that the five dimensions produce independent cognitive effects. A reader who understands this can use the five dimensions as a diagnostic checklist when they experience comprehension failure.

#### 22. [ASSUMPTION-CHALLENGE] The Hidden Assumption: Comprehension Monitoring Is Automatic *(Line 469)*

> [!assumption-challenge] The Hidden Assumption: Comprehension Monitoring Is Automatic
> **The assumption**: If something goes wrong with comprehension, the reader will notice. The monitoring function is built in — you feel confused when you don't understand.
> 
> **Why it's hidden**: Confusion IS the signal of comprehension failure at the text-base level. When you fail to decode a word or parse a sentence, you experience confusion immediately. This makes it natural to assume that comprehension monitoring extends to the situation model level.
> 
> **What happens if it's wrong**: If situation model failures do not automatically produce confusion — if the fluency signal can persist even when no coherent situation model is being constructed — then readers cannot rely on their felt sense of comprehension as a monitoring signal. They need explicit, deliberate monitoring strategies that test the situation model rather than the fluency experience.
> 
> **Assessment**: This assumption fails. The [[The-Fluency-Metacognition-Paradox|fluency-metacognition paradox]] and the [[Metacognitive-Monitoring|metacognitive monitoring]] literature both confirm that readers' comprehension confidence is predicted by processing fluency rather than comprehension quality. The implication is uncomfortable but important: if you feel like you understand what you are reading, that feeling is evidence of successful decoding, not evidence of successful situation model construction. These are different things, and only the latter constitutes genuine comprehension.

#### 26. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Automatic-Assembly Fallacy: A Named Principle *(Line 508)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Automatic-Assembly Fallacy: A Named Principle
> The central mistake in the received view can be named and generalized: the **Automatic-Assembly Fallacy** is the misconception that adequate processing at a lower level of analysis will automatically produce coherent representations at a higher level, without dedicated attention or strategy directed at that higher level. In reading comprehension, it takes the form of believing that sentence-level understanding automatically produces situation model coherence. But the assumption is generalizable: it appears wherever complex, multi-level systems are processed, and in each case the same error — trusting that local success implies global coherence — produces the same practical failures. The Fallacy is not about reading specifically; it is about the cognitive conditions for producing integrated representations at any level of analysis above where deliberate attention is currently deployed.

#### 27. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Text-Base Trap: Illusory Comprehension as a Stable Equilibrium *(Line 511)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Text-Base Trap: Illusory Comprehension as a Stable Equilibrium
> The combination of the fluency illusion and the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy produces what can be called the **Text-Base Trap**: a stable cognitive equilibrium in which a reader successfully processes the text base, experiences smooth fluency, interprets that fluency as comprehension, and therefore generates no monitoring responses that would initiate the additional operations required for situation model construction. The trap is stable because no negative feedback is generated — the reader feels fine, the text processing is proceeding normally, and there is no phenomenological signal of the absence of a situation model. The Text-Base Trap is the mechanism by which readers "read" extensively without building transferable comprehension. It is self-sustaining until the reader encounters a comprehension test that specifically targets the situation model — at which point the absence becomes undeniable.

#### 28. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Metacognitive Inversion Required *(Line 518)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Metacognitive Inversion Required
> The reconstruction demands a metacognitive inversion that is genuinely difficult to execute. The received view trains readers to monitor at the text-base level: when you feel confused, re-read. The reconstruction requires monitoring at the situation model level: even when you do NOT feel confused, periodically test your model. This is counterintuitive because it requires generating a monitoring activity in the *absence* of a negative monitoring signal. It is the equivalent of periodically checking whether your GPS destination is still correct even when the current driving instructions are clear. The absence of a wrong-turn warning does not mean you are going to the right place — and the absence of a comprehension failure signal does not mean you are building a coherent situation model.

#### 37. [DEFINITION] [[Situation-Model|Situation Model]] (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 611)*

> [!definition] [[Situation-Model|Situation Model]] (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> **Definition:** The mental representation of the situation described by a text — not the text's content but the world the text is about, constructed by integrating text propositions with prior knowledge through active inference. Boundary condition: a situation model is not a summary of what the text says; it includes substantial inferred and background content not explicitly stated. This term was reconstructed in this analysis: the received view effectively denied that situation models are a distinct representational level.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The central construct that the bottom-up received view fails to properly account for; what the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy mistakes for an automatic product of sentence-level processing.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Ability to answer inference questions, apply information to new scenarios, and explain the *why* and *how* of described events — tasks that do not follow from text-base processing alone.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** Introduced by Teun van Dijk & Walter Kintsch (1983); extended and formalized in Kintsch (1988, 1998); operationalized in the Event Indexing Model (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998).
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Mental-Models]], [[Elaborative-Inference]], [[Schema-Theory]]

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Propositional Text Base (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978) *(Line 622)*

> [!definition] Propositional Text Base (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978)
> **Definition:** The network of semantic propositions explicitly stated by a text, representing what the text *says* at the level of sentence meanings and their immediate connections. Boundary condition: the text base is NOT the situation model; the two are distinct representational levels that are empirically dissociable.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The level of representation that the received view mistakenly treats as equivalent to full comprehension; the level of representation that the Text-Base Trap produces as an outcome masquerading as genuine comprehension.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Ability to answer recognition questions that closely paraphrase the text's language; ability to produce near-verbatim summaries.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** Formalized by Kintsch & van Dijk (1978) as part of the early Construction-Integration model development.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Shallow-Processing]], [[Semantic-Memory]]

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Automatic-Assembly Fallacy (Claude analytical synthesis, this report) *(Line 633)*

> [!definition] Automatic-Assembly Fallacy (Claude analytical synthesis, this report)
> **Definition:** The mistaken belief that adequate processing at a lower level of representational analysis automatically produces coherent integration at a higher level, without dedicated attention or strategy directed at that higher level. In reading, it takes the form of believing that sentence-level understanding produces situation model coherence automatically. Boundary condition: the Fallacy does not apply when the text is extremely simple, the prior knowledge is extremely rich, and the inferential demand is minimal — in those conditions, something like automatic assembly does occur.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Named original synthesis providing the generalizable principle at the heart of the bottom-up received view's failure; transfers to software architecture, organizational management, quantitative modeling, and any domain with multi-level integration.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** The Fallacy is active when a reader (or engineer, manager, analyst) expresses satisfaction with local quality without having executed any global coherence check.
> 
> **Etymology/Intellectual Lineage:** Claude analytical synthesis; mechanistically grounded in the dissociation literature, the CI model, and the Event Indexing Model.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Situation-Model]], [[The-Fluency-Trap]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Cognitive-Architecture]]

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Text-Base Trap (Claude analytical synthesis, this report) *(Line 644)*

> [!definition] Text-Base Trap (Claude analytical synthesis, this report)
> **Definition:** A stable cognitive equilibrium in which a reader successfully processes the propositional text base, experiences processing fluency, interprets that fluency as comprehension, and therefore generates no monitoring responses that would initiate the additional operations required for situation model construction. The trap is self-sustaining in the absence of external comprehension testing that specifically targets the situation model.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Explains the mechanism by which readers can read extensively without developing transferable comprehension; the practical instantiation of the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy in reading.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Feeling of having understood while being unable to paraphrase, apply, or explain the material — particularly its causal and intentional structure.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[The-Fluency-Trap]], [[The-Fluency-Illusion]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Shallow-Processing]]

#### 41. [DEFINITION] [[Elaborative-Inference|Elaborative Inference]] (McKoon & Ratcliff, 1992; Graesser et al.) *(Line 653)*

> [!definition] [[Elaborative-Inference|Elaborative Inference]] (McKoon & Ratcliff, 1992; Graesser et al.)
> **Definition:** An inference that goes beyond what the text explicitly states to elaborate the situation model with information derived from prior knowledge — for example, inferring that a character is angry because the text described them slamming a door. Boundary condition: elaborative inferences are not required for text-base construction but are necessary for rich situation model construction; they are not generated automatically by most readers.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The primary cognitive mechanism bridging the text base to the situation model; its absence is the proximate cause of situation model failure in specific comprehenders.
> 
> **Operational Indicators:** Answering correctly on inference questions that require information not stated in the text; spontaneously generating explanatory and predictive elaborations during reading.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Prior-Knowledge]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Elaborative-Interrogation]]

#### 42. [DEFINITION] [[The-Fluency-Trap|Fluency Illusion]] / [[The-Fluency-Metacognition-Paradox|Fluency-Metacognition Paradox]] *(Line 662)*

> [!definition] [[The-Fluency-Trap|Fluency Illusion]] / [[The-Fluency-Metacognition-Paradox|Fluency-Metacognition Paradox]]
> **Definition:** The phenomenon by which smooth, effortless processing of surface code and propositional text base generates subjective confidence in comprehension quality that does not accurately reflect the quality of the situation model constructed. Boundary condition: the illusion operates most powerfully when texts are linguistically familiar even when situationally novel — when the reader can parse the sentences easily while lacking the prior knowledge to integrate them into a coherent situation model.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The mechanism by which the Text-Base Trap becomes stable; the reason why monitoring strategies targeted at the situation model level must be explicitly deployed rather than relying on naturally arising monitoring signals.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Metacognitive-Calibration]], [[The-Fluency-Trap]], [[Judgment-of-Learning]]

#### 44. [DEFINITION] Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston, & Graesser, 1995) *(Line 676)*

Boundary condition: the model describes competent readers; poor comprehenders show less sensitivity to dimension shifts, indicating less active model monitoring.

#### 45. [DEFINITION] [[Matthew-Effect-in-Reading]] (Stanovich, 1986) *(Line 683)*

> [!definition] [[Matthew-Effect-in-Reading]] (Stanovich, 1986)
> **Definition:** The self-amplifying advantage in reading development by which good readers, through extensive reading, develop richer vocabulary and prior knowledge that makes subsequent reading easier and more productive, while poor readers develop less, making subsequent reading harder — resulting in compounding divergence between strong and weak readers over time. Named after the biblical principle that advantages accrue to those who already have them.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Explains why situation model deficits are not static problems but self-sustaining ones; prior knowledge being constitutive of comprehension means that knowledge-poor readers cannot bootstrap their way out of poor comprehension through sheer attentiveness.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Decoding]], [[Prior-Knowledge]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Lexical-Automaticity]]

#### 46. [DEFINITION] Construction-Integration (CI) Model (Kintsch, 1988) *(Line 690)*

> [!definition] Construction-Integration (CI) Model (Kintsch, 1988)
> **Definition:** A computational model of text comprehension proposing two cyclically alternating phases: construction (building an initial network of propositions from text plus associated prior knowledge activations) and integration (refining the network through spreading activation to produce a coherent representation). The situation model emerges from successful integration cycles.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Provides the mechanistic account of why the received view fails: poor situation models result from inadequate integration cycles, not inadequate construction — and integration is dependent on prior knowledge, not decoding skill.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Elaborative-Inference]], [[Semantic-Memory]]

#### 51. [TENSION] Decoding-First vs. Comprehension-Strategy-First Instruction *(Line 743)*

> [!tension] Decoding-First vs. Comprehension-Strategy-First Instruction
> **Position A (Thesis):** Reading instruction should focus first on decoding, because decoding is the binding constraint and no comprehension strategy can compensate for inadequate word recognition.
> 
> **Position B (Challenge):** Comprehension strategy instruction — particularly inference-generation and situation-model monitoring — should be introduced in parallel with decoding instruction, because waiting for decoding automaticity before teaching comprehension strategies delays the development of situation model construction skills.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The empirical evidence supports a conditional synthesis: for early readers with genuine decoding difficulties, decoding-first instruction is correct. For readers with adequate (even if not perfectly automatized) decoding, comprehension strategy instruction produces significant gains independently of decoding.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** Instructional sequencing decisions affect millions of readers; getting the sequence wrong by extending decoding-first instruction beyond the stage where decoding is the binding constraint is one mechanism by which the Matthew Effect is perpetuated.
> 
> **This Report's Resolution:** The reconstructed understanding partially dissolves this tension by distinguishing developmental stages: decoding-first is correct when decoding is the binding constraint; situation-model strategy instruction should begin once decoding no longer constrains comprehension.

#### 52. [TENSION] Active Inference Generation vs. Minimal Inference Hypothesis *(Line 754)*

> [!tension] Active Inference Generation vs. Minimal Inference Hypothesis
> **Position A:** Skilled comprehension requires active, extensive inference generation during reading — readers should spontaneously generate elaborative, predictive, and causal inferences as they go.
> 
> **Position B (McKoon & Ratcliff, 1992):** Readers generate only the minimal inferences required to maintain local coherence; global elaborative inferences are generated retrospectively during recall rather than on-line during reading.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** Genuinely contested. The evidence supports a position between the two extremes: on-line bridging inferences (necessary for local coherence) are generated automatically; elaborative inferences are generated strategically by skilled readers and more rarely by less skilled ones.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** The degree to which inference generation can and should be explicit versus automatic shapes the practical recommendations that follow from the reconstructed understanding.
> 
> **This Report's Resolution:** The reconstruction takes the middle position: some inference generation is automatic and should be (local coherence inferences); situation-model-level inferences are strategically deployed and are the primary target for comprehension strategy development.

#### 53. [TENSION] Prior Knowledge as Enabling vs. Prior Knowledge as Prerequisite *(Line 765)*

> [!tension] Prior Knowledge as Enabling vs. Prior Knowledge as Prerequisite
> **Position A:** Prior knowledge enables better comprehension by providing schemas that facilitate inference — it is a helpful accelerant.
> 
> **Position B (this report's reconstruction):** Prior knowledge is constitutive of comprehension — without the relevant schemas, the inferential operations required for situation model construction cannot be executed, making knowledge not just helpful but necessary at some threshold.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The evidence strongly supports Position B for knowledge-poor readers facing knowledge-demanding texts; the constitutive claim is supported by studies showing that verbal ability cannot compensate for topic knowledge deficits.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** If prior knowledge is enabling, the practical recommendation is to read carefully; if it is constitutive, the practical recommendation is to build background knowledge *before* reading demanding texts, not just during.
> 
> **This Report's Resolution:** The constitutive claim survives scrutiny, with the qualification that the threshold varies by text: some texts can be adequately comprehended with thinner prior knowledge, while others have a knowledge-floor beneath which situation model construction breaks down regardless of reading strategy.

#### 54. [OPEN-QUESTION] What Is the Minimal Viable Situation Model? *(Line 776)*

> [!open-question] What Is the Minimal Viable Situation Model?
> The dialectical process established that situation model construction is necessary for genuine comprehension but left open the question of how *complete* a situation model must be to count as adequate. Readers do not represent all five Event Indexing dimensions equally, and practical comprehension does not require perfect integration of every dimension. What is the threshold of situational coherence above which comprehension is "good enough" for different purposes? This is unanswered and matters for practical guidance about when to shift from active situation-model monitoring to productive engagement with the next passage.

#### 55. [DEBATE] The Generalizability of the Specific Comprehender Research *(Line 779)*

> [!debate] The Generalizability of the Specific Comprehender Research
> The specific comprehension difficulty literature is primarily based on children and adolescents. It is less clear whether the same clean dissociation between decoding and situation model construction is as prevalent in adult readers, or whether adult-typical reading failures show a different profile. Some researchers argue that adult poor comprehenders typically show both decoding limitations and inference generation failures, making the dissociation less clean. This dispute matters for whether the specific comprehender findings can be straightforwardly generalized to adult self-directed learners.

#### 69. [PROTOCOL] The Situation-Model Construction Protocol (Per Reading Session) *(Line 908)*

> [!protocol] The Situation-Model Construction Protocol (Per Reading Session)
> **Context:** Use when reading any complex, non-trivial text where genuine understanding (not merely recognition) is the goal. Particularly important when reading material that will need to be applied, discussed, or integrated with other knowledge.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 
> **Before Reading (2-5 minutes):**
> 1. Preview structure (headings, intro paragraph, concluding paragraph) to build skeletal situation model
> 2. Generate 3-5 questions from headings: convert "The Mechanism of X" to "What is the mechanism of X and why does it work that way?"
> 3. State current belief in 1-2 sentences: "I currently believe that X because Y." This is the anchor against which updates will be measured.
> 
> **During Reading (applied continuously):**
> 4. After every section: state in one sentence what the section contributed to the situation model — not what it *said* but what it *means for the described world*
> 5. When confusion occurs: name the dimension of the confusion — is it temporal, spatial, causal, intentional, or entity? This targets re-reading precisely
> 6. Do NOT annotate for fluency; annotate only when (a) your situation model is being updated, (b) a question arises that you cannot answer, (c) a cross-domain connection occurs, (d) you disagree with a claim
> 7. Every 10-15 minutes: close or look away from the text and state in 2-3 sentences: "The situation this text describes is... The key mechanism is... The current state of affairs is..."
> 
> **After Reading (5-10 minutes):**
> 8. Close the text; reconstruct the situation model in writing or speech: who, what, why, how, when/where
> 9. Identify which of the five dimensions you can represent clearly and which are vague — vague dimensions indicate where the model is thin
> 10. Complete the application prompt: "Based on this, I would expect X to be true in my own experience/domain. I could test this by Y."
> 
> **24-48 Hours Later:**
> 11. Answer the questions generated in Step 2 without the text
> 12. Check answers against annotations; gaps identify where the situation model degraded during forgetting
> 
> **Success Criteria:** You can explain the text to someone who hasn't read it in terms of the *situation* it describes (not just what it says); you can answer novel application questions; you can identify what surprised you and why.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls (post-dialectical awareness):**
> - Feeling like you understand (fluency) without testing whether you do (model reconstruction)
> - Annotating for attention rather than for model updates
> - Skipping Step 3 (stating prior belief) and thereby losing the before-after comparison
> - Monitoring local clarity exclusively ("did I understand that sentence?") instead of global coherence ("does my situation model account for this?")

> **Q:** What is the Construction-Integration model's account of why poor situation models form?
> 
> **A:** In the CI model, comprehension proceeds through cycling construction phases (building a network of propositions from text plus activated prior knowledge) and integration phases (refining the network through spreading activation to produce coherence). Poor situation models form when the integration phase fails — when the spreading-activation convergence that should produce a coherent situation model does not reach a coherent outcome. This failure is most commonly caused by inadequate prior knowledge (insufficient integration constraints) or failure to generate the elaborative inferences that bridge propositions to a world-representation.
