> [!key-claim] The Core Comparative Claim

#### 3. [FRAMEWORK-PROFILE] Self-Determination Theory — Edward Deci & Richard Ryan (1985–2017) *(Line 160)*

> [!framework-profile] Self-Determination Theory — Edward Deci & Richard Ryan (1985–2017)
> **Core claim**: Human beings are active, growth-oriented organisms who naturally tend toward psychological integration, competence development, and social participation. This natural tendency is supported or undermined by the degree to which the social environment satisfies three [[Basic-Psychological-Needs]]: [[Autonomy-Need|autonomy]] (the experience of volitional causation of one's actions), [[Competence-Need|competence]] (the experience of effective engagement with challenging activities), and [[Relatedness-Need|relatedness]] (the experience of meaningful connection with others). When these needs are satisfied, people exhibit autonomous motivation, psychological well-being, deep engagement, and integrative development. When they are frustrated or thwarted, people exhibit controlled motivation, psychological ill-being, and developmental rigidity.
> 
> **How it works**: SDT is not a single theory but a family of six related mini-theories. [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]] (CET) explains how external events (rewards, deadlines, feedback) affect [[Intrinsic-Motivation|Intrinsic Motivation]] by influencing perceived autonomy and competence. [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]] (OIT) explains how initially external regulations become progressively internalised along the [[Internalization-Continuum|internalization continuum]] from [[External-Regulation|external regulation]] → [[Introjected-Regulation|introjected regulation]] → [[Identified-Regulation|identified regulation]] → [[Integrated-Regulation|integrated regulation]] → intrinsic motivation. [[Causality-Orientations-Theory|Causality Orientations Theory]] describes stable individual differences in tendency toward autonomy-oriented, control-oriented, or [[Amotivation|amotivated]] processing of environmental cues. Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT) specifies the need satisfaction mechanisms in detail. Relatedness Motivation Theory (RMT) addresses quality of relationships, and Goal Contents Theory (GCT) addresses the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic aspiration content.
> 
> **Distinctive contribution**: What SDT uniquely provides, and what no SRL framework replicates, is a *qualitative* theory of motivation — one that distinguishes not just high versus low motivation but the experiential and developmental quality of different motivational forms. A student can be highly motivated (high effort, persistence) under controlled regulation (anxiety about failure, desire for approval) or under autonomous regulation (genuine interest, personal meaning). SDT's central empirical finding is that these motivational qualities, despite sometimes producing similar short-term effort levels, have dramatically different long-term consequences for learning depth, creativity, well-being, and dispositional growth.
> 
> **Evidence base**: Four decades of research across educational, clinical, work, health, and sport contexts. The SDT framework is supported by experimental studies (controlled vs. autonomous condition manipulations), large-scale survey research using instruments like the [[MSLQ]], and cross-cultural replication demonstrating need universality. The undermining effect of controlling rewards on intrinsic motivation has been meta-analytically confirmed (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999; over 100 studies). Need satisfaction has been prospectively associated with well-being, vitality, and learning quality across cultural contexts.
> 
> **When it shines**: SDT is most powerful when the question is about the *quality* of motivation rather than its quantity — why a highly motivated student experiences learning as draining rather than vitalising, why reward systems produce compliance without genuine engagement, why autonomy-supportive environments produce learning depth advantages that persist after the environment changes.
> 
> **Known limitations**: SDT is primarily a theory of motivational dynamics, not of cognitive processes. It provides limited guidance on the specific metacognitive strategies through which autonomous motivation is converted into skilled performance. The three-need model, while empirically robust, has been challenged on cultural grounds (collectivist cultures may weight relatedness differently). The [[Undermining-Effect]] — while meta-analytically confirmed — applies primarily to initially intrinsically motivated activities; its boundaries remain theoretically debated.

#### 4. [FRAMEWORK-PROFILE] Zimmerman's Cyclical SRL Model — Barry Zimmerman (2000–2002) *(Line 179)*

> [!framework-profile] Zimmerman's Cyclical SRL Model — Barry Zimmerman (2000–2002)
> **Core claim**: Academic learning is most effective when learners proactively manage their own cognitive processes in service of personally set goals. [[Self-Regulated Learning — SRL|Self-regulated learning]] is not simply a cognitive or behavioural trait but a cyclical, self-directive process organised around three recursive phases: [[Forethought-Phase|forethought]], [[Performance-Phase|performance]], and [[Self-Reflection-Phase|self-reflection]]. Each phase influences the next, creating a developmental spiral through which strategies, self-beliefs, and goal representations are progressively refined.
> 
> **How it works**: In the *forethought phase*, learners engage in task analysis (goal-setting, strategic planning) and motivational belief activation (activating self-efficacy perceptions, outcome expectations, goal orientation, and intrinsic interest). In the *performance phase*, learners engage in self-control (implementing planned strategies — self-instruction, imagery, attention focusing, task strategies) and self-observation (monitoring outcomes through self-recording and self-experimentation). In the *self-reflection phase*, learners engage in self-judgment (evaluating performance against standards, attributing causes to outcomes) and self-reaction (drawing adaptive or defensive inferences about strategy adjustment, setting modified goals). The cycle recurs continuously, creating a feedback loop that either improves regulatory sophistication over time or, in its absence, reinforces ineffective patterns.
> 
> **Distinctive contribution**: Zimmerman's framework uniquely provides a *processual architecture* for self-regulation — a temporal map of how regulatory episodes unfold, enabling both fine-grained analysis of where regulation breaks down and targeted intervention design. No other major SRL framework provides this phase-level temporal granularity as clearly. The cyclical structure also captures the developmental aspect of self-regulation: expert learners have well-developed forethought routines, sensitive performance monitoring, and sophisticated self-reflection practices that novice learners lack.
> 
> **Evidence base**: Extensive research on expert-novice differences in academic, athletic, musical, and writing contexts. Zimmerman's research with expert versus novice students in academic domains demonstrates that experts show more extensive forethought (planning, goal-setting, strategic planning), more active monitoring during performance, and more sophisticated causal attribution in self-reflection. The SRLIS (Self-Regulated Learning Interview Schedule) has been used across numerous educational domains. Studies of prodigy development in domains like chess, music, and athletics converge on similar phase structures.
> 
> **When it shines**: Zimmerman's model is most useful when the question is about the *process quality* of a learner's self-regulatory episodes — diagnosing where in the forethought-performance-reflection cycle a learner is failing and designing targeted process interventions. It is especially valuable in analysing deliberate practice, where the cyclical structure maps directly onto the structure of effective practice sessions.
> 
> **Known limitations**: Zimmerman's model has been criticised for underspecifying the motivational dynamics within phases. While it acknowledges that goal orientation and self-efficacy activate during forethought, it does not theorise *why* some learners have access to functional motivational beliefs and others do not — that is, it is descriptively rich but aetiologically limited regarding motivational genesis. The model also treats the three phases somewhat abstractly, without a detailed account of the cognitive mechanisms involved within phases.

#### 5. [FRAMEWORK-PROFILE] Pintrich's Integrative SRL Framework — Paul Pintrich (2000–2004) *(Line 196)*

> [!framework-profile] Pintrich's Integrative SRL Framework — Paul Pintrich (2000–2004)
> **Core claim**: Self-regulated learning is a process by which learners actively and constructively set goals for their learning and attempt to monitor, regulate, and control their cognition, motivation, behaviour, and context, guided by and constrained by their goals and the contextual features of the environment. Unlike frameworks that treat motivation and cognition separately, Pintrich explicitly theorises their joint operation across four regulatory areas (cognition, motivation/affect, behaviour, context) and four regulatory phases (forethought/planning, monitoring, control, reflection/evaluation).
> 
> **How it works**: The framework maps sixteen cells in a 4×4 matrix. Learners regulate their *cognition* through metacognitive planning (setting learning goals, activating prior knowledge) and monitoring (comprehension checking), control (applying cognitive and metacognitive strategies), and evaluation (judging learning outcomes). They regulate their *motivation/affect* through goal adoption (choosing mastery or performance orientations), monitoring (awareness of anxiety, boredom, interest), control (effort management, self-efficacy maintenance), and evaluation (attributional processes). They regulate their *behaviour* through time management, effort regulation, and help-seeking. They regulate their *context* through environment management and task selection. The [[MSLQ]] (Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire), developed largely by Pintrich, operationalises these components.
> 
> **Distinctive contribution**: Pintrich's unique contribution is the systematic treatment of *motivational self-regulation* — the idea that learners not only regulate their cognition but actively regulate their motivational states, and that motivation regulation is as important as cognitive regulation for learning quality. This is the conceptual bridge point between SDT and SRL: Pintrich's "motivation/affect" regulatory area acknowledges that goal adoption, anxiety management, and interest generation are things learners *do*, not just things that happen to them. This anticipates the SDT-SRL interface without fully theorising it.
> 
> **Evidence base**: Extensive research with the [[MSLQ]] across secondary and higher education contexts, spanning cultures and subject domains. Pintrich's work on goal orientation and strategy use demonstrated that mastery goal orientation predicts deep processing strategies, while performance-avoidance orientation predicts surface strategies and disengagement. The four-area framework has been validated through factor-analytic and path-modelling studies.
> 
> **When it shines**: Pintrich's framework is most useful for comprehensive assessment of learning quality across multiple regulatory dimensions simultaneously — it is the "full panel diagnostic" of SRL frameworks, identifying whether difficulties lie in cognitive, motivational, behavioural, or contextual regulatory areas.
> 
> **Known limitations**: The framework's comprehensiveness is also its limitation — at sixteen cells, it risks becoming a taxonomy rather than a theory, cataloguing what happens without explaining the causal dynamics. The motivational theory embedded within it is largely drawn from [[Achievement-Goal-Theory]] and expectancy-value theory, without a systematic account of need satisfaction or internalization dynamics.

#### 6. [FRAMEWORK-PROFILE] Winne & Hadwin's Metacognitive Processing Model — Philip Winne & Allyson Hadwin (1998–2008) *(Line 213)*

> [!framework-profile] Winne & Hadwin's Metacognitive Processing Model — Philip Winne & Allyson Hadwin (1998–2008)
> **Core claim**: Self-regulated learning is fundamentally a cognitive event — specifically, an event of metacognitive monitoring and control operating on information-processing tasks. Learners build internal cognitive conditions (schemas, beliefs, prior knowledge) and external cognitive conditions (notes, resources, environmental structures) that they then deploy in tasks through operations. Metacognitive monitoring generates traces (cognitive products) that serve as feedback for calibration and strategy adjustment. Regulation, on this view, is essentially the management of *cognitive conditions, operations, products, and evaluations* (COPES) — a recursive information-processing cycle.
> 
> **How it works**: The COPES model specifies that self-regulation involves managing four categories of conditions (cognitive and contextual prerequisites), four types of operations (searching, monitoring, assembling, rehearsing), products (the outcomes of cognitive work), and evaluations (comparisons of products against standards). Critically, Winne & Hadwin distinguish *four phases* of SRL: defining the task (comprehending what is required), setting goals and planning (developing a representation of desired outcomes), enacting tactics and strategies (executing the plan), and *metacognitively adapting* studying (updating internal standards based on monitored outcomes). Monitoring quality — the accuracy of learners' judgments about their own cognitive states — is theorised as the linchpin of adaptive regulation.
> 
> **Distinctive contribution**: Winne & Hadwin provide the most detailed cognitive account of *monitoring* in the SRL literature. Their concept of *calibration* — the accuracy of the relationship between monitoring outputs and actual performance — is the theoretical foundation for research on the [[Dunning-Kruger-Effect|Dunning-Kruger phenomenon]], [[Illusion-of-Knowing|illusions of knowing]], and [[Fluency-Illusion|fluency illusions]] in learning. By treating SRL as an information-processing event, they generate the most precise predictions about when regulation will succeed or fail based on the quality of metacognitive information.
> 
> **Evidence base**: Research on metacognitive calibration, trace methodology (using verbal protocols, eye-tracking, log files as evidence of cognitive processes), and computational modelling of SRL. The trace methodology has been influential in identifying the specific cognitive operations involved in regulation that self-report measures cannot capture.
> 
> **When it shines**: Winne & Hadwin's model is most useful when the question is about the *calibration quality* of a learner's metacognitive monitoring — whether they accurately know what they know and don't know, whether their strategy selection is appropriately calibrated to task demands.
> 
> **Known limitations**: The model is motivationally thin. It treats motivation essentially as a cognitive condition (beliefs about task value, self-efficacy) rather than as a distinct psychological system with its own needs-based architecture. This limits its explanatory power for motivation-based regulatory failures — cases where calibration is adequate but motivation quality undermines regulatory enactment.

#### 10. [COMPARATIVE-FINDING] Autonomy: Need vs. Achievement vs. Cognitive Property *(Line 260)*

> [!comparative-finding] Autonomy: Need vs. Achievement vs. Cognitive Property
> **Type**: Complementarity
> 
> The four frameworks are not competing in their accounts of autonomy — they are describing it at different levels of analysis. SDT's autonomy need describes the *motivational substrate* that makes willing self-direction possible. Zimmerman's self-direction describes the *process architecture* through which that willingness is expressed. Winne & Hadwin's cognitive autonomy describes the *information-processing signature* of self-direction. These three levels (motivational, processual, cognitive-informational) are compatible; understanding learning failure requires diagnosing at which level the deficit lies. A learner may have autonomy-supportive conditions (SDT level satisfied) but lack the process architecture to enact self-direction (Zimmerman level deficient). Or they may have the process repertoire but not the metacognitive accuracy to deploy it effectively (Winne level deficient). The frameworks are not rivals here — they are complementary diagnostic lenses.

#### 11. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Impoverishment of Motivation in SRL Models *(Line 275)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Impoverishment of Motivation in SRL Models
> The evidence suggests that SRL frameworks systematically underspecify the motivational architecture of self-regulation — not because motivation is irrelevant to SRL researchers, but because the motivational theories they import (expectancy-value, goal orientation, self-efficacy) are primarily *cognitive* theories of motivation that share the information-processing commitments of the frameworks themselves. SDT's qualitative distinctions — between types of regulation that produce different phenomenological and developmental outcomes despite similar effort levels — are systematically invisible to frameworks organised around cognitive constructs. This is not a matter of taste; it has empirical consequences. Studies consistently show that autonomous versus controlled motivation predicts learning depth, creative elaboration, and conceptual change beyond what self-efficacy and goal orientation alone predict.

#### 12. [COMPARATIVE-FINDING] The Energy-Architecture Distinction *(Line 284)*

> [!comparative-finding] The Energy-Architecture Distinction
> **Type**: Complementarity
> 
> The most productive structural insight from comparing these frameworks is the distinction between motivational energy and regulatory architecture. SDT theorises the quality and source of the energy; SRL frameworks theorise the architecture through which that energy is converted into skilled performance. This is analogous to distinguishing between a fuel and an engine — you need both, and neither alone predicts how far you will travel. A learner with high-quality autonomous motivation but poor regulatory architecture will have rich motivational resources but be unable to channel them into disciplined practice. A learner with sophisticated regulatory architecture but controlled motivation will be able to enact regulatory cycles but will do so under psychological strain, with consequences for persistence, creativity, and well-being. Only by integrating the two accounts do we get a complete explanation of learning quality.

#### 13. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Regulatory Overhead of Controlled Motivation *(Line 299)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Regulatory Overhead of Controlled Motivation
> The most important insight at the cognitive-motivational interface emerges from placing SDT alongside Winne & Hadwin's processing model. Controlled motivation — regulation from introjection, external pressure, ego-involvement — imposes what might be called *regulatory overhead*: a layer of self-surveillance, performance anxiety, and contingent self-esteem management that competes with the cognitive resources available for actual task engagement. This overhead does not appear in motivational terms alone, nor in cognitive load terms alone; it becomes visible only when SDT's motivational quality analysis is placed alongside a cognitive processing account. A learner operating from introjected regulation must simultaneously process task content, monitor performance relative to self-relevant standards, manage anxiety, and maintain the ego-protective defensive strategies that controlled motivation requires. This regulatory overhead explains why autonomous motivation consistently predicts not just greater effort but qualitatively deeper processing — autonomous motivation allows cognitive resources to be directed fully at the task rather than split between task and self-surveillance.

#### 14. [COMPARATIVE-FINDING] Environment as Context vs. Environment as Object of Regulation *(Line 310)*

> [!comparative-finding] Environment as Context vs. Environment as Object of Regulation
> **Type**: Complementarity
> 
> SDT and SRL frameworks treat the relationship between learner and environment differently in a way that is illuminating rather than contradictory. SDT treats the environment as the *source* of need-satisfying or need-frustrating conditions — as the context that shapes motivational quality. SRL frameworks, especially Zimmerman's and Pintrich's, treat the environment as an *object of regulation* — as something that skilled learners actively modify to support their own learning. Together, these perspectives suggest a developmental trajectory: initially, learners depend on external environments to provide autonomy support (SDT level); as regulatory sophistication develops, learners develop the capacity to create their own need-supportive environments even when external conditions are less than ideal (SRL level). This developmental integration is a genuinely important practical insight that neither framework produces independently.

#### 16. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Internalization-Regulation Isomorphism *(Line 335)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Internalization-Regulation Isomorphism
> The deepest structural complementarity between SDT and SRL frameworks is not just that they cover different territories but that they describe the *same developmental transformation* from different levels. SDT's internalization continuum describes the qualitative motivational shift from "I do this because I must" to "I do this because I genuinely value it" to "I do this because it is part of who I am." Zimmerman's SRL model describes the developmental shift from "I need others to monitor and direct my learning" to "I can monitor and direct my own learning" to "I routinely and spontaneously monitor and direct my learning in sophisticated ways." These are the *same* developmental event viewed through different lenses: the development from heteronomy to autonomy in both motivational and cognitive-regulatory terms. The isomorphism suggests that there may be a single developmental process — call it *self-determination in learning* — that SDT captures at the motivational level and SRL frameworks capture at the cognitive-processual level.

#### 17. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Effortlessness-Effort Paradox *(Line 348)*

> [!tension-identified] The Effortlessness-Effort Paradox
> SDT's ideal is the effortlessly absorbed intrinsically motivated learner; Zimmerman's ideal is the effortfully and systematically self-regulating deliberate practitioner. These ideals are not identical, and their coexistence in the "ideal learner" is theoretically underdeveloped. One resolution: intrinsic motivation describes the *motivational quality* of engagement (volitional, non-pressured, interest-driven) while deliberate practice describes the *cognitive quality* of learning activity (systematically structured, feedback-rich, goal-directed). A learner could be intrinsically motivated (not pressured, genuinely interested) while engaging in deliberate, effortful practice. But the phenomenology differs from the "flow" ideal SDT typically invokes. This conflict is genuine and unresolved.

#### 18. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] Universal Needs, Variable Regulation *(Line 357)*

> [!tension-identified] Universal Needs, Variable Regulation
> SDT's universalism and SRL's implicit acknowledgment of cultural variability in regulatory strategies create a tension with practical implications. If need satisfaction is universal but the forms through which regulation is culturally organised are variable, then the same need-satisfying environment may need to look quite different across cultural contexts to produce equivalent regulatory quality. SDT acknowledges cultural variation in the *forms* of need satisfaction but has been challenged on whether even the basic need categories apply universally. SRL's variability evidence suggests the regulatory architecture is more culturally embedded than SDT's universalist metatheory implies.

#### 19. [COMPARATIVE-FINDING] Mastery Goals: Convergence Across Theoretical Routes *(Line 368)*

> [!comparative-finding] Mastery Goals: Convergence Across Theoretical Routes
> **Type**: Convergence
> 
> SDT via goal contents analysis, Zimmerman via deliberate practice research, Pintrich via [[Achievement-Goal-Theory]] integration, and Winne & Hadwin via standard-setting theory all converge on the recommendation that learners who orient toward mastery (learning, improvement, deep understanding) rather than performance (outperforming others, demonstrating ability) show superior long-term learning outcomes. This convergence from frameworks with different theoretical foundations, using different methods, and measuring different outcomes dramatically increases confidence in the recommendation. It is one of the most robust findings in educational psychology.

#### 20. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Robustness of Autonomy Support *(Line 381)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Robustness of Autonomy Support
> The convergent evidence across all four theoretical traditions — each independently concluding that autonomy-supportive environments produce superior learning outcomes — is the strongest empirical signal in this entire comparison. Whether the mechanism is need satisfaction (SDT), regulatory capacity development (Zimmerman), goal adoption (Pintrich), or internal standard quality (Winne & Hadwin), the practical upshot is identical: environments that provide structure while respecting learner agency, that offer genuine rationale rather than coercive pressure, and that acknowledge learners' feelings and perspectives produce better learning than controlling, pressuring environments.

#### 22. [BEST-PRACTICE] SDT as Environmental Diagnosis *(Line 405)*

> [!best-practice] SDT as Environmental Diagnosis
> SDT is the strongest diagnostic lens for identifying how environmental features are affecting motivational quality. Before designing any learning environment change, run an SDT audit: Which features of the current environment are autonomy-thwarting (controlling language, external pressure, reward contingencies that undermine interest)? Which undermine competence (unclear expectations, insufficiently challenging tasks, negative or ego-involving feedback)? Which undermine relatedness (social comparison climates, lack of genuine teacher involvement, competitive rather than cooperative structures)? Addressing these deficits is motivational first aid — necessary before any SRL intervention can take hold.

#### 23. [WARNING] Common Navigation Errors *(Line 424)*

> [!warning] Common Navigation Errors
> **Error 1 — Treating motivational and regulatory failures as the same**: A student who lacks autonomous motivation requires fundamentally different intervention than a student with autonomous motivation but poor regulatory strategies. Applying strategy instruction to a motivationally controlled student adds cognitive burden without addressing the root condition. Applying motivational support to a learner with poor regulatory architecture addresses the energy quality without providing the engine. Diagnosis must precede intervention.
> 
> **Error 2 — Using SDT to explain *all* learning failure**: SDT is powerful but is not a complete theory of learning failure. A learner with high autonomous motivation can still fail to learn if their metacognitive monitoring is poorly calibrated (Winne & Hadwin's domain) or if their regulatory phase architecture is underdeveloped (Zimmerman's domain). SDT explains why motivated people sometimes fail to sustain effort; it does not explain why they sometimes fail to deploy effective strategies even when motivated.

#### 24. [ASK-YOURSELF-THIS] Navigational Application *(Line 431)*

> [!ask-yourself-this] Navigational Application
> Think of a learning challenge you are currently facing — something you want to master but are struggling with. Apply the SDT lens: Is the difficulty primarily motivational (lacking autonomous engagement, experiencing need frustration)? Apply the Zimmerman lens: Is the difficulty processual (poor forethought planning, inadequate monitoring, superficial self-reflection)? Apply Winne & Hadwin's lens: Is the difficulty calibration-based (overconfident in areas you don't know, inadequate sensitivity to monitoring signals)? Different diagnoses lead to different interventions — and conflating them leads to ineffective help-giving.

#### 25. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Sport Psychology: Motivation-Practice Integration *(Line 434)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Sport Psychology: Motivation-Practice Integration
> In sport psychology, the SDT-SRL interface recurs with remarkable structural similarity. Research on athlete development distinguishes between intrinsic motivation for sport (SDT level) and deliberate practice engagement quality (SRL level). Athletes with high intrinsic motivation but poor deliberate practice structure improve slowly; athletes with sophisticated practice habits but controlled motivation (training only for external validation) show poorer long-term resilience, burnout risk, and transfer of skills. The field has developed integrated frameworks (Vallerand's Hierarchical Motivation Model; Côté's developmental model) that explicitly attempt what SDT and SRL have not yet achieved together in educational contexts: a unified account of motivational quality and regulatory architecture in skilled performance development. The parallel is not superficial — it is structurally identical.

#### 27. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Motivational-Regulatory Architecture of Learning *(Line 453)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Motivational-Regulatory Architecture of Learning
> The deepest integrative insight from placing SDT alongside the major SRL frameworks is this: *quality of learning is the product of two jointly necessary systems operating in coordination — a motivational system that determines the quality and source of the energy available for learning, and a regulatory system that determines how efficiently and effectively that energy is converted into skilled cognitive performance*. SDT theorises the motivational system; SRL frameworks theorise the regulatory system. Neither is sufficient alone. A learner with high-quality autonomous motivation but underdeveloped regulatory architecture will have rich motivational resources that cannot be efficiently channelled into disciplined, progressively calibrated learning. A learner with sophisticated regulatory architecture but controlled motivation will be able to execute learning episodes under psychological strain, with consequences for creativity, depth, and long-term engagement that gradually erode even the motivational resources they began with. The fully functioning learner — what Deci & Ryan call the "fully functioning person" and what Zimmerman calls the "expert self-regulator" — is characterised by the integration of both: the motivational vitality that comes from need satisfaction providing the energy substrate, and the regulatory sophistication that ensures that energy is converted into genuine learning rather than mere performance or compliance.

#### 28. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] Internalization as the Developmental Bridge Between Frameworks *(Line 458)*

> [!original-synthesis] Internalization as the Developmental Bridge Between Frameworks
> A second integrative synthesis emerges from examining the developmental trajectories described by both traditions. SDT's internalization continuum describes the developmental movement from externally regulated motivation to integrated, autonomous motivation. Zimmerman's SRL development describes the movement from other-regulated (scaffolded, teacher-directed) learning to self-regulated learning. These developmental stories are not independent — they are two aspects of the same developmental transformation. The child who is externally regulated motivationally (doing tasks to avoid punishment or gain rewards) is also characteristically other-regulated cognitively (dependent on teacher monitoring, external feedback, scaffolded task structures). The mature self-determined learner is both autonomously motivated (doing tasks for personally meaningful reasons) and self-regulated (managing their own cognitive processes without external scaffolding). Internalization in SDT's sense — the progressive ownership of external values and regulations — is the motivational side of the same developmental coin whose cognitive side is the gradual development of internal regulatory capacity. A complete theory of learning development needs both accounts.

#### 29. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Therapeutic Change → Learning Change: A Structural Parallel *(Line 483)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Therapeutic Change → Learning Change: A Structural Parallel
> In psychotherapy, autonomous motivation for change (SDT level) predicts therapeutic engagement and outcome better than techniques alone; but autonomous motivation without skill acquisition (the SRL analogue) is equally insufficient. The most effective therapeutic formats — [[Motivational-Interviewing|motivational interviewing]] combined with evidence-based skill training — are precisely those that address both the motivational quality dimension (building autonomous motivation for change) and the regulatory architecture dimension (building specific regulatory skills for the desired behaviour change). This is the therapeutic equivalent of the SDT-SRL integration this report has advocated for learning contexts. The structural parallel is exact: the integrated intervention theory this report implies for learning is already being implemented in clinical psychology.

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Autonomous Motivation (SDT) *(Line 544)*

> [!definition] Autonomous Motivation (SDT)
> Motivation arising from volitional endorsement of one's actions, where the perceived locus of causality is internal. Includes intrinsic motivation (engaging for inherent interest/enjoyment) and well-internalised identified and integrated regulation. Distinguished from controlled motivation by the experience of willingness rather than pressure. Predicts deeper learning, greater creativity, more persistence under difficulty, and better well-being. *Note: SRL frameworks use the term "self-regulation" for the cognitive side of what SDT calls "autonomous motivation" — these terms overlap but are not synonymous.*

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Controlled Motivation (SDT) *(Line 547)*

> [!definition] Controlled Motivation (SDT)
> Motivation arising from external or introjected pressures — rewards, deadlines, approval-seeking, ego-involvement, guilt, shame. The perceived locus of causality is external (or internal but pressuring). Produces compliance and effort but degrades learning quality, creativity, and well-being under pressure conditions. *See also: [[Undermining-Effect]], [[Introjected-Regulation]], [[External-Regulation]].*

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Self-Regulated Learning / SRL *(Line 550)*

> [!definition] Self-Regulated Learning / SRL
> The proactive, metacognitively-directed management of one's own cognitive, motivational, behavioural, and contextual processes in service of self-set learning goals. SRL is distinguished from simple learning effort by its cyclical, self-directed quality — learners monitor and regulate their own processes rather than simply responding to external instruction. *Note: SRL frameworks describe the cognitive architecture of regulation; SDT describes the motivational quality of its enactment.*

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Forethought Phase (Zimmerman) *(Line 553)*

> [!definition] Forethought Phase (Zimmerman)
> The first of three phases in Zimmerman's cyclical SRL model, occurring before a learning task. Involves task analysis (goal-setting, strategic planning) and motivational belief activation (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, goal orientation). Quality of forethought determines the quality of subsequent performance and self-reflection phases. *SDT connection: autonomous motivation is the motivational substrate that enables productive forethought; controlled motivation or amotivation produces defensive or avoidant forethought.*

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs (SDT) *(Line 556)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs (SDT)
> Three universal organismic requirements whose satisfaction supports psychological well-being, autonomous motivation, and integrative development: [[Autonomy-Need|autonomy]] (experienced volitional causation), [[Competence-Need|competence]] (felt effectiveness in challenge), and [[Relatedness-Need|relatedness]] (experienced meaningful connection). Distinct from cultural preferences or idiosyncratic desires — these are theorised as universal requirements, analogous to biological needs, with consistent cross-cultural support.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Monitoring (Winne & Hadwin) *(Line 559)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Monitoring (Winne & Hadwin)
> The ongoing process of tracking one's own cognitive states during learning — assessing comprehension, judging learning progress, comparing performance against goals. Quality of monitoring determines calibration accuracy (whether one accurately knows what one knows) and thus the quality of adaptive regulatory responses. *SDT connection: competence-need frustration degrades monitoring quality by inducing ego-protective rather than accuracy-seeking processing.*

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Internalization (SDT) *(Line 562)*

> [!definition] Internalization (SDT)
> The developmental process by which external regulations are progressively transformed into personally owned values, identities, and motivational orientations. Moves through external → introjected → identified → integrated regulation, with intrinsic motivation as an independent endpoint. *SRL parallel: the developmental progression from other-regulated to self-regulated learning is the cognitive-processual side of the same developmental event.*

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Regulatory Overhead *(Line 565)*

> [!definition] Regulatory Overhead
> Original analytical concept developed in this report. The additional cognitive-affective burden imposed by controlled motivation (specifically introjected regulation) — the self-surveillance, performance anxiety, and contingent self-esteem management that compete with cognitive resources available for task engagement. Explains why autonomous motivation predicts not just greater effort but qualitatively deeper and more creative processing.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Self-Determined Self-Regulation *(Line 568)*

> [!definition] Self-Determined Self-Regulation
> Integrative concept developed in this report. The state in which both motivational quality (autonomous, need-satisfying — SDT level) and regulatory architecture (sophisticated, cyclical, well-calibrated — SRL level) are jointly optimal. Characterised by deliberate practice that is phenomenologically experienced as vitalising rather than depleting. Neither high autonomous motivation nor sophisticated SRL alone produces this state; it requires their integration.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] MSLQ (Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire) *(Line 571)*

> [!definition] MSLQ (Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire)
> Pintrich's major assessment instrument for self-regulated learning. Measures both motivational components (value, expectancy, affect) and cognitive/metacognitive strategy use across multiple subscales. Used extensively in research on SDT-SRL integration studies. The motivational subscales partially operationalise SDT constructs; the cognitive subscales operationalise Zimmerman's and Pintrich's process components.

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Calibration (Winne & Hadwin) *(Line 574)*

> [!definition] Calibration (Winne & Hadwin)
> The accuracy of the relationship between a learner's online monitoring outputs (judgments about their own learning progress) and actual performance outcomes. Well-calibrated learners know what they know and don't know; poorly calibrated learners may be overconfident (producing [[Fluency-Illusion|fluency illusions]], [[Illusion-of-Knowing|illusions of knowing]]) or underconfident. *SDT connection: competence need satisfaction supports accurate calibration; competence need frustration tends to produce defensive misattribution.*
