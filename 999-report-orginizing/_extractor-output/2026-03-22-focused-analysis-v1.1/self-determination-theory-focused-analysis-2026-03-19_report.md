#### 4. [DEFINITION] [[Amotivation]] *(Line 204)*

> [!definition] [[Amotivation]]
> The absence of intentional self-regulation — neither intrinsically nor extrinsically motivated. The person either does not see a connection between their actions and outcomes (helplessness), does not value the activity, or does not feel competent to perform it. Amotivation is associated with the worst psychological outcomes of any regulatory position, including greater wellbeing costs than even external regulation. It is not passive; it involves active disengagement that requires energy to sustain.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] [[External-Regulation]] *(Line 207)*

> [!definition] [[External-Regulation]]
> Behavior regulated by external contingencies — rewards, punishments, deadlines, surveillance. The [[Perceived-Internal-Locus-of-Causality|locus of causality]] is entirely external; the person acts to obtain or avoid consequences that originate outside the self. This is the most heteronomous form of intentional regulation. Crucially, external regulation does not cease when the contingency is removed — but behavior typically does, because the regulatory structure has not been internalized at all.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] [[Introjected-Regulation]] *(Line 210)*

> [!definition] [[Introjected-Regulation]]
> Behavior regulated by partially internalized external constraints, now represented *within* the person as ego-involving demands. The person acts to avoid shame, protect [[Self-Esteem|self-esteem]], or maintain conditional approval — but these demands are experienced as pressures, not as genuine expressions of the self. Introjection is the theoretical lynchpin of the internalization analysis: it is the site where external regulation enters the person but does not become part of the self. It is internal in location but external in phenomenological character.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] [[Identified-Regulation]] *(Line 213)*

> [!definition] [[Identified-Regulation]]
> Behavior regulated by conscious valuing — the person has endorsed the goal or activity as personally important, even if it is not intrinsically enjoyable. The [[Perceived-Internal-Locus-of-Causality|locus of causality]] has shifted inward: the person acts because *they* see the value, not because an external agent rewards them for it or because they fear internal sanction. Identified regulation is typically associated with substantially better outcomes than introjection — greater persistence, higher quality engagement, and better psychological wellbeing.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] [[Integrated-Regulation]] *(Line 216)*

> [!definition] [[Integrated-Regulation]]
> The most autonomous form of extrinsic motivation. The person has assimilated the identified value into a coherent sense of self — the activity is pursued not merely because it is valued in isolation but because it coheres with the person's broader values, goals, and sense of identity. Integration differs from identification in its *systemic* character: identified regulation can be isolated and context-specific, while integrated regulation is woven into the self-concept as a whole. Integration is associated with the best outcomes short of intrinsic motivation and, unlike intrinsic motivation, can apply to activities one does not find inherently enjoyable.

#### 9. [DEFINITION] [[Intrinsic-Motivation]] *(Line 219)*

> [!definition] [[Intrinsic-Motivation]]
> Engagement driven by inherent satisfaction in the activity itself — the activity is its own reward. This is the form of motivation that first drove Deci and Ryan's research program, and it remains the paradigm case of self-determined motivation. However, [[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory]] established that intrinsic motivation is fragile: it can be undermined by the wrong kinds of external events. This fragility is central to understanding the practical implications of the continuum.

#### 10. [KEY-CLAIM] Two Underlying Dimensions *(Line 224)*

> [!key-claim] Two Underlying Dimensions
> Regulatory style is best understood as varying along two related dimensions: (1) **[[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality]]** — the degree to which the person experiences themselves as the origin versus the pawn of their own behavior; and (2) **degree of integration** — how thoroughly the regulatory structure has been assimilated into the coherent self-system. These two dimensions are correlated but not identical, which is why the [[Autonomous-Motivation-vs.-Controlled-Motivation|autonomous/controlled distinction]] cuts across the continuum at identified regulation rather than at integration.

#### 11. [DEFINITION] [[Basic-Psychological-Needs]] — The Three *(Line 235)*

> [!definition] [[Basic-Psychological-Needs]] — The Three
> *[[Autonomy-Need|Autonomy]]*: The need to experience one's behavior as volitional and self-endorsed — to feel like the author of one's actions rather than their instrument. *[[Competence-Need|Competence]]*: The need to feel effective in interactions with the environment — to experience mastery and growth. *[[Relatedness-Need|Relatedness]]*: The need to feel connected to others, to care and to be cared for within secure relational contexts. These are *psychological* needs in the strict sense: their satisfaction supports growth, integration, and wellbeing; their frustration produces ill-being and defensive processing.

#### 16. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Introjection Is Not a Way Station *(Line 264)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Introjection Is Not a Way Station
> The evidence accumulated over thirty years of SDT research suggests that introjected regulation is not merely a developmental stepping-stone toward identification. For many people in many domains, introjection is a stable regulatory endpoint — and its stability is maintained precisely by the conditions that create it. Controlling environments that produce introjection are self-sustaining systems: the [[Conditional-Regard]] that drives introjection communicates that the person's value is contingent on performance, which makes releasing the introjection feel existentially dangerous. The person is trapped in controlled motivation not because they haven't internalized "enough" but because the conditions for further internalization — specifically, unconditional relatedness support — are absent.

#### 17. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Conditional Regard Paradox in Educational Practice *(Line 273)*

> [!tension-identified] The Conditional Regard Paradox in Educational Practice
> Most educational quality assurance systems that invoke SDT argue for autonomy-supportive environments and intrinsic motivation. Yet most of these same systems use evaluative practices — grading, ranking, competitive assessment — that structurally resemble conditional regard. The tension is genuine: an educational environment cannot simultaneously communicate that students are valued unconditionally as persons *and* that their academic worth is determined by performance on graded assessments. SDT does not resolve this tension; it sharpens it. The practical implication — that meaningful motivational transformation requires changes to evaluative structures, not merely to the interpersonal warmth of the teacher — is one the literature acknowledges but rarely follows through on.

#### 18. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] Frustration Is Not Merely the Absence of Satisfaction *(Line 280)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] Frustration Is Not Merely the Absence of Satisfaction
> The evidence from studies using bidimensional need measures — assessing both satisfaction and frustration independently rather than assuming they are endpoints of a single scale — consistently shows that frustration predicts ill-being outcomes over and above what low satisfaction predicts, and that the two dimensions have different antecedent profiles. [[Need-Thwarting]] environments that actively block need satisfaction produce different psychological outcomes (including more rigid defensive processes, greater [[Controlled-Motivation|controlled motivation]], and more severe ill-being) than environments that are merely indifferent to need support. This finding reconstitutes SDT as a theory not just of flourishing (promoted by need satisfaction) but of damage (produced by need frustration). The [[Satisfaction-Frustration-Asymmetry]] has significant implications for intervention design: need-supportive environments undo the effects of neutral environments, but they may not fully reverse the effects of need-thwarting environments. Damage and deprivation are not the same thing.

#### 19. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Undermining Effect Is a Window into the Internalization Mechanism *(Line 289)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Undermining Effect Is a Window into the Internalization Mechanism
> The undermining effect is often treated in SDT as a standalone finding about the hazards of reward — a practical warning against incentivizing activities people already enjoy. But its deeper theoretical significance is as an existence proof for the claim that motivational regulation is causally affected by perceived locus of causality. The finding does not merely show that rewards can be bad; it shows that people's sense of authorship over their own behavior is psychologically real and has measurable downstream consequences. It establishes that the continuum's dimension of perceived locus of causality is not merely descriptive but explanatory — changes in perceived locus of causality cause changes in subsequent motivation. This is the empirical foundation for claiming that the continuum describes a mechanism, not a taxonomy.

#### 23. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Blocked Tendency Framing Changes the Clinical Interpretation *(Line 315)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Blocked Tendency Framing Changes the Clinical Interpretation
> If internalization is a natural tendency that can be blocked rather than a capacity that must be actively developed, the intervention logic for motivation change is fundamentally different. The therapist, teacher, or organizational designer is not primarily trying to install a motivation or teach a skill — they are primarily trying to *remove the obstacle* that is preventing a natural process from completing itself. This reframing has significant practical implications: it shifts emphasis from incentive design (adding positive contingencies to pull behavior toward desired regulation) to need-support design (removing need-thwarting conditions that prevent the organism's own integrative tendency from operating). The design question is not "how do we motivate this person?" but "what is preventing this person from operating from their own motivational resources?"

#### 24. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Self-Continuity Trap in Introjection *(Line 330)*

> [!tension-identified] The Self-Continuity Trap in Introjection
> SDT implies that introjected regulation can be released through the provision of unconditional regard and autonomy support — and this is empirically supported in intervention studies. But there is a deeper philosophical tension: for many people, the introjected regulation has been operating so long that it is now part of how they *understand themselves*. The student who has always worked hard out of fear of shame may experience that drive as their identity. Releasing the introjection would require not merely removing the conditional regard structure but reorganizing a self-concept that has been built around the introjected regulation. SDT's developmental account does not fully address this self-continuity problem — the theory explains why introjection stabilizes, but it underspecifies the process of dismantling a long-standing introjective structure without threatening the person's sense of who they are.

#### 25. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Integration Is a Form of Schema Restructuring *(Line 349)*

> [!analytical-insight] Integration Is a Form of Schema Restructuring
> The transition from identification to integration closely parallels the process [[Conceptual-Change-Theory]] describes as schema restructuring — not the addition of new information to an existing framework but the reorganization of the framework itself. Identification adds a new node to the value-network; integration rewires the network around it. This parallel suggests that integration, like schema restructuring, is cognitively expensive, relatively rare, and typically triggered by a form of cognitive-motivational disequilibrium — an experience of incoherence between a newly identified value and the existing self-system that demands resolution. The implication is that integration cannot be designed for directly; it can only be designed *toward* by creating the conditions (principally, reflective opportunity and need support) that allow the person to do the restructuring work themselves.

#### 26. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Integration Paradox as a Design Constraint *(Line 358)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Integration Paradox as a Design Constraint
> The Integration Paradox implies a specific constraint for motivation change design: you cannot begin with the internalization sequence. Before an autonomy-supportive environment can do its work of enabling identification and integration, it must first repair the sense of autonomy that controlled environments have eroded. This suggests a two-phase model for motivation intervention: Phase 1 is **autonomy restoration** — creating the relational and environmental conditions in which the person can re-experience choice, perspective acknowledgment, and unconditional regard, rebuilding the experiential foundation that the internalization mechanism requires. Phase 2 is **internalization facilitation** — providing rationale, structure, and challenge within the restored autonomy context. Educational, therapeutic, and organizational interventions that go directly to Phase 2 without attending to Phase 1 will typically produce introjection rather than identification, because the person does not have the experiential autonomy base from which genuine valuing can operate. The Integration Paradox is not a problem to be solved; it is a constraint to be designed around.

#### 27. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Avoidance-Driven Internalization Produces a Different Kind of Regulation *(Line 367)*

> [!analytical-insight] Avoidance-Driven Internalization Produces a Different Kind of Regulation
> The structures internalized through the avoidance pathway in SDT — particularly introjected regulation in its shame-avoidance form — are not merely weaker versions of approach-driven integrated values. They are functionally different objects with different psychological architectures. Approach-driven values, when activated, direct attention toward growth opportunities and engage positive affect systems. Avoidance-driven introjections, when activated, direct attention toward threat detection and engage anxiety systems. The person operating from introjected regulation is not engaging in a paler version of what the person with integrated values does; they are performing a qualitatively different cognitive-motivational operation. This is why the [[Controlled-Motivation|controlled/autonomous]] distinction is functionally more important than position on the continuum: the approach/avoidance distinction cuts across the entire continuum at the controlled/autonomous boundary.

#### 30. [BEST-PRACTICE] Autonomy Restoration Before Internalization Facilitation *(Line 388)*

> [!best-practice] Autonomy Restoration Before Internalization Facilitation
> Educational environments working with students who have been in controlled motivational contexts for extended periods — high-stakes testing environments, authoritarian classrooms, conditional regard from parents — should not begin with the standard SDT toolkit of rationale provision and autonomy support. These students may not have the experiential autonomy base from which genuine identification can operate. The first design priority is unconditional regard and low-stakes engagement: creating the relational context in which the student can re-experience choice without it feeling existentially dangerous. Only then does autonomy-supportive instruction — providing rationale, choice, and challenge — have the motivational soil it requires to produce identification rather than introjection.

#### 31. [WARNING] Neutral Is Not Sufficient When Prior Frustration Has Occurred *(Line 393)*

> [!warning] Neutral Is Not Sufficient When Prior Frustration Has Occurred
> If a student has come from a need-thwarting environment, a merely need-neutral environment will not produce recovery. The asymmetry between frustration and satisfaction means that need-supportive environments are required — actively providing competence support, unconditional relatedness, and autonomy experiences — rather than merely removing the need-thwarting conditions. This has significant implications for differentiated classroom design: students with different need-support histories require different environmental affordances, not just different content.

#### 32. [BEST-PRACTICE] Need-Support as the Therapeutic Precondition *(Line 404)*

> [!best-practice] Need-Support as the Therapeutic Precondition
> [[Relationship-Motivation-Theory]] within SDT identifies the quality of close relationships as a domain governed by the same three basic needs. In therapeutic contexts, the mechanism described in Phase IV implies that therapeutic change is unlikely to proceed through internalization in a therapeutic relationship characterized by conditional regard — even if the conditionality is subtle (the therapist's warmth contingent on client progress, insight, or compliance with the therapeutic model). The therapist's unconditional positive regard is not merely a therapeutic nicety; it is, according to the SDT mechanism, the structural precondition for the client's integration process.

#### 33. [KEY-CLAIM] Limitation 1: The Mechanism Is Underspecified at the Neural Level *(Line 411)*

> [!key-claim] Limitation 1: The Mechanism Is Underspecified at the Neural Level
> SDT's account of internalization is cognitive-motivational, not neuroscientific. The claim that environments "support" or "thwart" the organismic integration tendency is consistent with research on [[Cortico-Striatal-Learning-Loops|cortico-striatal learning loops]] and reward systems, but SDT does not provide a neural mechanism for the phenomenological shift from introjection to identification. This is not a fatal limitation — many psychological accounts operate at a level of description that does not require neural specification — but it does mean that the internalization account cannot be tested neurally in its current form.

#### 34. [KEY-CLAIM] Limitation 2: Cross-Cultural Variability in Need Expression *(Line 414)*

> [!key-claim] Limitation 2: Cross-Cultural Variability in Need Expression
> The universality of the three basic psychological needs has been supported cross-culturally in need-satisfaction surveys, but the expression of autonomy need varies substantially across cultural contexts. In contexts that place high value on relational harmony and role compliance, the experience of "choice" may operate differently than in individualistic Western contexts. SDT researchers (Ryan & Deci, 2003) have addressed this by distinguishing between the universal need for autonomy and the culturally variable ways in which autonomy is expressed and satisfied, but the mechanism account presented here does not fully integrate this cultural variability.

#### 35. [KEY-CLAIM] Limitation 3: The Bidirectionality Question *(Line 417)*

> [!key-claim] Limitation 3: The Bidirectionality Question
> Most SDT internalization research examines the movement from external to autonomous regulation. The movement in the other direction — from integration back to introjection or external regulation — is empirically documented (sustained need frustration can erode previously integrated regulation) but mechanistically underspecified. The developmental account is asymmetric in ways that may not be warranted by the underlying theory.

#### 37. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Internalization Metabolism Model *(Line 441)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Internalization Metabolism Model
> The internalization mechanism described throughout this analysis is most accurately captured by a metabolic metaphor. [[Integration-Metabolism|Integration metabolism]] describes the process by which external regulatory material is broken down and rebuilt into forms that can be incorporated into the organism's own structure. Healthy metabolism requires nutritive input (need support), adequate digestive capacity (sufficient felt autonomy to evaluate and choose), and absence of toxic substances (need thwarting, conditional regard). When the nutritive conditions are adequate, the organism transforms external regulation progressively — from introjection (raw material taken in but undigested) through identification (broken down and partially incorporated) to integration (fully assimilated into the organism's own structure). When the conditions are inadequate, the process stalls or produces pathological byproducts: introjected regulations that are incompletely digested and metabolically burdensome, consuming regulatory energy without being incorporated into the self-system. The metabolic framing has a further advantage: it connects the internalization account to [[Learning-Metabolism|learning metabolism]] and the [[The-Learning-Metabolism-Framework|learning metabolism framework]] in the PKB, suggesting that motivational metabolism and cognitive metabolism operate through structurally analogous processes, both requiring nutritive environmental conditions to proceed to completion.

> [!cross-domain-connection] Principle 1 → Habit Formation (BJ Fogg, Identity-Based Habits)
> The [[Nutritive-Environment-Requirement|Nutritive-Environment Requirement]] operates in [[Habit-Formation]] research. [[Identity-Based-Habits|Identity-based habit formation]] (Clear, 2018) argues that sustainable behavioral change requires the person to first adopt a new identity ("I am a person who runs") before the specific behavior ("I run three times a week") can be stabilized. The structural parallel to SDT is exact: sustainable habits require identified or integrated regulation — the behavior must be experienced as an expression of the self. The analogy holds further: habit environments that are purely contingency-based (reward for compliance) produce behavioral compliance without identity change, the habit-formation equivalent of introjection. The prediction: identity-first approaches to habit change will produce more durable behavior change than incentive-first approaches, particularly after the incentive is removed. *Transfer encoding*: When designing behavior change interventions, check whether the person has a relational or identity basis from which to choose the target behavior. If not, identity/autonomy work precedes behavior design.

#### 41. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Principle 2 → Ecological Systems — Threshold Effects *(Line 499)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Principle 2 → Ecological Systems — Threshold Effects
> The [[Satisfaction-Frustration-Asymmetry]] has a structural analogue in ecological systems theory's threshold concepts. In ecosystem ecology, degradation of a functional system below a threshold can produce a regime shift — a reorganization of the system around a different attractor state — that cannot be reversed by simply restoring the depleted input. A coral reef bleached by thermal stress, for instance, does not recover automatically when temperatures return to normal; it has shifted to a different regime that requires active restoration intervention. The structural parallel: need frustration in SDT moves the motivational system below a threshold beyond which simple need restoration is insufficient. *Transfer encoding*: In any complex adaptive system, check whether apparent "deprivation" has produced a regime shift (qualitative reorganization) rather than merely a temporary deficit. If so, restoration protocols must address the reorganization, not just the input level.

#### 42. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Principle 3 → Schema Theory and Conceptual Change *(Line 502)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Principle 3 → Schema Theory and Conceptual Change
> The Integration Paradox has a precise structural analogue in [[Schema-Theory]] and [[Conceptual-Change-Theory]]. Schema accommodation — the revision of existing cognitive frameworks in response to anomalous data — requires a foundational schema stable enough to recognize the anomaly as anomalous. A student who lacks the foundational schema cannot experience the disequilibration that triggers accommodation; they can only ignore or distort the anomalous input through assimilation. Exactly like SDT's Integration Paradox: the process that is supposed to revise the cognitive structure requires a stable cognitive structure as a prerequisite. *Transfer encoding*: When encountering a learner who does not respond to conceptual change interventions, check whether the prerequisite schema is in place — not merely whether the student has encountered the anomalous information. The integration paradox applies to cognitive as well as motivational development.

#### 43. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Meta-Transfer Observation: All Three Principles Are About the Self as a System *(Line 505)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Meta-Transfer Observation: All Three Principles Are About the Self as a System
> The three abstract principles extracted from this SDT analysis share a structural feature: they all concern the conditions under which a *self-organizing* system successfully internalizes externally sourced material. The Nutritive Environment Requirement says the environment must not corrupt the internalization process with contingency. The Asymmetry of Thwarting says deprivation of system requirements creates damage the system cannot self-repair. The Integration Paradox says internalization requires the internal resources that internalization is supposed to produce. These are all principles about systems that govern themselves — and their common structure suggests that the SDT analysis may be identifying something about self-organizing systems generally, not merely about human motivation. The highest-transferability domain of this analysis is the design of *any* environment intended to support the self-governing transformation of externally imposed structure into internally generated organization.

#### 44. [BEST-PRACTICE] Application Bridge 1: PKB Adoption Sequencing *(Line 508)*

> [!best-practice] Application Bridge 1: PKB Adoption Sequencing
> Applying the Nutritive Environment Requirement and the Integration Paradox to PKB adoption suggests a specific sequencing: do not begin with the structural demands of PKB practice (formatting, linking, metadata). Begin with open-ended, intrinsically motivated capture — anything that builds a felt sense of ownership over the system. Only after the learner has developed an identified relationship to PKB practice (they experience it as their own tool, not an obligation) introduce structural practices. The key adaptation: in PKB design, the "environment" is partially constituted by the system's structural requirements themselves. Overly demanding structural requirements imposed early create an introjection context within the PKB practice itself.

#### 45. [BEST-PRACTICE] Application Bridge 2: Organizational Change Management *(Line 511)*

> [!best-practice] Application Bridge 2: Organizational Change Management
> Applying the two-phase model (autonomy restoration → internalization facilitation) to organizational change suggests that change initiatives requiring genuine value adoption (not merely behavioral compliance) must begin with autonomy-supportive conditions rather than with training and communication. The common failure mode in organizational change — compliance without commitment — is the organizational equivalent of introjection: the new values have been taken in but not integrated, and they will fail to persist once external enforcement is relaxed. The need frustration asymmetry further implies that organizations with histories of need-thwarting management cultures cannot reverse this with merely need-neutral environments; actively need-supportive leadership is required.

#### 48. [DEFINITION] [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]] (Deci & Ryan, 1985) *(Line 558)*

> [!definition] [[Organismic-Integration-Theory]] (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The sub-theory within [[Self-Determination-Theory]] that addresses the process by which external regulations and values are internalized and integrated into the self. OIT posits an organismic tendency toward integration and identifies the environmental conditions that support or undermine the movement from external to autonomous regulation across the internalization continuum.

#### 49. [DEFINITION] [[Internalization-Continuum]] *(Line 561)*

> [!definition] [[Internalization-Continuum]]
> The progression from external regulation through introjection, identification, and integration to intrinsic motivation — not a spectrum of motivation amounts but a spectrum of degrees of integration between a regulatory process and the self-system. Position on the continuum reflects both the phenomenological locus of causality and the degree of structural integration of the regulation into the self.

#### 50. [DEFINITION] [[Introjected-Regulation]] *(Line 564)*

> [!definition] [[Introjected-Regulation]]
> A form of partially internalized regulation in which the person acts to avoid shame, protect self-esteem, or maintain conditional approval. Introjection is internal in location but external in functional character — the person experiences it as pressure rather than volition, making it a form of controlled rather than autonomous motivation. The critical theoretical status of introjection is as the site where internalization stalls when environmental conditions are inadequate.

#### 51. [DEFINITION] [[Identified-Regulation]] *(Line 567)*

> [!definition] [[Identified-Regulation]]
> A form of autonomous motivation in which the person consciously endorses the goal or activity as personally important. Identification represents the critical transition from controlled to autonomous motivation on the continuum; it involves a genuine act of volitional endorsement rather than compliance or ego-defending pressure.

#### 52. [DEFINITION] [[The-Organismic-Dialectical-Perspective]] *(Line 570)*

> [!definition] [[The-Organismic-Dialectical-Perspective]]
> The metatheoretical framework underlying SDT, positing that organisms are naturally active, growth-oriented, and integrative. This perspective grounds the claim that internalization is an active organismic tendency whose failure is attributable to environmental interference rather than inherent deficiency.

#### 53. [DEFINITION] [[Satisfaction-Frustration-Asymmetry]] *(Line 573)*

> [!definition] [[Satisfaction-Frustration-Asymmetry]]
> The finding that need satisfaction and need frustration are not mirror images (endpoints of a single scale) but qualitatively distinct processes with different causal antecedents and consequence profiles. Need frustration produces active damage that cannot be fully remediated by restoring need satisfaction, constituting SDT as a theory of psychological damage as well as flourishing.

#### 54. [DEFINITION] [[Integration-Paradox]] *(Line 576)*

> [!definition] [[Integration-Paradox]]
> The structural paradox within OIT in which genuine identification with an externally sourced regulation requires a prior base of felt autonomy — the very resource that controlled motivational environments deplete. This paradox implies that motivation change cannot begin at the internalization step but must first attend to autonomy restoration.

#### 55. [DEFINITION] [[Conditional-Positive-Regard]] (Rogers, 1959; Roth et al., 2009) *(Line 579)*

> [!definition] [[Conditional-Positive-Regard]] (Rogers, 1959; Roth et al., 2009)
> The relational pattern in which approval, warmth, and positive regard are made contingent on meeting performance standards. Research within SDT demonstrates that conditional positive regard is a primary driver of introjected (rather than identified or integrated) regulation, because it communicates that the person's worth is performance-contingent and thus creates the relational threat that makes releasing the introjection feel existentially dangerous.

#### 56. [DEFINITION] [[Autonomy-Support]] *(Line 582)*

> [!definition] [[Autonomy-Support]]
> The environmental quality of providing meaningful choice, acknowledging perspectives, offering genuine rationale, and minimizing pressure. Autonomy support is the primary environmental condition enabling the introjection-to-identification transition, because it communicates that the person can genuinely choose rather than comply, making volitional endorsement psychologically available.

#### 57. [DEFINITION] [[Undermining-Effect]] (Deci, 1971) *(Line 585)*

> [!definition] [[Undermining-Effect]] (Deci, 1971)
> The empirical finding that external rewards contingent on intrinsically interesting activities reduce subsequent intrinsic motivation when the rewards are withdrawn. Mechanistically interpreted in this report as an existence proof that perceived locus of causality is causally potent — changes in how people experience their own agency produce measurable downstream motivational consequences.

#### 58. [DEFINITION] [[Need-Frustration]] *(Line 588)*

> [!definition] [[Need-Frustration]]
> The active blockage of basic psychological need satisfaction by the environment — not merely the absence of need support but the presence of conditions that actively prevent needs from being met. Need frustration is distinguished from need dissatisfaction by its distinct causal profile and its more severe and less reversible consequence profile.

> [!further-exploration] Deepening Your Practice
> > [!topic-idea] [[Relationship-Motivation-Theory]]

>

> > The existing PKB note on motivational compounding can be read as an applied extension of the internalization account — asking how progressively more integrated regulation compounds motivationally over time. A focused analysis of the compounding model would examine whether the same environmental conditions (autonomy support, unconditional regard) that enable the introjection-to-identification transition also enable the identity-to-action compounding that the model posits.
>

> > COT specifies individual-level variations in the disposition to orient toward autonomy, control, or impersonality — treating these as stable individual differences in how people tend to process their environments. >

> > A transfer-oriented extension of this analysis would ask: what regulatory style is required for [[Far-Transfer]]? The hypothesis — grounded in both SDT and transfer research — is that far transfer requires integrated regulation: the person must be able to abstract structural principles from their domain-specific application, and this kind of voluntary structural abstraction requires the generative engagement associated with autonomous motivation. Controlled motivation may produce near transfer (applying the learned procedure) but not far transfer (extracting the transferable principle).
>

> 
> > [!topic-idea] **Self-Determination Theory and Organizational Culture Change: A Transfer-Domain Investigation**
> > Applying the two-phase model (autonomy restoration → internalization facilitation) and the need-frustration asymmetry to organizational culture change would constitute a genuine transfer-domain investigation. The investigation would ask: what is the organizational equivalent of conditional regard? How does organizational culture produce introjected compliance rather than integrated commitment? And what does autonomy restoration look like at the organizational level — not for individuals but for teams and divisions?
