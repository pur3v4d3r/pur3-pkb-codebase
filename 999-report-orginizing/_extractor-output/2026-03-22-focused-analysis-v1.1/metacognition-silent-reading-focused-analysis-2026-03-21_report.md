#### 4. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 201)*

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Regulation (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 204)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Regulation (Flavell, 1979)
> The actual executive control processes — planning, monitoring, and regulating — through which learners manage their cognitive activity in real time. [[Metacognitive-Regulation]] is not knowledge about strategies but the active deployment of strategies in response to monitoring signals. It is the "doing it" layer.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Experience (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 211)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Experience (Flavell, 1979)
> The conscious, affective-cognitive experiences that accompany reading — feelings of understanding, confusion, familiarity, or difficulty that arise during a reading episode. [[Metacognitive-Experience]] includes the "feeling of knowing," the sense that a passage is or isn't making sense, and the nagging sense that something was just missed. These experiences are the *interface* between the reading process and the monitoring system.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] The Monitoring-Control Model (Nelson & Narens, 1990) *(Line 220)*

> [!definition] The Monitoring-Control Model (Nelson & Narens, 1990)
> A two-level architecture in which (1) the *object level* represents cognitive processing — in reading, the processes of word identification, syntactic parsing, inference generation, and situation model construction; (2) the *meta level* monitors the object level and can issue control signals to it; and (3) crucially, these levels communicate through two distinct channels: **monitoring** flows upward (from object to meta) and **control** flows downward (from meta to object). [[Monitoring-Control-Coupling]] occurs when a monitoring signal reliably activates a corresponding control response. [[Monitoring-Gap]] occurs when monitoring signals are generated but fail to activate control.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998) *(Line 229)*

> [!definition] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998)
> The mental representation a reader constructs of the *world* described by a text — not the text's surface form and not its propositional content, but the integrated model of events, entities, states, and their temporal, causal, and spatial relations. A reader who constructs only a *textbase* (a representation of the text's propositions) understands the words but not the world. A reader who constructs a rich [[Situation-Model]] understands the world the text is about. Comprehension monitoring at the highest level is monitoring for situational coherence — does the model being built hang together? Are new events consistent with established states?

#### 9. [KEY-CLAIM] Monitoring vs. Regulation: A Hard Distinction *(Line 236)*

> [!key-claim] Monitoring vs. Regulation: A Hard Distinction
> Monitoring is the detection of a cognitive state — feeling confused, noticing an inconsistency, recognizing that a referent is unclear. Regulation is the action taken in response. These are empirically dissociable: monitoring can occur without regulation, and regulation (in the form of re-reading or slowing) can occur without conscious monitoring. The failure to keep this distinction sharp has muddled decades of research and intervention design.

#### 10. [KEY-CLAIM] Surface vs. Deep Processing in Monitoring *(Line 239)*

> [!key-claim] Surface vs. Deep Processing in Monitoring
> Readers can monitor at multiple levels of text representation. Monitoring for *surface coherence* (do the words parse?) operates at a lower level of processing than monitoring for *situational coherence* (does this event make sense in the world the text has constructed?). Most comprehension failures that matter — failures of inference, failures of integration, failures of causal understanding — occur at the situational level. But the monitoring signals that are easiest to generate operate at the surface level.

#### 13. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] What the Error Detection Literature Actually Shows *(Line 262)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] What the Error Detection Literature Actually Shows
> The error detection paradigm is often interpreted as demonstrating that readers have poor monitoring skills — that they lack the ability to detect comprehension failures. A more precise reading of the evidence suggests something subtler: readers have the *capacity* for monitoring but deploy it selectively and inconsistently. The critical variable is not capacity but the conditions under which monitoring is spontaneously activated. This reframing has direct implications for intervention: if the problem is selective deployment, then strategy instruction that provides strategies without addressing the conditions of deployment will have limited generalizability.

#### 14. [EVIDENCE] Garner (1987) — The Compile-to-Understand Illusion *(Line 267)*

> [!evidence] Garner (1987) — The Compile-to-Understand Illusion
> Garner's comprehensive review of metacognitive failures in reading identified a pattern she called "compile to understand" — the tendency of many readers to treat successful word-by-word processing as equivalent to successful comprehension. Readers who "compile" in this sense feel that they have understood a text if they have read all the words without stumbling, regardless of whether those words have been integrated into a coherent [[Situation-Model]]. This illusion — a specific variant of the [[Fluency-Illusion]] — is, Garner argues, the primary mechanism by which monitoring fails in most adult readers. The feeling of smooth, fluent reading suppresses the generation of monitoring signals that would otherwise be triggered by comprehension failure.

#### 15. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] Fluency as Both Resource and Liability *(Line 274)*

> [!tension-identified] Fluency as Both Resource and Liability
> Fluency in word recognition and decoding is a demonstrably necessary condition for skilled reading comprehension — readers who must allocate working memory to word decoding have fewer cognitive resources for comprehension monitoring (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974; Perfetti, 1985). This is the "simple view of reading" logic: decode fluently, and cognitive resources are freed for comprehension. Yet the evidence on the [[Fluency-Illusion]] — reviewed in Phase IV — demonstrates that *too much* fluency paradoxically suppresses the monitoring signals that comprehension requires. The reader who decodes effortlessly is, in some sense, *less* likely to notice when comprehension is failing than the reader who struggles slightly. This is not a trivial finding: it suggests that the solution to reading comprehension difficulties is not simply increased fluency practice, and that as students become more fluent, monitoring instruction becomes *more* rather than less important.

#### 16. [WHAT-THE-EVIDENCE-SUGGESTS] The Externalization Pattern Across Multiple Paradigms *(Line 287)*

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] The Externalization Pattern Across Multiple Paradigms
> A striking pattern emerges when the most effective reading interventions are examined structurally: they all *externalize* the monitoring process. Think-aloud protocols, self-explanation, reciprocal teaching, annotation — each works in part by moving metacognitive monitoring from an internal, invisible, easily-suppressed process to an external, visible, socially-consequential one. This is not incidental to their effectiveness: it is the mechanism. The implication is that the [[Monitoring-Gap]] is most reliably closed not by instructing readers to monitor internally more carefully, but by creating conditions in which monitoring must be expressed.

#### 19. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Fluency Trap as a Signal Contamination Problem *(Line 320)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Fluency Trap as a Signal Contamination Problem
> Here is the mechanism in precise form: the monitoring system receives two types of signals from reading — fluency signals (how smoothly the reading is proceeding at the processing level) and coherence signals (how well the content being processed integrates with the existing situation model). In skilled readers, fluency signals are strong, continuous, and salient. Coherence signals are weaker, intermittent, and easily masked. Because the metacognitive system is sensitive to processing fluency — the subjective feeling of reading ease — it uses fluency as a proxy for comprehension. When reading is fluent, the monitoring system interprets this as evidence of successful comprehension, regardless of whether the situation model being constructed is actually coherent. This is not irrational: for the vast majority of words in a familiar domain, fluency *is* a reasonable proxy. The trap is that it fails silently and consequentially at the boundary — precisely when comprehension is most at risk.

#### 20. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] Monitoring as a Cognitive Luxury That Becomes a Necessity *(Line 329)*

> [!analytical-insight] Monitoring as a Cognitive Luxury That Becomes a Necessity
> There is a deep irony in the working memory analysis: monitoring is most needed precisely when the reading task is most cognitively demanding, and most cognitively demanding precisely when it is most likely to be dropped due to resource constraints. Dense texts require the most active situation model construction, which consumes the most working memory, which leaves the least resources for monitoring, which is when monitoring failures have the most serious consequences. This is not mere bad luck — it is a structural feature of the cognitive architecture. It implies that readers cannot be expected to monitor effectively on their most difficult texts without external support that offloads the monitoring burden.

#### 21. [KEY-INSIGHT] The Asymmetry Between Signal Generation and Signal Interpretation *(Line 340)*

> [!key-insight] The Asymmetry Between Signal Generation and Signal Interpretation
> Monitoring signals are *generated* by the monitoring system but *interpreted* by the reader before they activate regulation. The interpretation is strongly influenced by the reader's prior knowledge, self-efficacy, and situational context. A reader with high prior knowledge in a domain interprets a monitoring signal as "I just need to read more carefully." A reader with low prior knowledge interprets the same signal as "This is hard, which is expected." In both cases, the interpretation suppresses regulation. The signal must be both generated and interpreted as a genuine comprehension failure to trigger regulatory action — and there are multiple points at which this can fail.

#### 22. [TENSION-IDENTIFIED] The Instructional Attribution Problem *(Line 347)*

> [!tension-identified] The Instructional Attribution Problem
> Comprehension monitoring interventions typically target one of these three mechanisms. But researchers and practitioners frequently attribute comprehension monitoring failures to the wrong mechanism — for instance, attributing effort abandonment to strategy ignorance and responding with more strategy instruction that doesn't change the effort-cost calculus. Or attributing signal threshold failure to strategy activation failure and responding with explicit strategy selection guides that don't address the signal's weakness in the first place. The field's inconsistent results may partly reflect this attribution confusion: interventions are often well-designed for the mechanism they target but misassigned to readers whose monitoring failures arise from different mechanisms.

#### 23. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Diagnosis Problem in Novice Readers *(Line 354)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Diagnosis Problem in Novice Readers
> Novice readers who monitor at all tend to monitor at a single level — usually the lexical level ("I didn't know that word"). This creates a diagnostic trap: they identify failure accurately at the level they monitor, but most consequential comprehension failures are occurring at higher levels that they are not monitoring. The word "nevertheless" was recognized effortlessly; what was missed was that "nevertheless" is here signaling a logical contrast that requires the reader to revise a causal inference made three paragraphs earlier. A reader who is monitoring only for vocabulary difficulties will not detect this failure at all — and will not notice that they need to deploy any fix-up strategy beyond looking up the word.

> [!cross-domain-connection] Monitoring Architecture in Expert Clinical Diagnosis: Medicine → Reading

#### 26. [BEST-PRACTICE] For Signal Threshold Failure: The Incoherence Flag Protocol *(Line 385)*

> [!best-practice] For Signal Threshold Failure: The Incoherence Flag Protocol
> Readers who fail to generate adequate monitoring signals benefit from explicit instruction in *what coherence failure feels like at the situation model level* — not just vocabulary difficulty or syntactic confusion. The Incoherence Flag Protocol involves training readers to actively flag, during reading, any moment when they cannot answer the question: "What just changed in the world of this text?" This question probes situation model construction directly and generates a salient signal even when the text itself is syntactically and lexically unproblematic. The key implementation detail: the flag must be explicit (a mark on the page, a note) rather than internal — because internal flags are suppressed by the fluency trap.

#### 27. [BEST-PRACTICE] For Strategy Activation Failure: Failure Type Diagnosis *(Line 388)*

> [!best-practice] For Strategy Activation Failure: Failure Type Diagnosis
> Readers who detect comprehension failure but cannot select an appropriate fix-up strategy benefit from instruction in failure type diagnosis. Failures fall into three broad types: (1) *local failures* — a specific word, phrase, or sentence is unclear (fix-up: re-read, look up); (2) *integrative failures* — a new piece of information doesn't fit the existing situation model (fix-up: return to the inconsistent earlier material, explicitly ask "what assumption was wrong?"); (3) *background knowledge failures* — the text assumes knowledge the reader doesn't have (fix-up: pause reading, acquire background, return). Teaching readers to distinguish these failure types and select the corresponding fix-up strategy closes a specific gap in the monitoring-regulation coupling.

#### 28. [BEST-PRACTICE] For Effort Abandonment: The Comprehension ROI Frame *(Line 391)*

> [!best-practice] For Effort Abandonment: The Comprehension ROI Frame
> Effort abandonment is a motivational regulation problem. The most effective intervention is not more instruction but a reframing of the value calculation: explicit attention to the *downstream consequences* of the comprehension failure being abandoned. "If I continue without resolving this confusion, what will I be unable to understand in the next section?" Making the causal chain from present confusion to future confusion explicit changes the perceived ROI of fix-up strategy deployment. This works best when readers have a goal representation — some sense of why they are reading and what they are trying to take away.

#### 29. [BEST-PRACTICE] For All Three Mechanisms: The Question-Before-Reading Protocol *(Line 394)*

> [!best-practice] For All Three Mechanisms: The Question-Before-Reading Protocol
> The most powerful single practice with evidence across all three mechanism types is generating specific, answerable questions *before* reading a passage or section — not generic "I wonder what this will be about" but specific, testable predictions: "This section is about X; I predict the author will argue Y because Z." Pre-reading questions set an explicit standard against which monitoring signals are evaluated (addressing signal threshold failure), prime relevant fix-up strategies (addressing activation failure), and create an investment in comprehension that raises the perceived value of regulation (addressing effort abandonment). The practice is both well-supported by evidence and underused in adult self-directed reading.

#### 32. [ORIGINAL-SYNTHESIS] The Three-Tier Monitoring Architecture Model *(Line 437)*

> [!original-synthesis] The Three-Tier Monitoring Architecture Model
> The evidence reviewed in this analysis supports a model of comprehension monitoring that distinguishes three tiers, each with its own monitoring processes, failure modes, and remediation strategies. **Tier 1: Lexical-Syntactic Monitoring** operates on individual words and sentences, is highly automatic in skilled readers, and generates strong, salient signals when it fails (unfamiliar words, garden-path sentences). Its primary failure mode is false negative — it rarely generates false comprehension signals at this level. **Tier 2: Propositional Coherence Monitoring** operates on inter-sentence consistency and local referent resolution, is partially automatic in skilled readers, and generates moderate-strength signals when it fails. Its primary failure mode is the signal threshold problem — signals are generated but frequently suppressed by fluency. **Tier 3: Situational Coherence Monitoring** operates on the global consistency of the situation model being built, is highly effortful even in skilled readers, and generates weak signals that are easily masked by Tier 1 fluency. Its primary failure mode is signal contamination — Tier 1 fluency provides a strong false positive that suppresses Tier 3 signals. Most comprehension failures that matter occur at Tier 3. Most strategy instruction targets Tier 1 failure, because those failures are the most salient. The monitoring-regulation gap is primarily a Tier 3 failure that has been misdiagnosed as a Tier 1 or Tier 2 problem.

#### 33. [ANALYTICAL-INSIGHT] The Practical Implication of the Three-Tier Model *(Line 442)*

> [!analytical-insight] The Practical Implication of the Three-Tier Model
> If the three-tier model is correct, then the most important single change in reading instruction would be to systematically teach readers what Tier 3 monitoring failure *feels like* — the specific, often subtle phenomenology of situation model incoherence, as distinct from the much more familiar experience of lexical or syntactic difficulty. This is not a content-knowledge intervention or a strategy-knowledge intervention. It is a metacognitive-experience calibration intervention: training readers to recognize and take seriously a signal that they currently generate but routinely dismiss.

#### 36. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] The Fluency Trap in Software Debugging: Reading → Software Engineering *(Line 473)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] The Fluency Trap in Software Debugging: Reading → Software Engineering
> Expert software developers exhibit a well-documented phenomenon in debugging that structural-isomorphically matches the fluency trap. Developers who are highly fluent with a particular codebase — who can read familiar code patterns automatically — tend to skip-read through code sections that match familiar patterns, even when those patterns contain the bug. The experienced developer's pattern-recognition fluency generates a strong "this looks right" signal that suppresses careful monitoring of the code's actual logic. Junior developers, paradoxically, sometimes find bugs that experts miss because their slower, more effortful code reading doesn't trigger the same fluency-based monitoring suppression. The intervention that works — code review by someone unfamiliar with the codebase, or structured line-by-line review protocols — maps exactly onto the externalization principle: the monitoring process must be made external and systematic to overcome the fluency trap.

#### 37. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] The Fluency Trap in Clinical Diagnosis: Reading → Medicine *(Line 476)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] The Fluency Trap in Clinical Diagnosis: Reading → Medicine
> Groopman's (2007) analysis of diagnostic error in medicine, subsequently supported by systematic empirical work (Croskerry, 2002), identifies a pattern called *premature closure* — the tendency of experienced clinicians to stop diagnostic monitoring once a plausible diagnosis has been generated. The mechanism is structurally identical to the fluency trap: clinical pattern recognition (analogous to decoding fluency) generates a strong "this makes sense" signal that suppresses continued monitoring of the differential. The corrective intervention — explicit forcing of alternative diagnostic hypotheses, structured "what might this also be?" protocols — is the clinical equivalent of the Incoherence Flag Protocol: an externalized monitoring procedure designed to override the signal contamination from pattern recognition fluency.

#### 38. [CROSS-DOMAIN-CONNECTION] Monitoring-Regulation Decoupling in Project Management: Reading → Organizational Systems *(Line 487)*

> [!cross-domain-connection] Monitoring-Regulation Decoupling in Project Management: Reading → Organizational Systems
> The project management literature extensively documents what amounts to the monitoring-regulation decoupling problem at an organizational scale. Teams frequently have monitoring systems that correctly detect scope creep, schedule slippage, or budget overrun — but fail to take corrective action. The mechanisms are structurally identical to those identified in reading: signal threshold failure (small slippages are not perceived as actionable), strategy activation failure (teams detect a problem but don't have a well-specified response protocol for that type of failure), and effort abandonment (the correction is perceived as too disruptive relative to the perceived severity of the problem). The organizational equivalent of the three-strategy-recommendation is: (1) explicit signaling thresholds defined in advance, (2) pre-specified response protocols matched to failure type, and (3) regular "consequence cascade" analysis that makes the downstream impact of present problems vivid.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Comprehension Monitoring (Baker & Brown, 1984) *(Line 563)*

> [!definition] Comprehension Monitoring (Baker & Brown, 1984)
> The process of evaluating one's own text comprehension during reading — detecting failures of understanding, inconsistencies between text and background knowledge, and gaps in situational coherence. Comprehension monitoring is a specific application of [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]] to the reading domain. It is distinguished from comprehension *testing* (external assessment of comprehension) by its real-time, self-directed character.

#### 43. [DEFINITION] Fluency Trap (Original Synthesis, this analysis) *(Line 566)*

> [!definition] Fluency Trap (Original Synthesis, this analysis)
> The mechanism by which reading fluency — the smooth, automatic processing of text at the lexical and syntactic levels — suppresses comprehension monitoring signals at the situational coherence level. Arises because the metacognitive system uses processing fluency as a proxy for comprehension success, generating false-positive comprehension signals when fluent decoding coexists with situation-model incoherence. A specific instantiation of the [[Fluency-Illusion]] in the context of comprehension monitoring.

#### 44. [DEFINITION] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983) *(Line 569)*

> [!definition] Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> The mental representation a reader constructs of the world described by a text — incorporating entities, events, spatial relations, temporal relations, causal relations, and intentional states. Distinguished from the *textbase* (a representation of the text's explicit propositions) and the *surface form* (a verbatim representation of the text's wording). Comprehension at the situation model level is the most important and most frequently under-monitored level of reading.

#### 45. [DEFINITION] Fix-Up Strategy *(Line 572)*

> [!definition] Fix-Up Strategy
> A regulatory action deployed in response to a detected comprehension failure. Fix-up strategies range on a continuum from local (re-reading a sentence) to global (returning to an earlier passage, seeking background knowledge, constructing a summary diagram). Strategy activation failure — the inability to select an appropriate fix-up strategy when a failure has been detected — is one of the three mechanisms of monitoring-regulation decoupling identified in this analysis.

#### 46. [DEFINITION] Monitoring-Regulation Decoupling *(Line 575)*

> [!definition] Monitoring-Regulation Decoupling
> The failure of detected comprehension failures to trigger corresponding regulatory actions. Distinguished from monitoring failure (not detecting a failure) by the presence of a monitoring signal that does not result in regulatory action. The three mechanisms identified in this analysis are signal threshold failure, strategy activation failure, and effort abandonment. Central concept from [[Monitoring-Control-Coupling]] theory (Nelson & Narens, 1990) applied to the reading domain.

#### 47. [DEFINITION] Three-Tier Monitoring Architecture (Original Synthesis, this analysis) *(Line 578)*

> [!definition] Three-Tier Monitoring Architecture (Original Synthesis, this analysis)
> An integrative model of comprehension monitoring distinguishing Tier 1 (lexical-syntactic monitoring), Tier 2 (propositional coherence monitoring), and Tier 3 (situational coherence monitoring). Each tier has distinct failure modes and remediation strategies. The central claim is that most consequential comprehension failures occur at Tier 3, while most strategy instruction targets Tier 1.

#### 48. [DEFINITION] Metacognitive Experience (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 581)*

> [!definition] Metacognitive Experience (Flavell, 1979)
> The conscious affective-cognitive experiences that accompany cognitive activity — including feelings of knowing, confusion, familiarity, and difficulty. In reading, metacognitive experiences are the primary interface between the comprehension process and the monitoring system: they serve as the trigger signals that should activate regulatory action. The fluency trap operates precisely at this level, generating false "feeling of comprehending" experiences that suppress regulatory activation.

#### 49. [DEFINITION] Textbase (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978) *(Line 584)*

> [!definition] Textbase (Kintsch & van Dijk, 1978)
> A propositional representation of a text's explicit content — what the text actually says, represented as a network of linked propositions. Distinguished from the [[Situation-Model]] (what the world described by the text is like). Readers who construct only a textbase can answer questions about the text's wording but cannot reliably answer questions about the world it describes. Most monitoring instruction that targets verbatim recall operates at the textbase level; most consequential comprehension failures occur at the situation model level.

#### 50. [DEFINITION] Signal Threshold Failure *(Line 587)*

> [!definition] Signal Threshold Failure
> The monitoring-regulation decoupling mechanism in which a comprehension failure generates a monitoring signal, but that signal's strength does not reach the threshold required to activate regulatory action. Occurs because many real-world comprehension failures — subtle causal inconsistencies, unstated assumptions, imprecise inferences — generate weak monitoring signals that are interpreted as unfamiliarity rather than genuine failure. Primary failure mode at Tier 2 and Tier 3 of the three-tier monitoring architecture.

#### 51. [DEFINITION] Externalization Protocol *(Line 590)*

> [!definition] Externalization Protocol
> An intervention that moves the monitoring process from internal (invisible, suppressible) to external (visible, documented) form. Examples include annotation, think-aloud, self-explanation, and structured dialogue. The analytical basis for externalization's effectiveness in closing the monitoring-regulation gap is that it bypasses the fluency trap: once monitoring is externalized, fluency signals in the primary reading process cannot suppress it because it is occurring outside that process.

#### 52. [DEFINITION] Effort Abandonment *(Line 593)*

> [!definition] Effort Abandonment
> The monitoring-regulation decoupling mechanism in which a comprehension failure is correctly detected, an appropriate fix-up strategy is identified, but the reader declines to deploy it because the perceived effort cost exceeds the perceived value of correction. A motivational regulation failure rather than a monitoring or strategy knowledge failure. Addressed by interventions that explicitly alter the perceived ROI of fix-up strategy deployment.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Metacognitive-Calibration]] in Reading Assessment
> > The analysis identifies miscalibrated monitoring signals as central to comprehension failure, but the question of how to *measure* and *train* metacognitive calibration in reading specifically is a rich extension. > > [!topic-idea] [[Self-Explanation-Effect]] as Architecture, Not Elaboration
> > This analysis reframes the self-explanation effect as evidence for the externalization principle rather than the traditional elaboration account. A focused analysis of the self-explanation research from this architectural perspective — specifically asking whether the monitoring-externalization component and the elaboration component can be experimentally dissociated — would both test the analysis's central claim and extend the [[Self-Explanation-Effect]] literature.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Transfer-of-Learning]] from Instructed to Spontaneous Monitoring
> > The analysis identified the lack of transfer from instructed to spontaneous monitoring as the field's central unresolved challenge. A dedicated treatment of this problem — connecting to the [[Transfer-of-Learning]] literature on near vs. far transfer, on fading scaffolds, and on the conditions for spontaneous application of trained strategies — would directly address the limitation identified in Phase V.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Working-Memory-Capacity]] and Individual Differences in Monitoring Vulnerability
> > Phase IV argues that working memory constraints make monitoring most vulnerable precisely when it is most needed. A focused analysis of individual differences in this vulnerability — asking whether readers with higher working memory capacity are less susceptible to the fluency trap and monitoring-regulation decoupling — would both test the mechanistic account and connect to the broader [[Working-Memory-Capacity]] and reading comprehension literature.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] Monitoring-Regulation Decoupling as Transfer Target: The Three-Mechanism Model Applied to Organizational Learning
> > Phase VII identified the structural analogue between reading monitoring failure and project management monitoring failure. A dedicated transfer-oriented analysis — applying the three-mechanism decoupling model (signal threshold, strategy activation, effort abandonment) systematically to organizational monitoring systems — would extend the principles identified here into the organizational learning and systems design literature. This is the primary transfer-oriented expansion topic for this analysis.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Pseudometacognition]] and the "Performing Monitoring" Problem
> > The analysis focuses on genuine monitoring failure, but a related and underexplored problem is *performative* monitoring — readers who produce the outputs of monitoring (annotations, question lists) without the underlying cognitive process. [[Pseudometacognition]] in the reading context deserves a focused analysis that distinguishes superficial strategy compliance from genuine monitoring engagement, and that examines the conditions under which externalization protocols devolve into performative rather than functional monitoring.
> 
> > [!topic-idea] [[Situation-Model]] Construction and Breakdown: A Deeper Dive
> > This analysis treats the situation model as the target of Tier 3 monitoring without fully examining *how* situation models are constructed and how their construction fails. A focused analysis of situation model breakdown — when readers construct incomplete, incoherent, or subtly incorrect situation models — and its relationship to the monitoring signals it generates would provide the architectural depth that Tier 3 monitoring requires for precise intervention design.
