#### 1. [ABSTRACT] Untitled *(Line 296)*

> [!abstract] Untitled
> [[John-Dewey|John Dewey]] (1859–1952) stands as arguably the most influential American philosopher of education, whose systematic theorization of [[Reflective-Thinking|reflective thinking]] fundamentally shaped modern conceptions of [[Critical-Thinking|critical thinking]] and transformed educational practice worldwide. As a founding figure in [[pragmatism]] and functional psychology, Dewey developed a comprehensive philosophical and psychological framework that revolutionized understanding of how learning occurs, how thinking should be cultivated, and how democratic societies should educate their citizens. His establishment of the [[University of Chicago Laboratory School]] in 1896 provided the experimental ground for testing his theories, producing empirical evidence that challenged traditional educational orthodoxy and laid the foundation for the [[progressive education movement]]. This exposition provides a comprehensive scholarly treatment of Dewey's contributions to critical thinking and educational psychology, tracing the intellectual genealogy of his ideas, explicating his theoretical architecture, examining the empirical foundations of his work, and analyzing his enduring influence on contemporary educational practice and critical thinking scholarship.

> [!the-philosophy] Untitled
> **The Deweyan Revolution**: Dewey's fundamental insight was that thinking itself could be systematically cultivated through educational design, that democracy required citizens capable of [[inquiry]]-based reasoning, and that schools must be transformed from institutions of rote transmission into laboratories for developing [[reflective intelligence]]. This vision challenged the entire edifice of nineteenth-century education.

#### 7. [CORE-PRINCIPLE] Untitled *(Line 340)*

> [!core-principle] Untitled
> **Pragmatic Epistemology**: The theory that knowledge is not the passive mirroring of antecedent reality but the active reconstruction of experience to enable more intelligent action—judging ideas by their consequences for problem-solving rather than their correspondence to fixed truths.

#### 8. [ARGUMENT] Untitled *(Line 349)*

> [!argument] Untitled
> Dewey's critique of traditional epistemology rested on showing that the spectator theory generated insoluble pseudo-problems (such as skepticism about the external world) precisely because it began from a false picture of the relationship between knower and known. If inquiry is understood not as mirroring reality but as transforming problematic situations into resolved ones, the traditional puzzles dissolve.

#### 9. [DEFINITION] Untitled *(Line 354)*

> [!definition] Untitled
> **Reflective Thinking**: "Active, persistent, and careful consideration of any belief or supposed form of knowledge in the light of the grounds that support it and the further conclusions to which it tends"—distinguished from mere daydreaming, associative thought, or uncritical acceptance of ideas.

#### 10. [INSIGHT] Untitled *(Line 363)*

> [!insight] Untitled
> Dewey's profound insight was recognizing that while these phases can be analytically distinguished, in actual inquiry they interpenetrate and cycle. Problem definition may need revision based on hypothesis testing; new suggestions may emerge during reasoning; observation may reveal aspects of the situation not initially apparent. Genuine inquiry is not linear but recursive.

#### 11. [CORE-PRINCIPLE] Untitled *(Line 370)*

> [!core-principle] Untitled
> **Principle of Continuity and Interaction**: Every experience both takes up something from previous experiences and modifies the quality of future experiences. Learning occurs through the interaction of organism and environment, not through passive absorption of information.

#### 12. [EXAMPLE] Untitled *(Line 377)*

The objective stimulus is identical; the experience differs based on what each student transacts with the situation.
