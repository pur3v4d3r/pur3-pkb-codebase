#### 2. [DEFINITION] Fallibilism *(Line 286)*

> [!definition] Fallibilism
> The philosophical thesis that any belief, however well-justified, could in principle be false, and that the evidence or reasoning supporting it could be overturned by subsequent inquiry. Fallibilism does not entail that our beliefs *are* false or probably false, nor that they are unjustified — only that their justification is inherently defeasible. The thesis applies paradigmatically to empirical knowledge but has been extended by various philosophers to moral, mathematical, and logical domains. Fallibilism is a thesis about the *epistemic status* of beliefs, not a prediction about how often they will be revised.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Infallibilism *(Line 289)*

> [!definition] Infallibilism
> The contrasting view that genuine knowledge requires certainty — that a knower can only be said to *know* something if their justification is strong enough to exclude the possibility of error. On this view, if there is any chance (however remote) that a belief is false, it does not qualify as knowledge in the strict sense. [[Descartes]]'s method of doubt in the *Meditations* (1641) is the classical expression: he withholds the label "knowledge" from any belief that can be doubted. Many ordinary language uses of "know" implicitly invoke infallibilist standards.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Defeasibility *(Line 294)*

> [!definition] Defeasibility
> A property of justification or reasons: a justification J for belief B is *defeasible* if there is some additional evidence E such that J together with E no longer justifies B. The concept captures the fallibilist claim that justification is always provisional. A defeasible reason is a genuine reason — it actually supports the belief — but it can be "defeated" by defeating evidence. This is to be contrasted with deductive entailment, which is indefeasible: if premises P1 and P2 deductively entail conclusion C, no additional information can make C false given P1 and P2 (though it might give us reason to reject P1 or P2).

#### 5. [KEY-CLAIM] The Fallibilist Minimal Thesis *(Line 297)*

> [!key-claim] The Fallibilist Minimal Thesis
> Fallibilism's core claim is this: **justified belief does not require the impossibility of error**. A person can know that p — can have well-justified, true, appropriately grounded belief that p — even if there is a non-zero epistemic probability that p is false. This separates fallibilism from both scepticism (which claims we know very little, because justification always falls short) and infallibilism (which claims we know only what we are certain about). Fallibilism holds that ordinary, provisional, defeasible justification is enough for genuine knowledge.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Epistemic Humility *(Line 306)*

> [!definition] Epistemic Humility
> The intellectual virtue of recognising the limits of one's knowledge, the possibility of one's errors, and the genuine epistemic standing of others who disagree. [[Epistemic-Humility|Epistemic humility]] is the dispositional correlate of the theoretical claim of fallibilism: if our beliefs could be wrong, then intellectual integrity demands that we hold them with a degree of tentativeness proportioned to our evidence, remain open to correction, and acknowledge what we do not know. Epistemic humility is not the same as intellectual timidity or the performance of uncertainty for social purposes — it is a cognitive and motivational orientation grounded in an accurate appraisal of one's epistemic situation.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Epistemic Calibration *(Line 309)*

> [!definition] Epistemic Calibration
> The degree to which a person's confidence in their beliefs matches the actual probability of those beliefs being true. A well-calibrated agent who says "I'm 80% confident in this" is right approximately 80% of the time across such claims. Fallibilism implies the importance of calibration: if all beliefs are potentially mistaken, then overconfidence is a systematic epistemic failing. Research in [[Cognitive-Psychology|cognitive psychology]] consistently shows that humans tend toward overconfidence — we are typically more certain than our evidence warrants. Calibration is thus both a theoretical commitment of fallibilism and a practical goal of epistemic education.

#### 9. [KEY-CLAIM] Peirce's Self-Correction Thesis *(Line 337)*

> [!key-claim] Peirce's Self-Correction Thesis
> The scientific method does not require that any particular inquiry be infallible. What it requires is that the method itself have the property of correcting its own errors over time. Error-detection and error-correction are baked into inquiry through the mechanisms of hypothesis testing, peer review, replication, and the social accountability of the scientific community. Individual fallibility and collective progress are not merely compatible — the acknowledgment of individual fallibility is a precondition of the collective process working.

#### 10. [COUNTER-ARGUMENT] Challenges to Falsificationism *(Line 346)*

> [!counter-argument] Challenges to Falsificationism
> Popper's program faces significant objections. The [[Duhem-Quine-thesis|Duhem-Quine thesis]] (developed independently by Pierre Duhem and W.V.O. Quine) points out that scientific theories are not tested in isolation: when an observation conflicts with a prediction, it is unclear whether to revise the core theory, an auxiliary hypothesis, or even a background assumption about the measuring instruments. This means that falsification is never as clean as Popper suggests — there is always logical room to protect a theory by adjusting its surrounding assumptions. Historians and sociologists of science (most notably [[Thomas-Kuhn|Thomas Kuhn]]) also noted that scientists do not in practice abandon theories upon encountering anomalies; they persist within paradigms until the weight of anomalies becomes overwhelming. These objections do not refute fallibilism, but they complicate the picture of exactly how scientific self-correction proceeds.

#### 11. [INSIGHT] Holism's Implication for Fallibilism *(Line 355)*

> [!insight] Holism's Implication for Fallibilism
> Quine's holism extends fallibilism in a radical direction: not only our factual beliefs but our theoretical commitments, methodological assumptions, and even certain logical principles are in principle revisable. This does not mean all beliefs are equally shaky. The web has a centre and a periphery, and beliefs near the centre are more deeply embedded and therefore more difficult (though not impossible) to revise without cascading effects. But it does mean that intellectual humility cannot be selective — confined to our factual beliefs while treating our theoretical frameworks as sacrosanct. Even our most fundamental ways of organising experience are, in the last analysis, tools we have fashioned and can, in the last analysis, refashion.

#### 13. [KEY-INSIGHT] Fallibilism Is Not an Invitation to Doubt Everything *(Line 387)*

> [!key-insight] Fallibilism Is Not an Invitation to Doubt Everything
> A common misreading of fallibilism takes it to imply that because any belief could be wrong, we have reason to doubt all beliefs equally. This is a serious error. The strength of justification varies enormously across beliefs: that the Earth is roughly spherical is incomparably better justified than any particular ten-day weather forecast. Fallibilism holds that *both* beliefs are revisable in principle, but it does not pretend that both stand in the same epistemic position. The appropriate response to fallibilism is calibrated confidence — not uniform uncertainty.

#### 14. [EXAMPLE] Self-Correction in Practice: The Case of H. pylori *(Line 394)*

> [!example] Self-Correction in Practice: The Case of H. pylori
> For most of the twentieth century, the medical consensus held that stomach ulcers were caused by stress and excess acid. The gastric environment was considered too acidic to support bacterial life. In 1982, Australian physicians [[Barry Marshall]] and [[Robin Warren]] observed that most ulcer patients were infected with a spiral bacterium, *Helicobacter pylori*. Their claim was dismissed and their submitted papers rejected. Marshall famously drank a culture of H. pylori, developing gastritis, then treated himself with antibiotics. Over the following decade, increasingly rigorous evidence accumulated, the consensus shifted, and in 2005 Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The episode illustrates both the fallibility of scientific consensus and the self-correcting power of inquiry when conducted with appropriate method and courage.

#### 17. [BEST-PRACTICE] Communicating Scientific Fallibilism *(Line 423)*

> [!best-practice] Communicating Scientific Fallibilism
> One practically important implication of fallibilism in science communication is the importance of accurately representing the degree of certainty attached to scientific claims. Saying "the evidence strongly supports X" is both more accurate and more epistemically honest than "science has proven X." The former communicates the actual epistemic status of the claim; the latter creates unrealistic expectations that, when any revision occurs, can feed public distrust. Fallibilist communication is not weaker than infallibilist communication — it is more honest and ultimately more credible.

#### 18. [BEST-PRACTICE] Pedagogical Strategies for Fallibilist Education *(Line 432)*

> [!best-practice] Pedagogical Strategies for Fallibilist Education
> Several evidence-informed strategies cultivate fallibilist dispositions in students. *Explicitly modelling intellectual revision* — teachers visibly updating their own positions in response to student challenges — teaches that revision is a sign of intellectual strength, not weakness. *Arguing for positions one does not hold* (the classical *in utramque partem* exercise from rhetoric) develops the capacity to imaginatively inhabit alternative perspectives. *Asking students to identify conditions under which their own conclusions could be wrong* transforms the act of forming a belief into an occasion for epistemic self-examination. *Structured academic controversy* — having students research and present opposing sides of genuine disputes — develops appreciation for the genuine complexity of contested questions.

#### 19. [IMPORTANT] The Boundary Problem: Where Does Fallibilism Apply? *(Line 445)*

> [!important] The Boundary Problem: Where Does Fallibilism Apply?
> One genuinely contested question is whether fallibilism applies universally — to formal disciplines like mathematics and logic — or whether it is specifically a thesis about empirical knowledge. Most fallibilists hold that mathematical and logical truths are not revisable in the same way as empirical claims: once a proof is valid, it is valid, and no observation could overturn it. The Quinean position, however, suggests that even logical principles are in principle revisable (though in practice almost never revised). This debate remains unresolved and touches on deep questions about the relationship between formal and empirical knowledge.

#### 20. [WARNING] The Performative Contradiction Objection *(Line 448)*

> [!warning] The Performative Contradiction Objection
> A common objection to fallibilism is that it seems to undermine itself: if all beliefs could be wrong, then the belief that "all beliefs could be wrong" could itself be wrong — which would mean some beliefs could not be wrong, contradicting fallibilism. This objection has some dialectical bite, but most fallibilists defuse it by noting that fallibilism is best understood as a *methodological* claim about the appropriate epistemic attitude rather than a first-order metaphysical claim about the nature of truth. The fallibilist does not need to be certain that fallibilism is correct — only that it is the best-supported, most epistemically responsible orientation available. The claim can be held provisionally, as all claims should be.

#### 21. [WARNING] Fallibilism and Motivated Scepticism *(Line 451)*

> [!warning] Fallibilism and Motivated Scepticism
> In practice, fallibilism can be weaponised. Those with ideological commitments to denying well-established findings — in climate science, vaccine safety, evolutionary biology — sometimes invoke fallibilist language ("science changes all the time," "scientists have been wrong before") to manufacture apparent doubt about findings that are in fact overwhelmingly supported. This motivated deployment of fallibilism confuses the legitimate epistemic point that *in principle* scientific findings are revisable with the empirically false claim that *in this case* the evidence is genuinely contested. Genuine fallibilism attends to the actual weight of evidence; it does not treat all revisability claims as equally weighty.

#### 25. [DEFINITION] Fallibilism *(Line 507)*

> [!definition] Fallibilism
> The epistemological thesis that justified beliefs can be false and that the evidence supporting them can be overturned. Fallibilism does not entail doubt about all beliefs, low confidence, or the denial of knowledge — it entails that knowledge is compatible with defeasibility. Distinguished from scepticism (which denies we have knowledge) and from relativism (which denies objective standards of justification).

#### 26. [DEFINITION] Defeasibility (Defeasible Justification) *(Line 510)*

> [!definition] Defeasibility (Defeasible Justification)
> A property of a justification: it is defeasible if there exists possible additional evidence that would weaken or remove it. All empirical justification is thought by fallibilists to be defeasible. Contrast with deductive entailment, which is indefeasible. Introduced into epistemology prominently by Roderick Chisholm and later systematised in the work of John Pollock.

#### 27. [DEFINITION] Corroboration (Popperian) *(Line 513)*

> [!definition] Corroboration (Popperian)
> Karl Popper's term for the epistemic standing earned by a hypothesis that has survived rigorous attempts at falsification. A highly corroborated hypothesis is not *verified* — it has not been confirmed as certainly true — but it has demonstrated its robustness by repeatedly surviving tests designed to refute it. Corroboration is explicitly not a measure of probability of truth but of tested resilience.

#### 28. [DEFINITION] Epistemic Holism *(Line 516)*

> [!definition] Epistemic Holism
> The thesis, associated primarily with W.V.O. Quine, that individual beliefs do not face empirical evidence in isolation but as part of an interconnected system. When experience conflicts with our expectations, we must revise our beliefs somewhere, but logic does not uniquely determine where. Holism supports fallibilism by showing that revision is always possible, and complicates Popperian falsificationism by showing that individual statements cannot be straightforwardly refuted without affecting surrounding assumptions.

#### 29. [DEFINITION] Defeat / Defeater *(Line 519)*

> [!definition] Defeat / Defeater
> A defeater for a belief is any evidence or consideration that rationally requires the holder of that belief to revise it. *Rebutting defeaters* provide direct evidence against the target belief. *Undercutting defeaters* provide evidence against the reliability or applicability of the justification for the belief. The defeater framework, developed by John Pollock, is the primary formal vocabulary for understanding how fallibilist revision works.

#### 30. [DEFINITION] Calibration (Epistemic) *(Line 522)*

> [!definition] Calibration (Epistemic)
> The degree to which an agent's confidence in their beliefs matches the frequency with which such beliefs are true. A well-calibrated agent who expresses 70% confidence in a class of claims is right approximately 70% of the time. Overconfidence — the prevalent human tendency — is the systematic practical violation of fallibilist norms. Calibration research connects epistemology to cognitive psychology and decision science.

#### 31. [DEFINITION] Open Society (Popper) *(Line 525)*

> [!definition] Open Society (Popper)
> Karl Popper's term for a society organised on fallibilist principles — one that treats its institutions, policies, and governing ideas as revisable and maintains the structural conditions (free inquiry, free press, independent institutions) necessary for error-detection and correction. Contrasted with the "closed society," which treats its foundational commitments as certain and therefore inviolable, eliminating the mechanisms of self-correction.

#### 32. [DEFINITION] Intellectual Humility *(Line 528)*

> [!definition] Intellectual Humility
> An intellectual virtue consisting in the accurate recognition of one's epistemic limitations, susceptibility to error, and dependence on others for knowledge. Intellectual humility is the dispositional correlate of fallibilism: if we could be wrong, intellectual integrity demands that we hold beliefs with appropriate tentativeness, acknowledge the force of opposing arguments, and remain genuinely open to revision. Distinct from intellectual timidity (excessive self-doubt) and intellectual arrogance (unwarranted certainty).

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Confirmation Bias *(Line 531)*

> [!definition] Confirmation Bias
> The systematic tendency to seek, notice, weight, and remember information that confirms existing beliefs, while neglecting, dismissing, or underweighting disconfirming information. Confirmation bias is the primary psychological mechanism by which fallibilism is violated in practice — not through explicit philosophical commitment to infallibilism but through motivated asymmetric processing of evidence. First formally identified in Peter Wason's selection task experiments (1960s).

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Progressive Approximation *(Line 534)*

> [!definition] Progressive Approximation
> The fallibilist model of epistemic progress as an asymptotic approach toward truth — each stage of inquiry producing theories that are more accurate, more comprehensive, and better supported than their predecessors, while remaining imperfect and in principle revisable. Associated with Nicholas Rescher's philosophy of inquiry. Preserves the objectivity and meaningfulness of knowledge claims while honouring the permanent possibility of revision.
