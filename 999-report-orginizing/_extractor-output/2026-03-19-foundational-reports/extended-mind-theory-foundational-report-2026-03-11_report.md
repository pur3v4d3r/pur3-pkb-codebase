#### 2. [DEFINITION] Classical Cognitivism *(Line 286)*

> [!definition] Classical Cognitivism
> The theoretical framework, dominant in cognitive science from the 1960s–1990s, holding that cognition consists in computational processes over internal, brain-bound representations. The mind is understood as an information-processing system with a principled boundary at the organism's skin or skull. External environments figure only as inputs and outputs, never as constituents of cognitive processes proper.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Internalism (Philosophy of Mind) *(Line 291)*

> [!definition] Internalism (Philosophy of Mind)
> The position that the intrinsic, physical properties of a system's internal states — specifically, states inside the skin or skull of the organism — are both necessary and sufficient to determine the content and character of mental states. Internalism is the target of both content externalism (Putnam, Burge) and vehicle externalism (Clark, Chalmers). NOT the same as claiming that nothing external is causally relevant to cognition — only that the cognitive system itself is internally bounded.

#### 4. [KEY-CLAIM] The Otto-Inga Parity Argument *(Line 304)*

> [!key-claim] The Otto-Inga Parity Argument
> If a piece of information (a) is reliably accessible, (b) is automatically endorsed when accessed, (c) plays the same functional role in guiding behaviour as a biological memory trace would, then there is no principled reason to deny that it constitutes a belief — regardless of whether it is stored in neurons or in a notebook. The location of storage is cognitively irrelevant; the functional role is what counts.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] The Parity Principle (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) *(Line 309)*

> [!definition] The Parity Principle (Clark & Chalmers, 1998)
> If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognising as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (for that time) part of the cognitive process. The principle is explicitly functionalist in character: cognitive status is determined by causal-functional role, not by material constitution or spatial location. It does NOT entail that all external processes count as cognitive — only those meeting the functional equivalence criterion.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Active Externalism *(Line 316)*

> [!definition] Active Externalism
> Clark and Chalmers's term for their own position: the view that the *vehicles* of cognitive states — the physical processes constituting cognition, not merely the contents they carry — can extend beyond the boundary of brain and body. Distinguished from passive externalism (content externalism) by its claim that the world plays an active, vehicle-level role in cognitive processes, not merely a background role in determining representational content.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Passive Externalism (Content Externalism) *(Line 319)*

> [!definition] Passive Externalism (Content Externalism)
> The view, associated with Putnam and Burge, that the *contents* of mental states are partly determined by factors external to the individual — the physical environment (natural kind terms) or social community (linguistic norms) — while the *vehicles* or physical realisers of those states remain brain-bound. Active externalism is a stronger, more radical claim: it extends the vehicles themselves.

#### 10. [KEY-CLAIM] Extended vs. Embedded Cognition — A Critical Distinction *(Line 346)*

> [!key-claim] Extended vs. Embedded Cognition — A Critical Distinction
> Embedded cognition claims the mind exploits environmental structure; Extended Mind Theory claims the mind *incorporates* environmental structure. On the embedded view, removing the environment impairs cognitive performance but does not alter the boundary of the cognitive system. On the extended view, removing the environmental component is like removing part of the brain — it genuinely diminishes the system, not merely its resources. This distinction has real consequences for how we think about cognitive dependency, disability, and the design of learning environments.

#### 11. [EVIDENCE] Navigation as Distributed Cognition — Hutchins's Core Findings *(Line 355)*

> [!evidence] Navigation as Distributed Cognition — Hutchins's Core Findings
> Hutchins's studies of ship navigation demonstrated that: (a) no single crew member possesses all the knowledge required to navigate the vessel; (b) cognitive work is achieved through the coordinated interaction of crew members, physical artefacts (charts, plotting tools, alidades), and encoded procedures; (c) errors at the system level often arise not from individual incompetence but from breakdowns in the distribution of cognitive labour across the system; (d) the physical arrangement of the work environment encodes knowledge in a form that shapes and scaffolds individual cognition. These findings support the view that cognitive analysis must take the entire sociotechnical system as its unit of analysis.

#### 12. [COUNTER-ARGUMENT] The Coupling-Constitution Objection (Adams & Aizawa) *(Line 362)*

> [!counter-argument] The Coupling-Constitution Objection (Adams & Aizawa)
> Just because two systems are causally linked does not mean they are parts of the same system. The hammer is causally coupled to the carpenter's arm when she swings it, but we do not conclude that the hammer is part of the carpenter's motor system. Similarly, Otto's notebook is causally coupled to his cognitive processes, but this does not show it is part of his cognitive system. The coupling-constitution inference, Adams and Aizawa argue, is a fallacy. What is needed, and what Clark and Chalmers fail to provide, is a principled account of the **mark of the cognitive** — a criterion distinguishing genuinely cognitive processes from merely causally relevant ones.

#### 13. [COUNTER-ARGUMENT] The Cognitive Bloat Problem *(Line 369)*

> [!counter-argument] The Cognitive Bloat Problem
> A related objection, raised by [[Robert Rupert]] among others: if we accept the Parity Principle without restriction, we seem committed to the view that vast swathes of the environment — every book in a library, every website, every storage system a person could potentially use — constitute parts of extended cognitive systems. This bloat seems to make the concept of a cognitive system so expansive as to lose theoretical purchase. Defenders respond that genuine cognitive extension requires a sufficiently intimate and reliable coupling — not mere potential accessibility — but spelling out that criterion with precision is an ongoing challenge.

#### 16. [KEY-CLAIM] Conditions for Genuine Cognitive Extension (Clark) *(Line 392)*

> [!key-claim] Conditions for Genuine Cognitive Extension (Clark)
> For an external element to qualify as a genuine cognitive extension rather than a merely useful tool, Clark proposes that it must satisfy at least the following: (1) reliable and consistent availability when the cognitive task demands it; (2) direct accessibility without effortful search; (3) automatic endorsement — information retrieved from it is not systematically double-checked before guiding action; (4) past endorsement — the information stored in it was endorsed by the individual as accurate. Together, these conditions narrow the class of external elements that count as genuine cognitive extensions to those that are functionally equivalent to the most intimate forms of internal storage.

#### 17. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) *(Line 399)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The use of the environment to reduce the cognitive demands placed on internal biological cognitive systems. Offloading encompasses strategies ranging from writing things down to arranging physical objects spatially to using digital reminders. Importantly, offloading is not the same as cognitive extension in Clark and Chalmers's sense: offloading is a strategy for reducing cognitive load, while extension is a claim about the actual constitution of the cognitive system. The distinction is subtle but theoretically important: a person can offload extensively without any of the offloaded processes qualifying as part of their extended cognitive system.

#### 18. [EXAMPLE] Tool Incorporation in Expert Practice *(Line 406)*

> [!example] Tool Incorporation in Expert Practice
> The blind person who navigates with a cane offers one of Clark's favourite examples of extended cognition in action. An experienced cane user does not attend to the point where the hand meets the cane; they attend to the end of the cane, where it contacts the ground. The information about ground texture and obstacles is phenomenologically experienced at the tip of the cane, not at the palm. The cane has become, in functional and phenomenological terms, part of the body schema — an extension of the tactile system. This is not merely a metaphor of convenience; it describes a genuine reorganisation of the perceptual-motor system around the tool.

> Traditional "closed-book" assessments create artificial conditions in which learners must perform without the extended cognitive systems they habitually rely on. This is not an argument against all forms of closed-book assessment, but it is an argument for clarity about what such assessments are actually measuring.

> On an extended mind analysis, cognitive disability is not necessarily a property of an individual brain but of the organism-environment system. A person with Alzheimer's disease in an environment richly furnished with cognitive supports (clear labelling, reliable reminders, accessible notes) may function at a higher level than the same person in a cognitively impoverished environment. This reframes both the design of care environments and the assessment of individual cognitive capacity.

#### 24. [IMPORTANT] The Coupling-Constitution Problem Remains Unresolved *(Line 452)*

> [!important] The Coupling-Constitution Problem Remains Unresolved
> The most serious limitation of Extended Mind Theory is that the coupling-constitution objection has not been definitively answered. Clark's Parity Principle provides a functional criterion for cognitive extension, but the question of whether functional equivalence is *sufficient* for cognitive constitution — or whether something additional is required — remains contested. Readers should hold the positive claims of the theory with corresponding tentativeness.

#### 25. [IMPORTANT] The Operational Criteria Are Underspecified *(Line 455)*

> [!important] The Operational Criteria Are Underspecified
> Clark's conditions for genuine cognitive extension (reliable availability, direct access, automatic endorsement, past endorsement) constitute a reasonable heuristic but not a precise decision procedure. In borderline cases — an occasionally consulted notebook, a search engine used frequently but not automatically trusted — it is genuinely unclear whether the external element qualifies as a cognitive extension or merely a useful tool. The theory does not yet have the precision needed to resolve hard cases.

#### 26. [WARNING] The Dependency Risk in Cognitive Extension *(Line 458)*

> [!warning] The Dependency Risk in Cognitive Extension
> An underexplored implication of relying on extended cognitive systems is dependency and vulnerability. If Otto's notebook is part of his cognitive system, what happens when he loses it? Cognitive extension through technology creates new forms of cognitive vulnerability: device failure, data loss, network unavailability, and platform obsolescence are, on the extended mind view, not merely inconveniences but cognitive impairments. This dependency concern has practical implications for how we design extended cognitive systems and plan for their robustness.

> [!methodology-and-sources] Evidence Base for This Report
> The core claims of Extended Mind Theory are primarily philosophical and theoretical, grounded in thought experiments and conceptual analysis rather than controlled empirical research. The empirical evidence cited — Hutchins's ethnographic work on distributed cognition, neurological studies of tool incorporation, cognitive offloading research — provides convergent support but does not directly confirm the constitutive claims of the extended mind hypothesis. Most empirical research on cognitive offloading and environmental scaffolding is compatible with both the weaker embedded-cognition view and the stronger extended-mind view, and does not adjudicate between them. The theoretical framework is intellectually powerful and generative; its empirical status requires careful qualification. The research base draws disproportionately on WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic) populations, and cross-cultural investigation of cognitive extension practices is a significant gap.

#### 31. [DEFINITION] Extended Mind Theory (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) *(Line 511)*

> [!definition] Extended Mind Theory (Clark & Chalmers, 1998)
> The philosophical thesis that cognitive processes can genuinely extend beyond the boundary of brain and body into the environment. When an external element — a notebook, a tool, a digital device — plays the same functional role as an internal cognitive state, satisfying conditions of reliable availability, direct access, and automatic endorsement, it qualifies as a constituent part of the cognitive system. Distinguished from the weaker claim that the mind merely depends on or is causally influenced by environmental factors. The theory is a form of **vehicle externalism**: it holds not merely that the contents of thoughts are partly determined by the environment (content externalism) but that the physical vehicles of thought can extend into it.

#### 32. [DEFINITION] Parity Principle (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) *(Line 514)*

> [!definition] Parity Principle (Clark & Chalmers, 1998)
> The central argumentative tool of Extended Mind Theory: if a process performed outside the head would count as cognitive if it were performed inside the head (meeting standard criteria of cognitive status), then it counts as cognitive when performed outside the head. The principle is explicitly functionalist: cognitive status is determined by causal-functional role, not by material constitution or spatial location. It does NOT entail that all external processes are cognitive — only those satisfying functional equivalence with internal processes. Critics argue it commits the coupling-constitution fallacy; defenders argue it consistently applies functionalist commitments already accepted in cognitive science.

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Coupling-Constitution Fallacy (Adams & Aizawa) *(Line 517)*

> [!definition] Coupling-Constitution Fallacy (Adams & Aizawa)
> The alleged error of inferring, from the fact that two systems are causally coupled, that one thereby constitutes part of the other. Adams and Aizawa argue that Clark and Chalmers commit this fallacy: because Otto's notebook is causally coupled to his cognitive processes, they infer it constitutes part of his cognitive system. The objection insists that causal relevance and constitutive membership are distinct relations, and that establishing the former does not establish the latter. The mark of the cognitive — whatever distinguishes genuine cognitive processes from causally relevant non-cognitive ones — must be specified independently of coupling relations.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Mark of the Cognitive *(Line 520)*

> [!definition] Mark of the Cognitive
> The property or properties that distinguish genuinely cognitive processes from merely causally relevant ones. Identifying the mark of the cognitive is the central challenge posed by critics of Extended Mind Theory. Adams and Aizawa propose intrinsic, non-derived representational content as the mark; Clark rejects this criterion as theoretically unmotivated and argues that functional role provides the appropriate criterion. The debate about the mark of the cognitive has proved productive: it has forced more precise accounts of what cognitive status means.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] 4E Cognition *(Line 523)*

> [!definition] 4E Cognition
> An umbrella term for four related theoretical movements challenging Classical Cognitivism: Embodied cognition (the body shapes cognitive processes constitutively, not merely causally), Embedded cognition (cognitive systems are deeply adapted to and exploit environmental structures), Enacted cognition (cognition is brought forth through sensorimotor coupling, not representation of a pre-given world), and Extended cognition (cognitive processes can literally extend into the environment). The four Es are related but distinct: a theorist can accept embodied and embedded cognition without accepting the stronger extended cognition claim.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) *(Line 526)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The deliberate or habitual use of environmental resources to reduce demands on internal cognitive systems. Examples include writing lists to reduce memory load, setting alarms to support prospective memory, and using spatial arrangement of objects to encode task state. Cognitive offloading is empirically well-documented and clearly adaptive. Distinguished from cognitive extension: offloading accepts that the relevant cognitive work is happening (or no longer happening) internally; extension claims the work may be genuinely distributed across the organism-environment system.

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Distributed Cognition (Hutchins, 1995) *(Line 529)*

> [!definition] Distributed Cognition (Hutchins, 1995)
> The theoretical framework holding that cognitive processes are distributed across individuals, artefacts, and environmental structures in sociotechnical systems, and that the appropriate unit of cognitive analysis is the system as a whole rather than the individual mind within it. Developed through ethnographic study of real-world cognitive systems (naval navigation, airline cockpits, scientific laboratories). Convergent with but methodologically distinct from Extended Mind Theory: Hutchins focuses on social-material systems; Clark and Chalmers focus on individual organism-environment coupling.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Vehicle Externalism *(Line 532)*

> [!definition] Vehicle Externalism
> The position that the physical vehicles of mental states — the material processes that carry and implement cognitive states — can extend beyond the boundary of brain and body. Distinguished from content externalism, which holds that the contents of mental states are partly environmentally determined while the vehicles remain internal. Extended Mind Theory is the most radical form of vehicle externalism. Less radical forms hold that the body (rather than the brain alone) is the vehicle of cognition — a position associated with embodied cognition.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Active Externalism *(Line 535)*

> [!definition] Active Externalism
> Clark and Chalmers's term for their own position, emphasising that the environment plays an active, dynamic role in constituting cognitive processes — not merely a passive role as the source of inputs or determinant of representational content. "Active" contrasts with the "passive" externalism of Putnam and Burge, where the environment determines content but the cognitive vehicles remain fully internal. Active externalism claims the environment is a dynamic, interactive constituent of the cognitive process as it unfolds.

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Scaffolding *(Line 538)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Scaffolding
> Environmental, social, or material structures that support cognitive performance by organising, simplifying, and augmenting the cognitive work an individual must perform. Scaffolding can be temporary (faded as competence develops) or permanent (constituting an ongoing cognitive extension). The concept derives from Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development and has been developed in instructional design, where it describes structured support for learners. Extended Mind Theory provides a theoretical account of how scaffolding can transition from external support to genuine cognitive extension.

#### 41. [CITE] Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. J. (1998). The extended mind. *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19. *(Line 545)*

> [!cite] Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. J. (1998). The extended mind. *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.
> The foundational paper of Extended Mind Theory. Essential primary source; the brevity and accessibility of the original paper make it highly recommended reading. > Clark's most comprehensive development and defence of Extended Mind Theory, responding directly to Adams and Aizawa and other critics. > [!cite] Adams, F., & Aizawa, K. (2008). *The Bounds of Cognition*. Blackwell.
> The most sustained and influential critique of Extended Mind Theory. Develops the coupling-constitution objection in detail and proposes the mark of the cognitive as an alternative framework for demarcating cognitive systems. Essential for understanding the strongest objections to Extended Mind Theory and for appreciating the genuine difficulty of the constitutive question.

#### 44. [CITE] Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. MIT Press. *(Line 554)*

> [!cite] Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. MIT Press.
> Landmark empirical study of navigation as distributed cognition, providing detailed descriptive and analytical accounts of how cognitive work is distributed across persons, artefacts, and spatial arrangements in a real-world sociotechnical system. One of the most important empirical works in the broader 4E tradition. Supports the extended-mind framework from an ethnographic direction.

#### 45. [CITE] Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press. *(Line 557)*

> [!cite] Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.
> Foundational text of enactivism and the 4E cognition movement. Argues that cognition is enacted through sensorimotor coupling rather than computed over internal representations. Essential background for understanding the broader intellectual movement within which Extended Mind Theory sits, and for appreciating the differences between embodied, enacted, and extended cognition.

> [!cite] Maravita, A., & Iriki, A. (2004). Tools for the body (schema). *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 8(2), 79–86.

Provides direct neurological evidence for one form of cognitive extension — the extension of the body schema — that is less theoretically contested than the extension of belief and memory. Supports the broader plausibility of extended mind arguments by showing that extension into tools is literally happening at the neural level.

> [!methodology-and-sources] Research Grounding for This Report
> This report draws primarily on the philosophical literature on Extended Mind Theory and the 4E cognition tradition, with supplementary engagement with empirical research on cognitive offloading, distributed cognition, and tool incorporation. >
