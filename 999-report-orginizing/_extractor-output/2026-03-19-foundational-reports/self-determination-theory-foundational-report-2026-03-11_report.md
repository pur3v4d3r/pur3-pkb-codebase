#### 2. [DEFINITION] Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000) *(Line 302)*

> [!definition] Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000)
> A macro-theory of human motivation, personality, and psychological well-being that posits three universal basic psychological needs — for autonomy, competence, and relatedness — whose satisfaction versus thwarting by social environments is the primary determinant of intrinsic motivation, healthy internalization, psychological vitality, and well-being. SDT is constituted by six integrated mini-theories, each addressing a distinct aspect of motivated behaviour and its social-contextual determinants. It is distinguished from other motivational frameworks by its organismic dialectical metatheory, its treatment of motivation as a quality (autonomous vs. controlled) rather than merely a quantity, and its explicit integration of social context into the motivational architecture.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Basic Psychological Needs (Deci & Ryan, 2000) *(Line 305)*

> [!definition] Basic Psychological Needs (Deci & Ryan, 2000)
> Innate, universal psychological requirements whose satisfaction is necessary and sufficient for ongoing psychological growth, integrity, and well-being, and whose thwarting reliably predicts psychological fragmentation, ill-being, and defensive or rigid functioning. In SDT, the basic psychological needs are autonomy, competence, and relatedness. They are termed "basic" in that they are not learned or socially constructed but are inherent features of human organismic functioning, analogous in their universality to biological needs for food and water, though expressed and satisfied through culturally specific means.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Autonomy (SDT usage) *(Line 308)*

> [!definition] Autonomy (SDT usage)
> The psychological need to be the author of one's own behaviour — to act from a sense of volition, choice, and psychological freedom, with a sense that one's actions reflect one's genuine values and interests. Critically, SDT's autonomy is NOT equivalent to independence, self-reliance, or the absence of social influence. A person can act autonomously while following another's request or directive, provided they have genuinely endorsed it. Autonomy need satisfaction occurs when social environments provide volition, reduce unnecessary pressure, and support self-endorsed regulation. Its opposite — need thwarting — occurs under conditions of coercion, surveillance, controlling rewards, and deadlines that create perceived external pressure.

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Competence (SDT usage) *(Line 311)*

> [!definition] Competence (SDT usage)
> The psychological need to feel effective in interacting with the environment — to experience mastery, to grow in capability, and to exercise one's capacities successfully. Competence need satisfaction is not simply a matter of believing one is capable (as in [[Self-Efficacy Theory]]); it requires ongoing, actualised experiences of effective action. Optimal challenge, clear structure, and informational (non-evaluative) feedback are the primary social conditions supporting competence need satisfaction. Its thwarting occurs when tasks are persistently too difficult or too easy, when feedback communicates failure in a humiliating or dismissive way, or when social comparison undermines the sense of growing effectiveness.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Relatedness (SDT usage) *(Line 314)*

> [!definition] Relatedness (SDT usage)
> The psychological need to feel connected to others — to care for and be cared for, to feel a sense of belonging and genuine social integration. Relatedness need satisfaction does not require constant social interaction; it requires the felt sense that one is genuinely connected to, valued by, and belonging with others. It is thwarted by social exclusion, interpersonal hostility, indifference, and conditional regard (being valued only for one's performance rather than as a person). Notably, in SDT, relatedness operates somewhat differently from autonomy and competence — it provides a background condition of safety that supports the exploration and internalisation associated with the other two needs, but it is less directly implicated in moment-to-moment motivational regulation.

#### 7. [DEFINITION] Intrinsic Motivation *(Line 317)*

> [!definition] Intrinsic Motivation
> The performance of an activity for its own sake — because the activity is inherently interesting, enjoyable, or satisfying, independently of separable outcomes or external pressures. Intrinsic motivation is the prototypical form of autonomous motivation and represents the purest expression of self-determination. It is supported by social conditions that satisfy competence and autonomy needs simultaneously, and it is vulnerable to conditions that introduce controlling external contingencies (CET's central claim). Intrinsic motivation is not the same as autonomous motivation; the latter is a broader category that includes identified and integrated regulation.

#### 8. [DEFINITION] Extrinsic Motivation *(Line 320)*

> [!definition] Extrinsic Motivation
> The performance of an activity for instrumental reasons — because it produces separable outcomes (rewards, avoiding punishments, social approval, self-esteem). In SDT, extrinsic motivation is not monolithic: it varies enormously in its degree of self-determination depending on how much the individual has internalised and integrated the reasons for the activity. External regulation (pure compliance) and introjected regulation (compliance driven by internal shame or ego-involvement) represent controlled extrinsic motivation; identified regulation (personal endorsement of the activity's value) and integrated regulation (full assimilation of the activity into one's self-concept and values) represent autonomous extrinsic motivation with psychosocial properties closer to intrinsic motivation than to external regulation.

#### 9. [DEFINITION] The Organismic Dialectical Perspective *(Line 323)*

> [!definition] The Organismic Dialectical Perspective
> SDT's metatheoretical foundation — the view that human beings are active organisms with inherent growth tendencies (the "organismic" part) that continually interact with, and are shaped by, social environments (the "dialectical" part). The perspective holds that neither biology alone nor environment alone determines outcomes; instead, the continuous transaction between the person's inherent tendencies and the nutritive or impoverishing qualities of social contexts is the proper unit of analysis for motivational psychology. This perspective explains why SDT insists on studying both person and environment, and why it resists both nativist and social constructivist reduction.

#### 11. [KEY-CLAIM] CET's Core Claim *(Line 349)*

> [!key-claim] CET's Core Claim
> External events have two functional aspects: an **informational aspect** (conveying information about competence and effectiveness) and a **controlling aspect** (exerting pressure to behave in specific ways and shifting perceived locus of causality from internal to external). The motivational consequence of an external event depends on which functional aspect predominates as interpreted by the recipient. Events that function primarily as informational support intrinsic motivation by satisfying competence need without threatening autonomy. Events that function primarily as controlling undermine intrinsic motivation by shifting perceived locus of causality toward external and thus thwarting autonomy need.

#### 12. [KEY-CLAIM] The Internalisation Continuum *(Line 358)*

> [!key-claim] The Internalisation Continuum
> OIT proposes a continuum of regulatory styles ordered by degree of self-determination:
> 
> **Amotivation** — the absence of intentional regulation entirely; the person neither intrinsically nor extrinsically motivated, experiencing the activity as incomprehensible or beyond their influence.
> 
> **External regulation** — behaviour controlled by external rewards, punishments, or demands; the most controlled form of extrinsic motivation. Locus of causality is entirely external.
> 
> **Introjected regulation** — a partially internalised, but not genuinely owned, form of regulation in which the person acts to avoid guilt, shame, or anxiety, or to bolster ego-esteem and gain approval. The regulation has been taken in but not integrated with the self. This produces contingent self-esteem and emotional instability.
> 
> **Identified regulation** — the person has genuinely endorsed the value or importance of the activity, even if they do not find it inherently enjoyable. Locus of causality is substantially internal. Associated with persistence, flexible functioning, and reduced anxiety compared to introjection.
> 
> **Integrated regulation** — the fullest form of extrinsic motivation internalisation: the activity and its values have been assimilated into the person's broader sense of self, are congruent with other held values, and are endorsed without conflict. Functionally similar to intrinsic motivation but differs in that the activity is still performed for its valued outcomes rather than for inherent satisfaction.
> 
> **Intrinsic motivation** — behaviour enacted for the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself; the most self-determined form of regulation.

#### 13. [INSIGHT] Introjection and the Costs of Conditional Regard *(Line 375)*

> [!insight] Introjection and the Costs of Conditional Regard
> The concept of [[Introjected Regulation]] deserves special attention because it illuminates a phenomenon that is frequently mistaken for genuine motivation. A student who works obsessively to avoid shame, or who is driven by a terror of intellectual inadequacy, may appear highly motivated by conventional standards — they produce work, they persist. But SDT predicts, and considerable evidence confirms, that introjected regulation produces fragile, ego-contingent functioning: these individuals are more vulnerable to catastrophic responses to failure, more prone to self-criticism and depression, less likely to engage in deep rather than performance-oriented learning, and more likely to disengage when external evaluation is removed. The social condition that most reliably produces introjected regulation is [[Conditional Regard]] — the practice of giving warmth and approval contingent on performance, thereby tying the person's fundamental sense of relatedness to their outputs. This makes conditional regard one of the most motivationally harmful of all parenting and teaching practices, despite its frequent deployment.

#### 14. [KEY-CLAIM] The Universality Thesis *(Line 382)*

> [!key-claim] The Universality Thesis
> BPNT's universality thesis holds that the three basic needs are necessary psychological nutriments for all humans — not in the sense that all humans consciously value them or seek them explicitly, but in the sense that their satisfaction and thwarting have equivalent functional consequences across persons and contexts. Just as all humans need vitamin C regardless of whether they know it or can articulate the desire for it, all humans suffer from autonomy thwarting and benefit from autonomy support regardless of whether they would describe autonomy as a conscious goal. This claim has significant cross-cultural implications and has been the subject of substantial empirical investigation and debate (see Phase V).

#### 15. [KEY-CLAIM] Goal Contents and Well-Being *(Line 393)*

> [!key-claim] Goal Contents and Well-Being
> GCT predicts, and extensive evidence confirms, that placing high relative importance on extrinsic goals compared to intrinsic goals is associated with lower well-being, higher anxiety, more depression, and poorer relationship quality, even when absolute wealth or attainment is controlled for. The mechanism is that extrinsic goals are fundamentally need-non-satisfying: pursuing wealth or fame does not, in itself, satisfy needs for competence (mastery for its own sake), autonomy (genuine self-expression), or relatedness (genuine connection), even when those goals are attained.

#### 17. [COUNTER-ARGUMENT] The Measurement Challenge *(Line 419)*

> [!counter-argument] The Measurement Challenge
> A significant methodological concern across the SDT literature is that the primary measures of need satisfaction (the Basic Psychological Needs Scale and its variants), regulatory style (the Self-Regulation Questionnaire), and well-being are all self-report instruments administered to the same participants at the same time, creating substantial common-method variance that may inflate apparent associations. The cross-sectional dominance of the literature — particularly in educational research — makes causal inference problematic: it is often unclear whether need-supportive environments produce better outcomes, or whether students who are already more motivated and well perceive their environments as more need-supportive. The longitudinal and experimental literature is growing but remains smaller than the cross-sectional observational base.

#### 19. [KEY-INSIGHT] Need Satisfaction as a Process, Not a Trait *(Line 437)*

> [!key-insight] Need Satisfaction as a Process, Not a Trait
> A theoretically important implication of the nutriment model is that need satisfaction is a dynamic process, not a stable individual trait. The same person can experience high autonomy need satisfaction in one context (a democratic, rationale-providing classroom) and low need satisfaction in another (a high-surveillance, outcome-focused examination environment) within the same day. This context-sensitivity means that SDT's predictions operate at multiple levels simultaneously: within-person variability in need satisfaction across daily contexts is itself a predictor of daily fluctuations in mood, energy, and performance; between-person differences in average need satisfaction across contexts predict individual differences in well-being over time; and between-context differences in need support predict aggregate differences in motivation and outcomes across environments.

#### 20. [THOUGHT-EXPERIMENT] The Homework Problem *(Line 448)*

> [!thought-experiment] The Homework Problem
> Consider a twelve-year-old assigned mathematics homework they find utterly uninteresting. Three teachers respond differently. Teacher A says: "You need to do this because it will be on the test, and your grade depends on it." Teacher B says nothing about homework and is mildly disappointed when it is not done. Teacher C says: "I know this particular practice set isn't very interesting, and I want to explain why I'm asking you to do it anyway — the procedural fluency you build here makes everything we do next term significantly easier, and I've seen students who skip this step struggle later in ways that genuinely frustrated them. You don't have to agree with me on this, but that's my reason." OIT predicts that Teacher C's approach — providing a genuine, non-manipulative rationale, acknowledging the student's perspective, and refraining from threat — is most likely to produce identified (genuinely self-endorsed) regulation over time, even though none of the three conditions makes the homework intrinsically interesting. What would you predict would happen to each student's regulation style after a semester?

#### 21. [EXAMPLE] Controlling vs. Informational Language *(Line 457)*

> [!example] Controlling vs. Informational Language
> The functional significance of a message is not determined solely by its semantic content but by the extent to which it conveys pressure versus information. Compare: "You should practice more if you want to keep your grade up" (controlling — includes implicit threat, shifts locus of causality externally) with "I've noticed that students who practice the algorithmic steps daily in the first week find the creative problem-solving in week two much more accessible — something to consider as you plan your time" (informational — provides competence-relevant information, preserves choice). Both convey roughly similar content, but SDT predicts that the informational framing preserves autonomous functioning while the controlling framing introduces perceived external pressure that may erode intrinsic interest and shift regulation toward external or introjected modes.

#### 22. [OBSERVATION] The Dark Triad of Need Thwarting *(Line 470)*

> [!observation] The Dark Triad of Need Thwarting
> Vansteenkiste and Ryan (2013) proposed that chronic thwarting of all three needs produces a characteristic "dark triad" of psychological symptoms: controlled motivation (driven by pressures rather than values), defensive self-processes (ego protection, denial, attribution bias), and psychological ill-being (depression, anxiety, somatic complaints). Each symptom is predicted to be distinct in its aetiology (tied to specific needs) while jointly constituting the overall portrait of need-thwarted functioning. Importantly, this analysis implies that measuring only the absence of positive motivation indicators (engagement, interest, vitality) is insufficient for detecting serious need deprivation — one must also measure the presence of defensive and ill-being indicators.

#### 25. [BEST-PRACTICE] Designing for Autonomy Support in Education *(Line 491)*

> [!best-practice] Designing for Autonomy Support in Education
> The empirically supported elements of an autonomy-supportive instructional style include: (1) providing meaningful, non-manipulative rationale for assignments and requirements that students may not find inherently interesting; (2) acknowledging student perspectives, including negative ones, as legitimate rather than as obstacles to be overcome; (3) using informational rather than controlling language — "you might consider" rather than "you must," "here's what I observe" rather than "you have to improve"; (4) offering meaningful choices within appropriate structural constraints; and (5) minimising surveillance, controlling deadlines, and evaluative pressure where these are not pedagogically necessary. Note that none of these elements requires abandoning structure, standards, or intellectual rigour.

#### 26. [WARNING] Common Misconceptions in Applying SDT *(Line 510)*

> [!warning] Common Misconceptions in Applying SDT
> Several misconceptions pervade popular appropriations of SDT. First: **autonomy support is not permissiveness**. Providing structure, setting high standards, and maintaining accountability are fully compatible with autonomy-supportive practice; what matters is how these elements are implemented, not whether they exist. Second: **intrinsic motivation cannot always be created**. Many necessary activities are not inherently interesting, and attempting to make them interesting through gamification or entertainment can be less effective than facilitating genuine identified regulation through rationale provision. Third: **removing external rewards does not automatically restore intrinsic motivation** once it has been undermined — the underlying need conditions must be addressed. Fourth: **SDT does not predict that all rewards are harmful** — only tangible, expected, performance-contingent rewards for initially interesting activities reliably show the undermining effect.

#### 27. [IMPORTANT] WEIRD-Sample Dominance *(Line 515)*

> [!important] WEIRD-Sample Dominance
> The foundational experimental work establishing CET (the undermining effect of rewards) was conducted almost exclusively with North American and European undergraduate participants. Although cross-cultural replications using BPNT measures have substantially expanded the evidence base, the theoretical bedrock rests on a narrow population. The generalisability of specific experimental findings to populations in the Global South, across age groups from early childhood to late adulthood, and across non-WEIRD cultural contexts remains incompletely established.

#### 28. [IMPORTANT] The Self-Report Measurement Problem *(Line 518)*

> [!important] The Self-Report Measurement Problem
> The vast majority of SDT research relies on self-report measures of need satisfaction, regulatory style, and outcome variables administered to the same participants in the same study session. This creates substantial common-method variance that may artificially inflate apparent associations between predictors and outcomes. The use of behavioural outcome measures (persistence, performance quality, physiological indicators), experience-sampling methodology (which samples need satisfaction and motivation in real time across daily contexts), and informant-report designs offers some protection but is underutilised relative to the conventional survey methodology.

#### 29. [IMPORTANT] The Integrated vs. Intrinsic Distinction *(Line 521)*

> [!important] The Integrated vs. Intrinsic Distinction
> OIT proposes a distinction between [[Integrated Regulation]] and [[Intrinsic Motivation]] — the former occurring when an extrinsic goal has been fully assimilated into the self-concept, the latter when activity is pursued for its inherent satisfaction. In practice, this distinction is empirically elusive: psychometric studies consistently find that integrated regulation and intrinsic motivation items are highly correlated and often load on the same factor. Whether they are truly distinct forms of regulation with distinct functional consequences, or whether the distinction is theoretically meaningful but practically negligible, remains an open question in the SDT literature.

#### 30. [IMPORTANT] The Cultural Autonomy Debate *(Line 524)*

> [!important] The Cultural Autonomy Debate
> The claim that autonomy is a universal basic psychological need is the most contested element of SDT's universality thesis. Cross-cultural research by [[Chirkov et al. (2003)]] and others shows that autonomy support predicts internalization and well-being even in collectivist contexts, supporting universality at the level of need function. But critics note that the very salience and conscious valuation of autonomy varies systematically across cultures, that the measurement instruments were developed in WEIRD contexts and may not translate perfectly, and that the expression of autonomy through harmony and social role fulfilment in collectivist cultures may be conceptually distinct from individualistic autonomy in ways SDT has not fully theorised.

> **Internal PKB Connections**
> 
> This report on [[Self-Determination Theory]] connects to existing knowledge in the PKB across multiple nodes, each relationship enriching both directions:
> 
> - **[[Achievement Goal Theory]]** — SDT and AGT address overlapping phenomena through distinct but complementary lenses. AGT's mastery/performance goal distinction maps imperfectly but productively onto SDT's autonomous/controlled motivation distinction: mastery goals tend to predict more autonomous engagement, but the relationship is not deterministic. The key SDT contribution to this connection is the mechanism: AGT describes the goal orientation; SDT explains why mastery goals are motivationally superior (they are more autonomy-satisfying and less threatening to competence need). Together the frameworks generate richer predictions than either alone — particularly for the design of [[Motivational Climate Design]] environments that are both mastery-oriented and need-supportive.
> 
> - **[[Feedback Design for Autonomy and Mastery]]** — This node in the PKB already draws on SDT principles (the informational vs. controlling functional significance distinction maps directly onto CET's analysis of feedback). This report deepens and broadens that node by providing the full theoretical underpinning for the feedback design principles established there, and by locating those principles within the larger framework of need satisfaction. The [[Feedback Design for Autonomy and Mastery]] node can now be understood as a practical instantiation of CET and BPNT within a specific instructional context.
> 
> - **[[Metacognition & Motivation]]** — The relationship between SDT and metacognitive theory is under-explored but theoretically rich. Autonomous motivation is empirically associated with deeper, more metacognitively sophisticated learning strategies. The mechanism may be that autonomous motivation frees attentional and cognitive resources from self-protective monitoring (avoiding shame, managing evaluative anxiety) that controlled motivation consumes, making deeper processing and metacognitive reflection more accessible. Conversely, metacognitive competence may support identified regulation by enabling learners to articulate the personal relevance and value of learning activities.
> 
> - **[[Growth Mindset]]** — [[Carol Dweck]]'s growth mindset framework and SDT connect through their shared commitment to a theory of psychological flourishing, but their mechanisms differ in important ways. Growth mindset operates through beliefs about the malleability of intelligence; SDT operates through need satisfaction. The two frameworks make convergent predictions in many contexts (both predict that mastery-focused, low-pressure environments support better outcomes) but diverge in others: SDT predicts that a person with a growth mindset operating in a highly controlling environment will still show motivational costs, because the need for autonomy is thwarted regardless of intelligence beliefs. Integrating the frameworks may require specifying the conditions under which growth mindset and need satisfaction operate as independent predictors vs. mediators of each other.
> 
> The pattern of connections across these nodes reveals that SDT occupies a foundational mechanistic role within the motivational cluster of this PKB: it provides the "why" — the need-based mechanisms — that many adjacent frameworks describe at the level of pattern without fully explaining.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Amotivation *(Line 574)*

> [!definition] Amotivation
> The absence of intentional regulation — neither intrinsic nor extrinsic motivation is operative. In SDT, amotivation arises when a person does not perceive a connection between their behaviour and outcomes (attributing outcomes to luck, others' power, or uncontrollable forces), when they believe the activity is not worth attempting, or when they lack the perceived competence to engage. Amotivation is NOT the same as laziness or disengagement from all activity; it is contextually specific — a student may be amotivated in mathematics but highly motivated in art. It is predicted by chronic need thwarting, particularly competence thwarting, and by low perceived locus of causality.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Autonomy Support *(Line 577)*

> [!definition] Autonomy Support
> An interpersonal style characterised by taking the other person's inner motivational perspective as the reference point for interactions — acknowledging their feelings and perspectives (including negative ones), providing meaningful and non-manipulative rationale for requests, using informational rather than controlling language, and minimising unnecessary pressure and surveillance. Autonomy support is NOT equivalent to permissiveness: it is compatible with, and indeed requires, clear structure and high standards. The key is that standards and structure are implemented in ways that preserve the person's sense of being the origin of their behaviour. The opposite construct is the **controlling style** — pressure, surveillance, contingent rewards, ego-involving comparisons, and the dismissal of internal states.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET) *(Line 580)*

> [!definition] Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)
> The SDT mini-theory addressing the effects of external events on intrinsic motivation. CET proposes that external events affect intrinsic motivation through their impact on perceived locus of causality (internal vs. external) and perceived competence. Events have two functional aspects: informational (conveying competence information) and controlling (applying pressure to specific behaviours). The predominant functional significance, as perceived by the recipient, determines the motivational consequence. Controlling events undermine intrinsic motivation by shifting perceived locus of causality externally; informational events can enhance it by satisfying competence need without threatening autonomy.

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Conditional Regard *(Line 583)*

> [!definition] Conditional Regard
> A parenting or pedagogical practice in which warmth, approval, or affection is made contingent on the child/student performing in desired ways — provided when performance is satisfactory, withheld when it is not. SDT predicts that conditional regard produces introjected regulation (the child works to maintain love and avoid its withdrawal rather than from genuine self-endorsement) and is associated with lower authentic self-esteem, higher contingent self-worth, and more fragile, ego-contingent functioning. It is contrasted with **unconditional regard** — maintaining warmth and care as a baseline, independent of performance, while still providing honest feedback about performance quality.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Identified Regulation *(Line 586)*

> [!definition] Identified Regulation
> A form of extrinsic motivation in which the person has personally identified with and endorsed the value or importance of an activity, even if they do not find the activity inherently interesting. The locus of causality is substantially internal; the person acts because they genuinely value the goal, not because of external pressure or internal shame. Identified regulation is an autonomous form of extrinsic motivation and predicts psychological outcomes (well-being, persistence, learning quality) substantially superior to introjected or external regulation, though typically inferior to intrinsic motivation for affective outcomes.

#### 39. [DEFINITION] Introjected Regulation *(Line 589)*

> [!definition] Introjected Regulation
> A form of extrinsic motivation in which the regulation has been taken in but not genuinely integrated — the person acts to avoid guilt, shame, or ego-threat, or to gain approval and self-esteem. The regulation is experienced as an internal compulsion or pressure rather than as a genuine expression of self. Introjection produces contingent self-esteem, ego-involvement, heightened anxiety around performance, and vulnerability to catastrophic responses to failure. It is a controlled form of extrinsic motivation, despite involving no external contingencies, because its locus of regulation is an internal pressure rather than a freely endorsed value.

#### 40. [DEFINITION] Need Thwarting *(Line 592)*

> [!definition] Need Thwarting
> The active frustration of basic psychological needs — not merely the absence of need satisfaction but the presence of conditions that directly impede need expression and fulfilment. Need thwarting is empirically and theoretically distinct from low need satisfaction: it is predicted by specific aversive contextual features (coercion for autonomy, persistent failure/humiliation for competence, exclusion/conditional regard for relatedness), and it specifically predicts ill-being (depression, anxiety, aggression) rather than simply reduced well-being. This distinction has significant implications for intervention design: creating need-satisfying conditions and removing need-thwarting conditions are partially independent tasks.

#### 41. [DEFINITION] Organismic Integration Theory (OIT) *(Line 595)*

> [!definition] Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)
> The SDT mini-theory describing the internalisation continuum — the process through which external regulations are progressively taken in, transformed, and (ideally) integrated into the person's self-concept and value system. OIT proposes a qualitatively ordered sequence: external regulation → introjected regulation → identified regulation → integrated regulation → intrinsic motivation. The theory specifies the social conditions (rationale provision, perspective acknowledgment, minimal pressure) that facilitate healthy progression toward integrated and identified regulation for activities that may not be inherently interesting. Failure of healthy internalisation leaves the person in external or introjected regulation — compliant but fragile.

#### 42. [DEFINITION] Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC) *(Line 598)*

> [!definition] Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC)
> A construct developed by [[Richard deCharms]] and central to SDT: the extent to which a person experiences the source of their behaviour as internal (self-initiated, volitional) versus external (controlled by environmental forces, pressure, or obligation). An internal PLOC is characteristic of autonomous motivation; an external PLOC is characteristic of controlled motivation. PLOC is not a fixed trait but is dynamically influenced by features of the immediate social environment — surveillance, controlling rewards, threats, and evaluative pressure all shift PLOC externally, while rationale provision, choice, and acknowledgment of internal states shift it internally.

> [!methodology-and-sources] Research Grounding for This Report
> This report draws on the primary theoretical literature of SDT from 1971 to the present — principally Deci and Ryan's original texts, the major review papers, and the meta-analytic syntheses. It represents an academic synthesis rather than a contribution of original empirical data. The claims made about basic psychological needs, the internalisation continuum, and the effects of need satisfaction and thwarting reflect the weight of the empirical literature as established in multiple meta-analyses and cross-cultural studies; they are not the author's independent derivations.
> 
> The theoretical architecture presented — the six mini-theories and their relationships — reflects SDT as presented by Deci, Ryan, and their collaborators. Where the report characterises debates (the cultural universality question, the integrated/intrinsic distinction), it attempts to represent the current state of the scholarly discussion fairly, though the framing inevitably reflects the perspective of the SDT tradition itself. Readers seeking more critical perspectives should consult Heine (2008) on the cultural psychology critique and Danner et al. (2016) on measurement issues in BPNT research.
> 
> The empirical evidence base for SDT is substantially stronger in some mini-theories (CET, BPNT) than others (RMT, COT), and substantially stronger for North American and Western European populations than for Global South populations. Practitioners should calibrate their confidence in applying SDT principles in accordance with this uneven evidentiary landscape.
