#### 2. [DEFINITION] Deductive Reasoning *(Line 283)*

> [!definition] Deductive Reasoning
> Deductive reasoning is a form of inference in which a conclusion is drawn from premises in such a way that, if the premises are true, the conclusion *must* be true. The relationship between premises and conclusion is one of logical necessity — the conclusion is contained within, or guaranteed by, the premises. Deductive reasoning moves from the general to the specific: from universal rules or principles to particular instances or applications. It is fundamentally distinct from reasoning in which the conclusion is merely probable, plausible, or well-supported by evidence. **What it is NOT**: deductive reasoning is not the same as careful, rigorous, or thoughtful reasoning in general. One can reason carefully using inductive or analogical inference, and one can reason deductively in sloppy or invalid ways.

#### 3. [DEFINITION] Validity *(Line 288)*

> [!definition] Validity
> A deductive argument is valid if and only if there is no possible scenario — no logically conceivable world — in which the premises are all true and the conclusion is false. Validity is a formal, structural property of arguments; it says nothing about whether the premises are actually true. An argument can be perfectly valid while resting on wildly false premises, and the conclusion will then be guaranteed only conditionally: *if* the premises were true, the conclusion *would* have to be true.

#### 4. [DEFINITION] Soundness *(Line 291)*

> [!definition] Soundness
> A deductive argument is sound if it is both (a) valid and (b) actually has all true premises. Sound arguments guarantee true conclusions. Soundness is the gold standard for deductive arguments used to establish real-world conclusions, but evaluating soundness requires both logical skill (to check validity) and factual knowledge (to verify the premises).

#### 5. [DEFINITION] Modus Ponens *(Line 304)*

> [!definition] Modus Ponens
> *If P then Q. P. Therefore, Q.* This is the most basic and psychologically robust valid inference form. If the antecedent (P) is affirmed and the conditional link holds, the consequent (Q) must follow. Example: "If it is raining, the ground is wet. It is raining. Therefore, the ground is wet." Modus ponens is so psychologically natural that it is often described as an "inference ticket" — humans execute it automatically, without deliberate effort.

#### 6. [DEFINITION] Modus Tollens *(Line 307)*

> [!definition] Modus Tollens
> *If P then Q. Not-Q. Therefore, not-P.* This form denies the consequent to deny the antecedent. It is logically valid but psychologically more demanding than modus ponens, because it requires reasoning backward through the conditional. Example: "If it is raining, the ground is wet. The ground is not wet. Therefore, it is not raining." Modus tollens is central to falsificationist science: if a theory predicts a result and the result does not occur, the theory must be revised.

#### 11. [DEFINITION] System 1 / System 2 (Evans & Stanovich) *(Line 365)*

> [!definition] System 1 / System 2 (Evans & Stanovich)
> System 1 processes are fast, automatic, and largely unconscious — they produce rapid intuitive responses by pattern-matching current input to stored associations. In deductive tasks, System 1 often generates plausible but logically invalid responses based on belief or semantic content; valid deductive reasoning typically requires System 2 to override these responses.

#### 14. [THOUGHT-EXPERIMENT] The Multiple-Model Problem *(Line 394)*

> [!thought-experiment] The Multiple-Model Problem
> Imagine you are told: "None of the economists are historians. Some of the historians are philosophers." How many distinct states of affairs are consistent with these premises? You need to consider at least two: one in which some philosophers are economists, and one in which no philosophers are economists. A valid conclusion must hold across *all* possible models. This is the central challenge of multi-model syllogisms — it is not a failure of logical understanding but a failure of mental bookkeeping under [[Working-Memory|working memory]] load.

#### 16. [EXAMPLE] Belief Bias in Action *(Line 410)*

> [!example] Belief Bias in Action
> Consider: *All vegetables are healthy. Chips are not healthy. Therefore, chips are not vegetables.* Many people accept this as valid. But it is logically invalid: it commits the fallacy of denying the antecedent. The reason it *feels* valid is that the conclusion is true by general knowledge. Now consider: *All mammals are warm-blooded. Whales are warm-blooded. Therefore, whales are mammals.* Also invalid (affirming the consequent). But again it feels valid because the conclusion is true. Belief bias is not just an error of the uneducated — it is a systematic artifact of how the human mind processes information, requiring explicit metacognitive override.

#### 19. [BEST-PRACTICE] Worked Examples and Proof Schemes *(Line 441)*

> [!best-practice] Worked Examples and Proof Schemes
> The research literature on proof instruction supports several evidence-based approaches. Providing worked proof examples, particularly with annotations explaining the logical *necessity* of each step (not just its correctness), helps students build proof schemas — templates for the logical structure of different proof types. Classifying student "proof schemes" (Harel & Sowder, 1998) reveals a developmental progression from empirical schemes (proof by example) through transformational schemes toward deductive schemes, suggesting that instruction should scaffold this progression rather than demanding immediate formal reasoning.

#### 20. [BEST-PRACTICE] Explicit Attention to Logical Form *(Line 444)*

> [!best-practice] Explicit Attention to Logical Form
> Instruction that makes the logical form of mathematical arguments explicit — distinguishing the conditional structure of theorems ("if A, then B"), practicing both modus ponens and modus tollens in mathematical contexts (proof by contrapositive), and explicitly flagging common invalid forms — improves proof performance. Students who can identify the logical skeleton of a problem — the "if...then" structure, the universal quantification, the existential claim — are better positioned to construct valid arguments than those who reason exclusively about mathematical content.

#### 21. [EXAMPLE] Inquiry-Based Learning as Deductive Scaffolding *(Line 453)*

> [!example] Inquiry-Based Learning as Deductive Scaffolding
> Structured inquiry activities that require students to form explicit predictions before observing results scaffold the hypothetico-deductive method in a pedagogically accessible way. Explicitly asking students to state: "If my hypothesis is true, then I expect to observe X" — and then comparing observation to prediction — builds the conditional reasoning structure that underpins scientific inference. Activities that require students to explain *why* a disconfirming result refutes the hypothesis (rather than merely confirming that the experiment "didn't work") directly train modus tollens in a meaningful context.

#### 22. [WARNING] The Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent *(Line 464)*

> [!warning] The Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent
> One of the most prevalent and practically consequential deductive errors is affirming the consequent: reasoning "If P then Q; Q; therefore P." In science, this corresponds to "confirming" a hypothesis by finding that its predictions are true — but true predictions are insufficient proof of a hypothesis, since other hypotheses might predict the same results. In everyday reasoning, it corresponds to reasoning from a favourable outcome back to its assumed cause. Explicitly teaching this fallacy — and providing practice detecting it in realistic contexts — significantly reduces its incidence.

#### 23. [WARNING] Deductive Reasoning ≠ Correct Conclusion *(Line 467)*

> [!warning] Deductive Reasoning ≠ Correct Conclusion
> A pervasive misconception among novice learners is that a logical-looking argument that leads to a true conclusion must be valid, and conversely, that a valid argument must be leading somewhere they agree with. These confusions collapse the validity/truth distinction and make it impossible for students to evaluate arguments independently of their prior commitments. Instructional attention to cases of valid arguments with false conclusions, and invalid arguments with true conclusions, is essential for correcting this misconception.

#### 24. [IMPORTANT] The Transfer Problem *(Line 472)*

> [!important] The Transfer Problem
> Perhaps the most significant unresolved question for education is whether formal deductive training in one domain — mathematics, logic, philosophy — transfers to other domains. The claim for "formal discipline" — the idea that logical training in any domain strengthens reasoning in all domains — was historically the primary justification for formal logic instruction. The empirical evidence, however, is mixed at best. Studies of logic instruction show modest positive effects on reasoning in domains similar to the instructional context, but limited evidence of broad transfer. This does not mean logic instruction is valueless — domain-specific deductive competence is valuable in itself — but it cautions against overstating the general cognitive benefits.

#### 25. [IMPORTANT] WEIRD Sample Limitations *(Line 475)*

> [!important] WEIRD Sample Limitations
> The majority of laboratory research on deductive reasoning has been conducted with Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (WEIRD) populations, typically undergraduates at North American or European universities. Cross-cultural research, including foundational work by [[Luria]] on reasoning in non-literate populations, suggests that performance on formal deductive tasks is substantially shaped by schooling and cultural exposure to formal argumentation. Educators should be cautious about treating Western laboratory norms as universal developmental endpoints.

#### 30. [DEFINITION] Categorical Syllogism (Aristotle) *(Line 538)*

> [!definition] Categorical Syllogism (Aristotle)
> A deductive argument consisting of two premises and a conclusion, each of which is a categorical statement expressing an inclusion, exclusion, or partial overlap between classes. The medieval tradition identified 256 possible syllogistic forms, of which 24 are valid. The canonical example (*Barbara*) has the form "All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P." What it is NOT: a categorical syllogism is not any three-sentence argument — the premises must express class relationships and the conclusion must be entailed by the structure, not the content.

#### 31. [DEFINITION] Validity (Logic) *(Line 541)*

> [!definition] Validity (Logic)
> A structural property of deductive arguments: an argument is valid if and only if there is no possible scenario in which all premises are true and the conclusion is false. Validity is truth-preserving but not truth-generating — a valid argument with false premises may have a false conclusion. Validity is assessed by examining the logical form of the argument, independently of the actual truth values of its component propositions. What it is NOT: valid does not mean true, correct, convincing, or evidence-supported.

#### 32. [DEFINITION] Belief Bias (Evans, Barston & Pollard, 1983) *(Line 544)*

> [!definition] Belief Bias (Evans, Barston & Pollard, 1983)
> A systematic error in deductive reasoning in which the perceived believability of a conclusion influences judgments of argument validity. Reasoners tend to accept invalid arguments with believable conclusions and reject valid arguments with unbelievable conclusions. Belief bias is explained within dual-process theory as the intrusion of System 1's rapid evaluation of conclusion plausibility into the System 2 task of assessing logical structure. It is among the most robust findings in the psychology of reasoning, observed across cultures and education levels.

#### 33. [DEFINITION] Mental Models (Johnson-Laird, 1983) *(Line 547)*

> [!definition] Mental Models (Johnson-Laird, 1983)
> Internal representational structures that capture the states of affairs described by premises in a deductive argument. On Johnson-Laird's Mental Models Theory, deductive reasoning proceeds by (a) constructing one or more mental models of the premises, (b) drawing a putative conclusion that is true in the initial model, and (c) checking whether alternative models of the premises exist in which the conclusion is false. Reasoning difficulty correlates with the number of models required to represent the premises. What it is NOT: mental models are not visual images, though they may have visuospatial properties; they are semantic representations with formal structure.

#### 34. [DEFINITION] Modus Tollens (Classical Logic) *(Line 550)*

> [!definition] Modus Tollens (Classical Logic)
> A valid deductive argument form: "If P then Q; not-Q; therefore not-P." It denies the consequent of a conditional to deny its antecedent, reasoning backward from a failed prediction to a refuted hypothesis. Modus tollens is the logical basis of scientific falsification and mathematical proof by contrapositive. It is psychologically more demanding than modus ponens and reliably elicits lower accuracy rates in laboratory studies, particularly in abstract presentations.

#### 35. [DEFINITION] Pragmatic Reasoning Schema (Cheng & Holyoak, 1985) *(Line 553)*

> [!definition] Pragmatic Reasoning Schema (Cheng & Holyoak, 1985)
> A content-specific cluster of production rules organized around a recurring social or causal situation, particularly permission and obligation structures. Unlike formal logical rules, pragmatic schemas are activated by thematic content and encode the inferential patterns appropriate to that content domain. The permission schema (roughly: "if action A is to be taken, precondition P must be satisfied") explains why people reason correctly on permission-framed versions of the Wason Selection Task even without formal logical training.

#### 36. [DEFINITION] Dual-Process Theory (Kahneman, Evans, Stanovich) *(Line 556)*

> [!definition] Dual-Process Theory (Kahneman, Evans, Stanovich)
> A broad theoretical framework distinguishing two types of cognitive processing: System 1 (fast, automatic, associative, low-effort, resistant to conscious override) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, rule-governed, effortful, sensitive to instruction). Deductive reasoning is paradigmatically a System 2 activity; deductive errors — including belief bias and affirmation of the consequent — are often explained as failures of System 2 to override System 1's rapid, heuristically generated responses. Individual differences in deductive performance correlate with System 2 capacity measures (working memory, fluid intelligence) and with the disposition to engage System 2 (actively open-minded thinking).

#### 37. [DEFINITION] Hypothetico-Deductive Method *(Line 559)*

> [!definition] Hypothetico-Deductive Method
> The logical structure of empirical scientific inference, formalised by Karl Popper and others. A scientist proposes a hypothesis (H), deduces observational predictions from it (O: if H, then O), conducts an experiment, and interprets the result: a positive result is consistent with H (but does not prove it); a negative result (not-O) logically refutes H via modus tollens (not-O; if H then O; therefore not-H). The hypothetico-deductive method explains why experiments can falsify but not definitively verify scientific theories.

#### 38. [DEFINITION] Actively Open-Minded Thinking (Stanovich) *(Line 562)*

> [!definition] Actively Open-Minded Thinking (Stanovich)
> A measurable cognitive disposition characterised by the tendency to seek information that might disconfirm one's current beliefs, to weigh evidence on its merits rather than its source, and to revise conclusions in light of counter-evidence. Actively open-minded thinking correlates positively with System 2 engagement in deductive tasks and with resistance to belief bias. It is both a cognitive skill and an epistemic virtue, and is considered by Stanovich to be a key component of individual rationality.
