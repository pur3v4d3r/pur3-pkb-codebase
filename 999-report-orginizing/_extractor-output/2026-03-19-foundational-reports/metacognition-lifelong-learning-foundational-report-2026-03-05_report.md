#### 2. [DEFINITION] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 289)*

> [!definition] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979)
> Knowledge and cognitive activity that takes as its object, or regulates, any aspect of any cognitive enterprise. Metacognition includes both what one knows about one's own cognitive processes and products (metacognitive knowledge) and the monitoring and regulation one exercises over those processes during ongoing cognitive activity (metacognitive regulation). It does NOT refer simply to self-awareness in general, nor to reflection in a colloquial sense — it specifically concerns the cognitive enterprise of thinking, learning, remembering, and understanding.

#### 7. [EVIDENCE] The Testing Effect: Calibration's Most Powerful Corrective *(Line 340)*

> [!evidence] The Testing Effect: Calibration's Most Powerful Corrective
> Roediger and Karpicke (2006) conducted a landmark experiment comparing three study conditions: repeated study (SSSS), one study period followed by three retrieval practice sessions (STTT), and alternating study and test (STST). One week later, the retrieval practice condition (STTT) produced 50% superior retention compared to repeated study. Beyond the memory benefit, retrieval practice also improves metacognitive calibration: the errors produced during self-testing provide accurate diagnostic information about what has not been learned, countering the fluency illusion.

> [!counter-argument] The Limits of Growth Mindset Research
> Replications of Dweck's growth mindset intervention studies have produced mixed results, with several large-scale studies finding small or negligible effects on academic outcomes, particularly when the original experimental conditions are not closely replicated. The metacognitive implications of mindset research remain robust, but growth mindset interventions should not be oversold as panaceas for educational underachievement.

#### 11. [KEY-INSIGHT] The Monitoring-Control Distinction *(Line 382)*

> [!key-insight] The Monitoring-Control Distinction
> Monitoring and control are often conflated in everyday discussion of metacognition, but they are distinct processes with distinct failure modes. Monitoring is the assessment process — generating information about the state of one's knowledge. Control is the regulatory process — using that information to make decisions about how to allocate cognitive resources. A learner can have excellent monitoring (accurately perceiving what they do and do not know) but poor control (failing to act on that information by reallocating study time). Conversely, a learner can have energetic control behavior that is misdirected because monitoring signals are inaccurate. Both components must function for effective self-regulation.

#### 15. [BEST-PRACTICE] The Retrieval-Predict-Compare Cycle *(Line 430)*

> [!best-practice] The Retrieval-Predict-Compare Cycle
> A practical, evidence-based metacognitive protocol for any learning task:
> 1. *Before study*: Make an explicit prediction about what you know and do not know (EOL/prospective calibration)
> 2. *During study*: Periodically close your materials and attempt to retrieve key concepts without looking (retrieval practice, self-testing)
> 3. *After retrieval*: Check your recall against the source material. Mark errors and gaps explicitly
> 4. *Allocate study time*: Direct subsequent study toward gaps, not toward already-recalled material
> 5. *After a delay*: Repeat the retrieval test 24-48 hours later. The spacing produces superior retention and more accurate JOLs
> This protocol integrates retrieval practice, spaced repetition, calibration training, and study time allocation — four of the most robustly supported metacognitive tools — into a single workflow.

#### 16. [WARNING] The Experience Trap in Professional Development *(Line 447)*

> [!warning] The Experience Trap in Professional Development
> One of the most persistent and consequential metacognitive errors in professional life is the equation of experience with competence. Research consistently shows that professional experience does not automatically produce skill improvement — and in some domains, inexperienced practitioners perform as well as or better than highly experienced ones on standardized assessments. Experience produces improvement only when it is accompanied by accurate feedback that allows monitoring signals to be calibrated against reality. Without that feedback, experience merely calcifies existing approaches, accurate or not. This pattern — called [[pseudoexpertise]] in some research traditions — is particularly dangerous in domains where feedback is delayed, ambiguous, or absent.

#### 17. [IMPORTANT] The Limits of Introspection *(Line 460)*

> [!important] The Limits of Introspection
> A profound philosophical limitation on all metacognitive research is that introspective access to one's own cognitive processes is genuinely limited. Cognitive science has documented extensive cases in which people's explanations of their own behavior bear little relationship to the actual causal processes. This does not invalidate metacognition as a capacity — the monitoring and regulatory functions are real and consequential — but it does mean that metacognitive knowledge, however sophisticated, is always partial, potentially inaccurate, and subject to systematic distortions. Genuine metacognitive humility includes acknowledging the limits of one's own self-knowledge.

#### 21. [DEFINITION] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979) *(Line 518)*

> [!definition] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979)
> The domain of cognition concerned with knowledge about and regulation of one's own cognitive processes. Encompasses metacognitive knowledge (what one knows about cognition), metacognitive experiences (the conscious, affective feelings accompanying cognitive activity), and metacognitive regulation (monitoring and controlling cognitive processes during ongoing activity). Boundary condition: metacognition specifically concerns cognitive enterprises — thinking, learning, remembering, understanding — and is distinct from general self-awareness or emotional self-regulation.

#### 22. [DEFINITION] Calibration *(Line 521)*

> [!definition] Calibration
> The correspondence between a learner's confidence in their knowledge and their actual knowledge level, measured across a range of judgments. A perfectly calibrated learner is confident on items they know and uncertain on items they do not. Overconfidence (confidence exceeding accuracy) is the most common calibration failure. Boundary condition: calibration is a property of the relationship between judgment and performance over many items; a single confident judgment cannot be calibrated in isolation.

#### 23. [DEFINITION] Self-Regulated Learning (Zimmerman, 1989) *(Line 524)*

> [!definition] Self-Regulated Learning (Zimmerman, 1989)
> The active, cyclical process by which learners set goals, select and deploy learning strategies, monitor their own performance, and adjust their approach based on feedback. SRL is cyclical — each learning episode feeds back into subsequent forethought and planning. It is distinguished from externally regulated learning by the locus of control: the learner, rather than a teacher or curriculum, drives the regulatory process.

#### 24. [DEFINITION] Desirable Difficulties (Bjork, 1994) *(Line 527)*

> [!definition] Desirable Difficulties (Bjork, 1994)
> Conditions of learning that impair performance during practice but enhance long-term retention and transfer. Examples include spaced practice (distributing study over time), interleaving (mixing problem types), and retrieval practice (testing oneself rather than re-studying). The "desirable" qualifier distinguishes these difficulties from undesirable difficulties that impose cognitive load without benefit (e.g., unclear instructions, irrelevant complexity).

#### 25. [DEFINITION] Judgment of Learning (JOL) *(Line 530)*

> [!definition] Judgment of Learning (JOL)
> A metacognitive judgment made during or after study assessing how well particular material has been learned and how likely it is to be remembered later. JOLs are the most extensively studied metacognitive monitoring judgment. Delayed JOLs (made after a brief delay from study) are more accurate than immediate JOLs because they require actual retrieval rather than relying on current processing fluency as a proxy for memory.

#### 26. [DEFINITION] Feeling of Knowing (FOK) *(Line 533)*

> [!definition] Feeling of Knowing (FOK)
> The metacognitive sense that one knows something even though one cannot currently retrieve it — the experience underlying the "tip-of-the-tongue" phenomenon. FOK judgments influence the allocation of study time: high FOK items receive additional study on the assumption that retrieval will be unlocked. The reliability of FOK as a predictor of subsequent recall is moderate, influenced by both genuine partial encoding and superficial familiarity.

#### 27. [DEFINITION] Fluency Illusion *(Line 536)*

> [!definition] Fluency Illusion
> The tendency to interpret ease of cognitive processing as evidence of deep learning or durable retention. When material is recently studied, familiar, or clearly presented, it processes easily — generating a signal that metacognitive monitoring typically interprets as indicating good learning. In reality, processing fluency reflects short-term accessibility, not long-term retention. The fluency illusion is the primary mechanism behind the ineffectiveness of passive re-reading as a study strategy.

#### 28. [DEFINITION] Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978) *(Line 539)*

> [!definition] Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978)
> The distance between what a learner can accomplish independently and what they can accomplish with support from a more knowledgeable other. Vygotsky argued that learning is most productive within this zone — neither so easy as to require no effort nor so hard as to exceed capacity even with support. From a metacognitive perspective, identifying one's own ZPD requires accurate self-assessment of current capability and appropriate challenge calibration.

#### 29. [DEFINITION] Declarative, Procedural, and Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge *(Line 542)*

> [!definition] Declarative, Procedural, and Conditional Metacognitive Knowledge
> A tripartite taxonomy of metacognitive knowledge developed from Flavell's framework. Declarative metacognitive knowledge is knowledge *that* (e.g., knowing that spaced practice is more effective than massed practice). Procedural metacognitive knowledge is knowledge of *how* to implement strategies (e.g., knowing how to space study sessions using a retrieval practice protocol). Conditional metacognitive knowledge is knowledge of *when* and *why* to apply strategies (e.g., understanding that spaced practice is most beneficial for material requiring long-term retention rather than short-term performance). All three are necessary for effective metacognitive regulation.

#### 30. [DEFINITION] Deliberate Practice (Ericsson, 1993) *(Line 545)*

> [!definition] Deliberate Practice (Ericsson, 1993)
> A highly structured form of practice specifically designed to improve performance, characterized by: activities at the edge of current ability (requiring full concentration), immediate and accurate feedback on performance, the opportunity to correct errors, and the guidance of a coach or teacher who designs practice activities targeting specific weaknesses. Deliberate practice is distinguished from mere experience or play by its explicit metacognitive structure: it requires ongoing monitoring of performance against a clear standard and strategic modification of practice based on identified gaps.
