# Situation Models in Text Comprehension

## Phase I: Orientation & Context Setting

> [!ask-yourself-this]
> Before reading further: What do you believe happens in your mind when you read a complex text and feel you have understood it? How would you distinguish between remembering what a text *said* and genuinely understanding what it *meant*? Articulate this as precisely as you can before encountering the technical vocabulary that follows — the contrast will be instructive.

### The Comprehension Problem

Reading is the epistemically central activity of academic life, and yet its most important cognitive product is also its most frequently misidentified one. When readers report that they have understood a text, they are typically reporting one of two things: that they can recall propositions from the text (what it asserted), or that the text produced a subjective feeling of comprehension — the phenomenological sense that meaning has been grasped. Neither of these, as the cognitive science of reading has decisively established, is equivalent to genuine comprehension. The psychological construct that captures what comprehension actually is — and what it requires the reader to build — is the **situation model**.

The situation model is not a property of the text. It is not a mental copy of the text's content, nor an improved paraphrase of it. It is a mental simulation of the state of affairs, scenario, event sequence, or abstract domain that the text describes or discusses. The distinction is not merely terminological. A reader who can recall that a historical account stated "the treaty failed due to mutual distrust between the signatories" has encoded a proposition — a unit of the text's meaning-content. A reader who has constructed a situation model understands what such distrust would look like in practice, why it would be instrumental in treaty failure, what historical pressures might have produced it, and how this particular instance fits within a broader pattern of interstate negotiation dynamics. The second reader has built a simulation; the first has stored a summary. These are different cognitive achievements, and the difference between them is what the situation model framework is designed to theorize, measure, and support.

The intellectual lineage of the situation model concept traces primarily to [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|Teun van Dijk and Walter Kintsch]]'s landmark 1983 monograph *Strategies in Discourse Comprehension*, in which the authors proposed that a comprehensive account of discourse processing requires distinguishing three levels of mental representation. This tripartite architecture — surface code, [[Textbase|textbase]], and [[Situation-Model|situation model]] — became one of the most productive theoretical frameworks in the cognitive psychology of language comprehension, generating a research tradition that continues to be scientifically active more than four decades after its initial formulation.

### Why This Matters

The situation model concept carries consequences that extend well beyond academic reading research. For the practitioner engaged in disciplined self-directed learning — particularly one attempting to build a coherent [[Self-Regulated-Learning|self-regulated learning]] practice grounded in cognitive science — the situation model framework provides the diagnostic vocabulary for understanding what is happening when comprehension feels elusive, and the mechanistic basis for understanding why certain reading strategies are effective and others are not.

The core pedagogical implication of situation model theory is this: comprehension strategies that do not target situation model construction are likely to produce fluent-feeling but shallow understanding. The most common reading failure in sophisticated readers is not inability to decode text or remember its propositions — it is the construction of an elaborated [[Textbase|textbase]] in the apparent absence of a genuinely grounded [[Situation-Model|situation model]]. The reader knows *what* the text asserted but cannot explain *why* it is true, cannot deploy the knowledge in novel contexts, and cannot reliably distinguish this text's claims from superficially similar but subtly different claims encountered elsewhere. Situation model theory provides the mechanistic account of why this failure mode occurs and how it can be addressed.

The relationship between this framework and the broader PKB is architecturally significant. Situation model theory functions as a hub in the knowledge graph's reading comprehension subgraph — it provides the representational target that [[Working-Memory-in-Reading|working memory research]], [[Schema-Theory|schema theory]], [[Advance-Organizers|advance organizer theory]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect|self-explanation research]], and the [[Self-Regulated-Learning|SRL literature]] are all, from different angles, supporting and explaining. Understanding what a situation model is, how it is constructed, and what impedes its construction yields a unified account of reading comprehension that makes each of those related topics more coherent.

### Scope of This Report

This report provides a comprehensive treatment of situation model theory as applied to text comprehension, with three integrated emphases. The **theoretical foundations** receive rigorous treatment across Phases II through IV, covering the three-level representation architecture, the Construction-Integration model, the Event Indexing Model, the Structure Building Framework, Constructionist Theory, and the mechanisms of inference generation that underpin situation model construction. The **practical applications** — including detailed annotation protocols specifically designed to support situation model construction — receive extended treatment in Phase V and Appendix Section 7. **Situation model construction techniques** are integrated throughout the report and systematized in the Practical Protocols appendix.

Excluded from scope: computational implementations of the C-I model (which merit their own report); neuroimaging evidence for embodied simulation (noted but not surveyed systematically); reading development in children; and the literature on narrative transportation, which addresses aesthetic rather than comprehension dimensions of reading.

> [!key-claim]
> The situation model is the cognitive gold standard for reading comprehension. It is not what a text says — it is what the text is *about*. Constructing one requires generating information beyond what the text explicitly states, integrating new content with prior knowledge, and building a coherent mental simulation of the described domain. All effective reading strategies work, ultimately, by facilitating this construction.

> [!reflection]
> 1. Can you identify a text you have read recently where you had a strong sense of understanding that later proved to be shallow — where you could recall propositions but could not explain the mechanisms? What did that reading experience feel like from the inside?
> 2. What would it mean for a reading strategy to be "effective"? What would you need to be able to do after reading that you could not do before?
> 3. If the situation model is the target, how should the quality of comprehension be measured? What tests would distinguish textbase-level from situation-model-level understanding?

## Phase II: Conceptual Foundations

### The Three-Level Architecture of Text Representation

[[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|Van Dijk and Kintsch]] proposed that when a reader processes a text, they simultaneously construct representations at three distinct levels of abstraction, each with different structural properties, different functional roles in comprehension, and different dependencies on prior knowledge. Understanding the architecture of these three levels — and the cognitive operations that connect them — is prerequisite to understanding either why comprehension fails or why reading strategies succeed.

> [!definition] **Surface Code**
> The surface code is the verbatim linguistic representation of the text — the specific words, syntactic structures, and grammatical forms as they appeared in the original text. It is the most transient of the three levels of representation, decaying rapidly in memory under normal reading conditions. Readers retain surface code only for the most recently processed clauses; within a few sentences, verbatim memory for specific phrasing degrades precipitously while meaning-level representations persist. The surface code is functionally important during reading — it carries syntactic cues, emphasis, and stylistic markers — but it is not the target of comprehension.
>
> **Boundary:** The surface code is not what comprehension produces and is not what skilled reading targets. The ability to reproduce exact phrasing does not constitute evidence of understanding; conversely, the inability to remember exact wording does not indicate failure of comprehension. Sachs's (1967) classic experiments demonstrated that surface form is forgotten while propositional content persists even within seconds of processing.
>
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Working-Memory]], [[Reading-Fluency]]

> [!definition] **Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)**
> The textbase is a propositional representation of the semantic content of the text — the network of propositions that can be derived from the text through semantic analysis, stripped of surface linguistic variation. A proposition, in the technical sense employed here, is the smallest unit of meaningful content that can receive a truth value. It is represented as a predicate-argument structure: e.g., FAIL(TREATY) or CAUSE(MUTUAL-DISTRUST, FAIL(TREATY)). The textbase is a network of such propositions connected by coherence relations — reference (shared arguments), causation, contrast, and elaboration — that spans the semantic content of the entire text.
>
> **Boundary:** The textbase represents what the text *asserted*, not what the described situation *is like*. A complete textbase supports recall of the text's claims and can sustain answers to text-explicit questions. It does not, however, necessarily support the generation of text-implicit inferences, the application of knowledge to novel problems, or the explanation of mechanisms underlying described events. A reader with a rich textbase but no situation model can answer "what did the text say?" but struggles consistently with "what does this mean in practice?" or "why is this the case?"
>
> **Common Misconception:** The most consequential misconception in applied reading practice is conflating textbase-level comprehension with genuine understanding. The ability to summarize a text's propositional content — even in one's own words — does not demonstrate situation model construction if the summary merely recombines the text's explicit propositions without generating inferentially derived content. Paraphrase tests are textbase tests, not situation model tests.
>
> **See also:** [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Chunking]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

> [!definition] **Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983; Kintsch, 1998)**
> The situation model is a multidimensional mental representation of the situation, state of affairs, events, or abstract domain described or argued about in a text. It integrates information derived from the text with information drawn from the reader's prior knowledge, world knowledge, and episodic memories to produce a representation of the *referent world* — what the text is about — rather than the text itself. The situation model captures the causal structure, the spatial configuration, the temporal sequence, the agents and their goals, and the mechanisms underlying the described phenomena, organized into a coherent simulation.
>
> **Boundary:** A situation model is not identical to the reader's general prior knowledge activated by the text, nor is it identical to the text's propositional content. It is a newly constructed, text-specific representation that synthesizes both. Two readers with identical prior knowledge but reading different texts will construct different situation models; two readers with different prior knowledge but reading the same text will also construct different situation models. The situation model is always a product of the intersection of text and reader.
>
> **Historical Development:** The term was introduced by [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|van Dijk and Kintsch (1983)]] drawing partly on Johnson-Laird's contemporaneous concept of [[Mental-Models|mental models]] (1983). The two constructs overlap substantially — Zwaan & Radvansky (1998) note that "situation model" tends to appear in discourse processing research while "mental model" appears in reasoning research, but both refer to analogical, simulation-based representations of described states of affairs. The situation model concept has been further developed through the Event Indexing Model's five-dimensional structural account (Zwaan et al., 1995) and through embodied simulation accounts (Zwaan, 2004; Barsalou, 1999) that propose the simulation is implemented in perceptual and motor systems.
>
> **Research Status:** The situation model is one of the most extensively validated constructs in cognitive psychology. Converging evidence from reading time studies, think-aloud protocols, primed recognition experiments, inference probing paradigms, and neuroimaging consistently supports its psychological reality as a distinct level of representation beyond the textbase.
>
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Mental-Models]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch]]

### The Referential Shift: From Text to World

The theoretical significance of the situation model lies in what it represents: not the text, but the *world the text describes*. This referential shift — from a linguistic representation of the text's content to a simulational representation of the text's subject matter — is what separates genuine comprehension from sophisticated memorization, and it is what makes the situation model the appropriate target for cognitive theories of reading.

Consider a reader processing a technical explanation of how a centrifugal pump operates. A textbase representation of this passage would consist of propositions: PUMP(centrifugal), ROTATE(impeller), FLUID(enter, eye-of-impeller), CENTRIFUGAL-FORCE(move-fluid, outward), PRESSURE-INCREASE(as-fluid-moves-outward). A reader with only this textbase knows what the text asserted about centrifugal pumps. But a reader with a [[Situation-Model|situation model]] has built a dynamic mental simulation: they can mentally trace the path of fluid through the pump casing, anticipate where pressure differentials would be highest, predict what would happen if the impeller speed were doubled, and explain why the pump would fail if operated in reverse. This reader can answer questions the text never posed, troubleshoot pump failures the text never described, and explain the mechanism to someone who has never read the passage. The textbase has been superseded by a simulation.

> [!insight]
> The referential shift from textbase to situation model is cognitively analogous to the difference between memorizing a map's legend and actually developing spatial knowledge of the territory. The legend tells you what the symbols represent; spatial knowledge lets you navigate routes the map never depicted, estimate travel times between locations, and identify alternate paths when a route is blocked. Situation models are navigational knowledge. Textbases are legend knowledge. Navigation requires the former; examination performance often only requires the latter — which is why examination performance is a poor proxy for genuine understanding.

### Coherence: The Organizational Principle of the Situation Model

A central organizing concept for situation model theory is **coherence** — the property of a representation in which all elements are consistently integrated into a unified whole. [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|Van Dijk and Kintsch]] distinguish two levels of coherence that correspond, roughly, to the textbase and situation model levels of representation.

> [!definition] **Local Coherence**
> Local coherence refers to the connectedness of adjacent or nearby textual units — the degree to which each sentence or clause can be linked to its immediate neighbors through shared reference, causal connection, or explicit discourse connectors. Local coherence is maintained when the reader can track how the current sentence relates to the previous one. It is primarily a textbase-level property and is supported by surface cues: repeated nominals, pronoun referents, temporal connectors ("then," "subsequently"), and causal connectors ("therefore," "consequently").
>
> **Boundary:** Local coherence is necessary but not sufficient for situation model construction. A text can be locally coherent — each sentence following naturally from the last — while being globally incoherent, conveying no comprehensible overall situation.
>
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Model]]

> [!definition] **Global Coherence**
> Global coherence refers to the unity and consistency of the mental representation as a whole — the degree to which all elements of the constructed representation fit together into a unified, non-contradictory situation model. Global coherence is a situation-model-level property: it requires that the reader have integrated not just adjacent sentences but all relevant portions of the text with each other and with prior knowledge into a representation in which the described agents, events, causal sequences, spatial contexts, and temporal structure form an integrated whole.
>
> **Boundary:** Global coherence requires substantially more inferential work than local coherence, because it demands the simultaneous integration of text content with prior knowledge across multiple representational dimensions. When domain knowledge is insufficient to support the required inferences, global coherence cannot be achieved even when local coherence is fully maintained — a pattern characteristic of expert-difficulty texts read by domain novices.
>
> **Research Status:** Global coherence failures are the dominant comprehension failure mode for skilled readers dealing with unfamiliar domains. The interaction between text coherence and reader knowledge is one of the most robustly established findings in the applied reading research literature (McNamara & Kintsch, 1996).
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Schema-Theory]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]]

> [!ask-yourself-this]
> When you read a complex text and feel confused, is the confusion local (you cannot connect *this* sentence to *the previous* one — a textbase-level failure) or global (you cannot see how *this section* fits into an overall picture of what is being argued — a situation model failure)? These are distinct failure modes requiring different corrective strategies. Local coherence failures are repaired by careful re-reading of adjacent sentences; global coherence failures require prior knowledge activation, structural surveying, or targeted inference generation.

### The Integration Test: A Practical Diagnostic for Situation Model Quality

One of the most practically valuable implications of the three-level architecture is what might be called the **integration test** for situation model quality. The test is simple: can you answer questions about the described situation that were *not* explicitly stated in the text? The ability to generate correct answers to such questions — *Why would this be the case? What would happen if X changed? How does this mechanism account for Y?* — is diagnostic evidence of situation model construction, because it requires the generation of inferentially derived content that could only be produced from a simulation, not from textbase recall.

Situating this test within the PKB's existing knowledge structure: the integration test is essentially a depth-of-processing probe ([[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]]) applied to comprehension. Questions that require only textbase access target the shallow processing level; questions that require inference generation target the deep, semantically integrated level at which [[Metacognitive-Monitoring|metacognitive monitoring]] should be directed.

The converse is equally diagnostic. When a reader's ability to answer questions degrades rapidly as questions shift from text-explicit to text-implicit content, this indicates a rich textbase with a shallow or absent situation model — the characteristic profile of what cognitive scientists call **illusions of knowing**: the confident subjective sense of comprehension unsupported by genuine inferential competence. The [[The-Fluency-Illusion|fluency illusion]] is the reading-specific variant of this general metacognitive failure.

> [!reflection]
> 1. Consider a text you have recently read and feel you understood well. Apply the integration test: could you answer questions about the described situation that the text never posed? Could you predict what would happen under conditions the text never described? If not, what does this reveal about the level at which your comprehension operates?
> 2. What is the relationship between the quantity of propositions you can recall from a text and the quality of the situation model you have constructed? Why might these diverge systematically?
> 3. Which of the three levels — surface code, textbase, situation model — do most conventional reading comprehension assessments actually measure? What does this imply about the validity of such assessments as measures of genuine understanding?

## Phase III: Theoretical Landscape

### The Construction-Integration Model

The most influential theoretical formalization of situation model construction is Walter Kintsch's **Construction-Integration (C-I) model**, developed through a series of papers from 1988 onward and comprehensively presented in *Comprehension: A Paradigm for Cognition* (1998). The C-I model is a computational theory of text comprehension that explains how readers build coherent mental representations from texts, even when those texts are ambiguous, elliptical, or partially contradictory.

> [!key-claim]
> The C-I model proposes that text comprehension proceeds through two distinct phases: a fast, constraint-unconstrained **construction phase** that generates an initial, partially incoherent representation drawing on text propositions and weakly associated prior knowledge; and an **integration phase** that applies a constraint-satisfaction process to the constructed representation, suppressing elements that fail to cohere with the emerging interpretation and strengthening elements that do. These two phases operate cyclically across reading — at roughly each sentence or clause boundary — rather than as two sequential passes over the entire text.

In the **construction phase**, the reader activates propositions directly stated in the text (or derivable by straightforward inference from adjacent elements), together with associates from prior knowledge — including weak, context-inappropriate associates. This phase is rapid, bottom-up, and does not discriminate between appropriate and inappropriate knowledge activation. Kintsch's deliberately chosen example illustrates this vividly: processing "The notes were sour because the seams split" activates propositions relevant to musical performance *and* propositions relevant to sewing, because both are weakly associated with elements of the sentence. The construction phase generates what Kintsch calls an "ill-formed, multiply ambiguous, and partially incoherent" representation — essentially, a rich but unresolved activation cloud.

In the **integration phase**, spreading activation operates over the constructed representation subject to a constraint: propositions that cohere with many other propositions gain activation while propositions that cohere with few others lose activation. The result is a constraint-satisfaction process that settles into a coherent interpretation — the reading that best satisfies the network of associative constraints. In the musical/sewing example, the reading context will typically cause the musical interpretation to win the constraint-satisfaction competition, with sewing-related activations suppressed.

> [!insight]
> The C-I model has a counterintuitive but practically significant implication: knowledge that is "wrong" in the sense of being context-inappropriate is nonetheless *activated* during the construction phase. Comprehension breakdowns can therefore occur not because relevant knowledge fails to activate, but because the integration process fails to adequately suppress inappropriate associations — a pattern documented in poor comprehenders by Gernsbacher and colleagues. This mechanism also explains why expert readers in an unfamiliar domain sometimes construct worse situation models than expected: prior knowledge activates strongly but in ways that compete with rather than support correct interpretation of the new domain's claims.

The C-I model operationalizes text structure through **propositional analysis** — a formal procedure for decomposing text into propositions, each consisting of a predicate and one or more arguments. This enables quantitative predictions about reading time at individual words and phrases, recall probabilities for individual propositions (propositions appearing in many other propositions' argument slots — "high-argument-overlap" propositions — should be better recalled), and the difficulty of inference generation tasks. These predictions have been tested against substantial empirical corpora with considerable success, making the C-I model the most extensively validated computational account of discourse comprehension.

### The Event Indexing Model

[[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch|The situation model]] as a theoretical construct was substantially elaborated by Rolf Zwaan, Mark Langston, and Arthur Graesser through the **Event Indexing Model** (1995), subsequently extended by Zwaan and Radvansky (1998). Where the C-I model provides a process account of *how* situation models are built, the Event Indexing Model provides a structural account of *what* they contain — the dimensions across which the reader tracks the described situation.

> [!definition] **Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser, 1995)**
> The Event Indexing Model proposes that readers construct situation models as multidimensional event spaces, tracking information simultaneously across five situational dimensions: (1) **temporal** — when events occur and how they are ordered; (2) **spatial** — where events occur and how agents and objects are positioned; (3) **causal** — what events causally produce what other events; (4) **intentional** — what goals agents are pursuing and how their actions serve those goals; and (5) **protagonist/entity** — who is present, what their states are, and how their participation changes over the course of the text. Changes in the situation along any dimension signal an event boundary and trigger increased processing effort, indexed empirically by reading time increases at situational transition points.
>
> **Boundary:** The five dimensions were specified for narrative texts. For expository and argumentative texts, interpretive adaptation is required: the "intentional" dimension maps onto authorial argumentative goals and the goals of theorists discussed; the "causal" dimension maps onto explanatory and inferential connections rather than narrative event chains.
>
> **Research Status:** Each dimension has received substantial empirical support through reading time and recognition memory studies. Causal discontinuities produce the largest reading time increases, suggesting the causal dimension is the most cognitively central — consistent with Constructionist Theory's explanation assumption and with the foundational causal network research of Trabasso & van den Broek (1985).
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Textbase]]

The Event Indexing Model is particularly useful for diagnostic purposes. A reader who has lost track of the temporal dimension — who no longer knows when described events are occurring relative to each other — will show predictable deficits specifically on temporal inference questions while maintaining performance on spatial and causal inference questions. This dimensional specificity allows situation model failures to be diagnosed with more precision than a global "I'm confused" assessment provides.

Zwaan and Radvansky (1998) reviewed an impressive corpus of empirical evidence showing that reading times increase when situational dimensions change. Spatial shifts produce measurable increases; temporal shifts produce larger increases; causal discontinuities produce the largest increases of all. This hierarchical sensitivity strongly suggests that readers monitor causal structure as their primary coherence concern, with temporal and spatial structure as secondary concerns — a finding with direct implications for annotation strategy: causal annotations should be the priority, with temporal and spatial annotations as supplementary structure-building tools.

> [!ask-yourself-this]
> Consider a text you recently found difficult. Apply the Event Indexing diagnostic: which of the five dimensions is least represented in your current situation model? Are you unclear about the temporal sequence? The spatial configuration? The causal mechanism? The intentional logic of the actors? The identity and state of the key entities? Pinpointing the dimensional gap is more actionable than a general sense of confusion, because it directs targeted rereading and inference generation toward the specific missing dimension.

### The Structure Building Framework

Morton Ann Gernsbacher's **Structure Building Framework** (1990) provides a complementary account of situation model construction emphasizing the dynamic processes by which coherent representational structures are built and rebuilt during reading.

Gernsbacher proposes three fundamental comprehension operations. **Foundation-laying** occurs when the reader processes initial text elements and begins constructing the representational structure onto which subsequent information will be mapped. The foundation provides the organizing scaffold that guides integration of incoming content. **Mapping** occurs when incoming information coheres with the current structure — the relevant memory nodes are enhanced and the new content is integrated. **Shifting** occurs when incoming information fails to cohere with the current structure, prompting the reader to lay the foundation for a new substructure. Frequent structure-shifting — characteristic of poorly organized texts or of readers lacking sufficient prior knowledge to bridge between sections — fragments the situation model into poorly integrated sub-models.

The Structure Building Framework introduces the empirically critical concept of **suppression** — the active inhibition of initially activated but task-irrelevant information. Suppression is the mechanism that prevents the initial activation cloud of the construction phase from contaminating the coherent interpretation produced by the integration phase. Individual differences in suppression efficiency predict comprehension skill across multiple studies: readers with less efficient suppression are less able to inhibit competing interpretations, alternative word meanings, and off-topic associations, resulting in less coherent situation models even when their vocabulary and decoding skills are equivalent to better comprehenders.

> [!insight]
> The suppression mechanism has a practically significant implication for reading strategy. Strategies that increase the salience of task-relevant information — pre-reading structural surveys, scenario priming questions, active annotation of causal structure — may work partly by providing a richer set of constraints for the integration phase to operate on, making suppression of irrelevant activations more efficient. Strategies that do not provide such constraints — passive re-reading, indiscriminate highlighting — leave the integration phase with insufficient guidance, potentially resulting in incomplete suppression and a less coherent situation model.

### Constructionist Theory

Graesser, Singer, and Trabasso's **Constructionist Theory** (1994) addresses a question that the C-I model leaves underspecified: which inferences are generated *automatically*, in real time, during comprehension, and which require deliberate effort or post-hoc reflection?

Constructionist Theory is organized around three assumptions that jointly constrain inference generation during reading. The **reader goal assumption** holds that readers pursue particular comprehension goals (constructing a coherent situation model, extracting specific information, entertaining themselves) and that inference generation is modulated by which goals are active — a reader extracting information for a specific purpose generates different inferences than a reader seeking deep understanding. The **coherence assumption** holds that readers by default seek coherence at both local and global levels and will generate whatever inferences are necessary to maintain that coherence. The **explanation assumption** — arguably the most important for practical reading strategy — holds that readers seek causal explanations for described events and will automatically generate causal antecedent inferences explaining *why* events occurred.

> [!key-claim]
> Constructionist Theory's specification of *which* inferences are generated automatically is the theoretical foundation for the prescriptive claim that reading strategies should explicitly target elaborative inference generation. Causal antecedent inferences (explaining why events occurred) and goal-motive inferences (explaining what agents are trying to achieve) are generated automatically because they directly serve the coherence and explanation assumptions. But elaborative depth — the richness of the situation model beyond minimal causal coherence — is *not* automatic. It requires deliberate prompting. This is why passive reading by skilled readers produces situation models that are causally coherent but mechanistically thin.

From these three assumptions, Constructionist Theory derives empirically testable predictions: predictive inferences (anticipating what will happen next) are generated only for highly constrained, highly predictable continuations; descriptive elaborations (adding sensory or environmental detail not required for coherence) are not generated automatically; and background knowledge inferences (linking text content to world knowledge) are generated only when their absence would produce a coherence failure.

The practical prescriptions of Constructionist Theory are direct: if you want elaborative depth beyond minimal causal coherence, you must deliberately activate the explanation assumption by asking yourself "why is this so?" at each major claim. This is the [[Self-Explanation-Effect|self-explanation]] strategy, and the Constructionist Theory framework explains precisely why it works — it extends the automatic inference generation that the explanation assumption drives into the elaborative domain where it does not operate spontaneously.

### The Landscape Model

Paul van den Broek and colleagues developed the **Landscape Model** (van den Broek, Risden & Husebye-Hartmann, 1995) as a dynamic account of the fluctuating activation of text concepts across reading cycles. The Landscape Model treats situation model construction as an iterative updating process: at each reading cycle (roughly each sentence), some text concepts are highly activated (foregrounded), others are moderately activated (backgrounded), and others are deactivated.

The Landscape Model's key contribution is specifying what determines activation persistence. Text concepts remain activated — and are thus integrated into the developing situation model — when they are (1) causally connected to current events, (2) connected to protagonist goals, (3) mentioned in immediately preceding text (recency effect), or (4) highly activated by prior cycles (reinstatement). This hierarchical account explains the **foregrounding effect**: causal and goal-relevant information persists in the situation model with greater fidelity than descriptive information matched for surface frequency.

For the practitioner, the Landscape Model implies a specific vulnerability: information that is structurally important for global coherence but spatially distant from the current reading position may deactivate before the text that requires it arrives. Long texts with globally important information introduced early and elaborated only much later are particularly susceptible to this failure. Strategic rereading — returning to key sections when coherence failures appear — is the direct repair strategy this model prescribes, but prevention is preferable: the pre-reading structural survey (Phase V) is designed precisely to maintain activation of the text's organizing structure across the full reading.

> 1. The C-I model and Constructionist Theory both propose that reading involves automatic processes (construction phase; bridging inference generation) and non-automatic processes (integration phase; elaborative inference generation). What does this dual-process architecture imply about the *conditions* under which skilled reading fails — even in expert readers?
> 2. The Event Indexing Model proposes that causal discontinuities produce the largest reading time increases of any situational shift. What does this suggest about where, in an expository text, the most cognitively demanding processing is occurring — and therefore where comprehension monitoring and annotation should be most active?
> 3. The Landscape Model predicts that structurally important early-introduced information decays unless actively reinstated. What reading strategies exploit this prediction to prevent the decay?

## Phase IV: Mechanisms & Processes

### Inference Generation: The Engine of Situation Model Construction

The construction of a situation model depends critically on inference generation — the capacity to derive information that the text implies but does not state. Without inference generation, reading produces a textbase; with it, reading produces a situation model. Understanding the types, mechanisms, and determinants of inference generation is therefore the most mechanistically direct path to improving comprehension.

> [!definition] **Bridging Inference**
> A bridging inference is an inference generated to establish local coherence between adjacent or nearby propositions — to bridge an apparent gap in the text's explicit content that would otherwise produce a coherence failure. When a reader encounters "Mary put the cake in the oven. When the timer rang, she took it out and cut it," the bridging inference BAKED(cake) is required to make the second sentence cohere with the first: without it, the referent of "it" is ambiguous and the causal sequence connecting the two clauses is unclear.
>
> **Boundary:** Bridging inferences are generated automatically and obligatorily during skilled reading; they are the minimum inferential requirement for maintaining local textbase coherence. Their automatic generation constitutes evidence of textbase construction, not of situation model construction. A reader who generates only bridging inferences — sufficient to connect each sentence to its neighbors — has a locally coherent textbase, not a situation model.
>
> **Research Status:** Extensively supported by reading time paradigms; bridging inference generation is indexed by increased reading times at inference-requiring locations, with reliable effect sizes across multiple research groups.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

> [!definition] **Elaborative Inference**
> An elaborative inference is an inference generated to enrich the situation model beyond the minimum required for local coherence — adding mechanistic depth, causal explanations, spatial structure, or integration with prior knowledge that the text implies but does not provide. When a reader processing a description of enzymatic catalysis generates a mental image of molecular geometry, or when a reader of a historical account infers the political pressures that must have shaped a particular decision, they are generating elaborative inferences that directly instantiate situation model dimensions.
>
> **Boundary:** Elaborative inferences are not generated automatically as a default during reading. They require deliberate cognitive engagement, are strongly modulated by prior knowledge availability and reading goals, and will not appear spontaneously in the situation model of a reader who is processing the text passively. This is the primary mechanistic explanation for why passive reading — even by skilled readers — produces shallow situation models.
>
> **Common Misconception:** Elaborative inference generation is sometimes perceived as a sign of distracted or unfocused reading — that the reader is "adding things that aren't there." The opposite is true. Elaborative inference generation is the primary mechanism of deep comprehension; think-aloud studies consistently show that better comprehenders generate more elaborative inferences, not fewer.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]]

> [!definition] **Causal Inference**
> A causal inference is an inference specifying causal antecedents or consequences of described events — the *why* and *how* underlying the text's narrative or explanatory structure. Causal inferences are generated with higher priority and greater automaticity than other elaborative inference types, consistent with Constructionist Theory's explanation assumption. They are the primary mechanism through which the causal dimension of the situation model (per the Event Indexing Model) is populated, and their quality is the strongest single predictor of subsequent comprehension performance.
>
> **Research Status:** Among the most robustly established findings in reading research. Trabasso & van den Broek (1985) demonstrated that causal connectivity — the number of causal connections a text event has within the causal network — predicts recall probability more reliably than serial position or subjective importance ratings.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Schema-Theory]]

### Working Memory Constraints on Model Construction

The construction of a situation model imposes substantial demands on [[Working-Memory|working memory]] — the limited-capacity cognitive system that maintains information in an actively accessible state while simultaneously processing new information. The [[Working-Memory-—-Baddeley-Model|Baddeley working memory model]]'s architecture — central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and episodic buffer — provides the processing substrate within which situation model construction occurs, and the capacity limits of this architecture are among the primary determinants of comprehension quality under controlled conditions.

[[Working-Memory-in-Reading|Working memory capacity in reading]] predicts a substantial portion of variance in comprehension scores (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980; Just & Carpenter, 1992). The underlying mechanism is inferential: readers with higher working memory capacity can hold more of the currently constructed situation model in active representation while processing new text, enabling them to maintain long-distance referential connections, track multiple causal chains simultaneously, and integrate current content with information introduced much earlier in the text.

The **foregrounding effect** reflects working memory's selective maintenance of situationally relevant content. The situation model is not represented as a static stored structure but as a dynamically foregrounded representation in which the "current state" of the simulated situation is maximally active. Readers continuously update this foregrounded representation as the text advances — suppressing outdated state information and activating new state information — and this updating process itself consumes working memory capacity. When working memory capacity is insufficient for both processing new text and maintaining the full foregrounded situation model, the situation model is either simplified (fewer dimensions actively maintained) or fragmented (multiple sub-models rather than one integrated model).

> [!insight]
> The cognitive difficulty of constructing a situation model is inversely related to the quality and relevance of prior knowledge. When [[Schema-Theory|prior knowledge schemas]] provide rich, well-organized structures into which incoming text content can be rapidly assimilated, the demand on working memory is reduced — situation model construction work is partially offloaded onto long-term memory schemas. When prior knowledge is absent or poorly organized, every aspect of the situation model must be constructed from scratch within working memory's limited capacity. This is why [[Cognitive-Load-Theory|cognitive load theory]]'s concept of germane load — the cognitive effort devoted to schema construction and automation — is so relevant to reading comprehension: reading demanding texts builds schemas that subsequently reduce the cost of comprehending similar texts, expanding the effective working memory available for situation model construction.

### Situation Model Updating and Failure Modes

An aspect of situation model construction that introductory accounts often underemphasize is the **updating** process — the continuous revision of the situation model as the text introduces information that is inconsistent with or elaborates upon the current model. A situation model is not built once at the text's opening and retained unchanged; it is continuously updated, revised, and in some cases fundamentally restructured in response to incoming content. This dynamic quality is one of the most cognitively demanding aspects of reading complex texts.

When a text introduces information that contradicts an element of the current situation model — a character revealed to have misrepresented their intentions, a causal explanation superseded by a superior one — the reader must detect the inconsistency, locate the element that is affected, and revise it. Empirical research using text-change paradigms demonstrates that readers engage in this updating process but that revisions are often incomplete: elements of the original situation model may persist in memory even after explicit contradiction, producing a phenomenon sometimes termed **belief perseverance in comprehension** — the text's correction has been encoded at the textbase level but has not fully propagated to the situation model.

The primary failure modes of situation model construction that emerge from the theoretical landscape surveyed in Phase III can be systematized as follows:

**Coherence failure**: The reader cannot generate the inferences required to connect current text content to any element of the existing situation model. This is most common when prior knowledge is insufficient to support the required elaborative inferences, and it is signaled phenomenologically by the experience of reading words that are individually understood but collectively meaningless.

**Overspecification failure**: The reader constructs a situation model that is detailed in some dimensions (typically the most surface-salient ones — character descriptions, specific numbers, vivid examples) while sparse in others (typically the causal and intentional dimensions). This produces a situation model that feels rich but cannot generate mechanistic inferences.

**Integration failure**: The reader constructs multiple locally coherent but globally disconnected sub-models rather than a unified situation model. This produces the characteristic experience of "understanding each section but not the whole" — a pattern that indicates the global coherence connection work has not been done.

**Update failure**: The reader has encoded a later correction at the textbase level but has not propagated it through the situation model, leaving outdated information active in the simulation. This produces the specific error pattern of drawing inferences consistent with the original, incorrect information even after its explicit contradiction.

> [!reflection]
> 1. When you notice a comprehension failure while reading, can you classify it according to the failure mode taxonomy above? Is it a coherence failure (cannot connect new content to existing model), overspecification failure (detail but no causal structure), integration failure (sub-models that do not cohere), or update failure (old information not revised)?
> 2. The foregrounding effect implies that information important for global coherence but introduced early in a text may deactivate before later text that requires it. What reading strategies would prevent this? (Consider what it means to "reinstate" information.)
> 3. Why would reading under time pressure or while fatigued specifically impair situation model quality more than textbase quality? Which processes — bridging inference generation, elaborative inference generation, or suppression — are most demanding, and which would be most vulnerable to reduced cognitive resources?

## Phase V: Applications, Implications & Limitations

### Reading Strategies That Specifically Target Situation Model Construction

Understanding the mechanisms of situation model construction yields prescriptions for reading strategy that are more specific, and more theoretically grounded, than generic advice to "read actively" or "take notes." The following strategies are derived from the theoretical and empirical literature in the preceding phases and are specifically designed to support simulation-level comprehension rather than textbase-level memorization.

**Pre-reading situation model seeding** involves deliberate prior knowledge activation before engaging with the text body. Reading abstracts, introductions, section headings, and conclusions before processing the body text is not preview-skimming in any superficial sense — it is the construction of a low-resolution situation model that will guide inference generation and coherence maintenance during full reading. This strategy directly exploits the [[Advance-Organizers|advance organizer]] mechanism: a partially constructed situation model reduces the working memory demand of constructing a full one by providing organizational structure into which incoming information can be rapidly assimilated. The pre-reading pass should focus on identifying the causal skeleton — the main claims and the logical connectives between them — not on memorizing specific propositions.

**Scenario priming** involves formulating, before reading, a concrete scenario question: *What situation, mechanism, or event sequence is this text going to help me understand?* This question primes the comprehension system to construct a situation model rather than accumulate textbase propositions. It operationalizes Constructionist Theory's reader goal assumption — a reader with an explicitly active comprehension goal generates more situation-model-building inferences than a reader processing without an explicit goal.

**Post-section elaborative interrogation** involves, at natural section breaks, closing the text and generating a three-part narrative paraphrase: *This section is about X, because Y, which means Z.* The three-part structure — topic, causal grounding, implication — forces situation model articulation rather than textbase recall. A reader who can produce the first part (X, the topic) but not the second and third (Y and Z, the causal grounding and implication) has a textbase but not a functional causal situation model. The act of generating this paraphrase, not merely thinking it, activates the [[Self-Explanation-Effect|self-explanation effect]] — one of the most robustly replicated interventions in reading research, producing substantially better comprehension than re-reading in every study that has compared them directly.

### Annotation as Externalized Situation Model

Annotation — the practice of writing in or alongside a text — is best understood through situation model theory not as a note-taking or highlighting practice, but as the **externalization of the situation model itself**. Annotations that support comprehension are annotations that capture situation model elements: causal relationships, dimensional structure, inference-generating connections, outstanding questions that signal coherence gaps. Annotations that fail to support comprehension are annotations that capture textbase elements: restated propositions, highlighted striking phrases, underlined claims whose importance was felt but not articulated.

This reframing directly resolves the annotation threshold question. Do not ask: "Is this interesting enough to annotate?" Ask: "Am I at a situation model junction?" Stop and annotate when you are at a point where a situation model element has just been built, updated, questioned, or found deficient — when the annotation will stabilize an element of the simulation in external form. Do not annotate to demonstrate engagement with the text; annotate to externalize the simulation so that it remains accessible across the disruptions of ongoing reading.

> [!warning]
> **The Over-Annotation Trap**: The most common annotation pathology is not under-annotation but over-annotation — marking so much text that annotations cease to carry signal. Over-annotation typically co-occurs with fluency illusions: the reader feels that highlighting and marking demonstrate engagement, while this activity actually substitutes for the effortful inferential work that situation model construction requires. If annotation feels comfortable and automatic, it is almost certainly capturing textbase content rather than situation model structure. A useful heuristic: if you are annotating more than 10-15% of text content, you are annotating propositions, not architecture.

### Annotation Strategies for Situation Model Construction

The following annotation taxonomy is derived directly from the Event Indexing Model's five dimensions and the inference type taxonomy developed in Phase IV. It constitutes the **DIME Annotation Protocol** — an original synthesis of Event Indexing theory and annotation research.

> [!best-practice]
> **Causal Chain Annotations (C-dimension):** Use directional markers (→) to make explicit the causal relationships between described events, mechanisms, or claims. When a text implies causation without stating it, writing the causal annotation IS the bridging inference made explicit and externalized: "[Event/state A] → [Event/state B] because [your generated causal explanation]." Causal annotations directly target the dimension of the situation model that most strongly predicts comprehension quality and transfer.

> [!best-practice]
> **Dimensional Gap Annotations (T, S, I, E-dimensions):** When you notice that one of the Event Indexing dimensions is unclear — you have lost track of when, where, who, or why — annotate the gap explicitly rather than reading over it: "T?: unclear when this follows from [prior event]" or "I?: unclear why this agent is pursuing this goal at this point." Gap annotations make comprehension failures explicit and located, converting vague confusion into specific, addressable deficits.

> [!best-practice]
> **Integration Annotations:** When you recognize a connection between current content and earlier text, other texts, or prior knowledge, annotate the connection immediately with a linking label: "CONNECTS TO: [[relevant-prior-concept]]" or "CONTRADICTS: [prior claim]." These annotations externalize the lateral structure of the situation model — the connections between sub-models and between text content and world knowledge — which are the most fragile elements of an emerging situation model and the first to be lost under reading time pressure.

> [!best-practice]
> **Question Annotations:** The most powerful single annotation type for situation model construction. Rather than noting that something is interesting, note the question it raises: "WHY does this follow from the previous argument?" or "WHAT WOULD FALSIFY this claim?" or "HOW does this mechanism account for [counterexample you are generating]?" Question annotations activate elaborative inference generation in a way that assertive annotations do not — they create an obligation to generate the answer, which is the act of situation model construction itself.

> [!best-practice]
> **Compression Annotations:** After annotating a section, attempt to compress your marginal notes into a single sentence capturing the causal structure of the section. If the compression cannot be produced, your annotations captured detail, not structure. The compression annotation at the section end is the highest-level summary of your situation model for that section.

### The Annotation Threshold Decision Framework

When to interrupt reading flow for annotation can be formalized through a threshold framework derived from situation model theory. The threshold question is: *am I at a situation model junction?* Annotation is warranted when any of the following conditions obtains:

1. A **causal mechanism** has just been explained — the mechanism is the single most important element of scientific and theoretical texts, and its explicit notation prevents its submergence beneath subsequent propositional detail.
2. A **foundational distinction** has just been drawn that will bear weight throughout the rest of the argument — distinctions are the terminological infrastructure of theoretical texts and must be externalized because their conceptual precision erodes rapidly under reading.
3. A **coherence failure** has been detected — a dimensional gap in the situation model that cannot be immediately resolved through elaborative inference generation should be flagged rather than passed over, because unannotated gaps persist invisibly.
4. An **integration opportunity** has been recognized — a connection between current content and prior knowledge or earlier text elements that will not remain spontaneously accessible should be notated immediately, because the resonance mechanism that produced the recognition will not reliably reproduce it on review.
5. A **claim requiring unstated justification** has been made — when the text asserts without explaining, annotating "WHY?" makes the need for resolution explicit and prevents the false sense of coherence that unchallenged assertion can produce.

> [!warning]
> **Common Annotation Mistakes:** (1) Highlighting without commentary — highlighted text is a flag that content seemed important, not a record of why it mattered or how it connects to anything. (2) Annotating as a substitute for comprehension monitoring — marking a confusing passage without articulating what is confusing does not repair the situation model gap. (3) Single-pass annotation — the most effective annotation systems involve at minimum a structural first pass (seed the situation model) and a detailed second pass (annotate dimensions and connections); single-pass annotation typically results in textbase-level notes with minimal structural capture.

### Retention Through Situation Model Construction

Retention is a function of [[Levels-of-Processing-Theory|processing depth]] at encoding. Situation model construction is, functionally, the deepest available level of processing — it requires integrating new content with prior knowledge at a meaning-and-mechanism level, generating causal inferences, and building an organized simulation that distinctively encodes the described domain. This is why situation model construction is itself the best retention strategy: the depth of processing required to build a genuine situation model produces the elaborated, distinctively encoded memory traces that are most resistant to forgetting.

The [[Spaced-Repetition|spaced repetition]] infrastructure in the PKB complements situation model construction rather than substituting for it. But the quality of spaced repetition review depends critically on the quality of the original encoding. A flashcard that tests textbase recall ("What did van Dijk and Kintsch propose?") produces a weaker memory trace during retrieval practice than a card that tests situation model comprehension ("What is the causal mechanism connecting working memory capacity to situation model quality?"). The latter requires reconstructing a simulation — which is the same cognitive act as constructing the original situation model — rather than merely retrieving a label.

### Limitations of the Framework

**Domain knowledge dependence**: The quality of the situation model a reader can construct is bounded above by the quality and quantity of their relevant prior knowledge. For texts in entirely unfamiliar domains, active inference generation strategies will fail to produce deep situation models because the schemas required to support elaborative inferences do not exist. This limitation means situation model quality is not purely a reading strategy variable; it is substantially a domain knowledge variable, and strategy improvements will plateau without the parallel development of domain knowledge.

**[[WEIRD-Sample-Bias|WEIRD-sample limitation]]**: The overwhelming majority of reading comprehension research has been conducted with Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic samples — primarily university undergraduates in North America and Western Europe. The generalizability of the framework's quantitative claims (specific effect sizes for coherence manipulations, specific working memory correlations) to readers in different linguistic, cultural, or educational contexts remains underexamined.

**Individual differences in suppression**: The Structure Building Framework's emphasis on suppression efficiency implies that individual differences produce qualitatively different situation model construction patterns, not merely quantitative differences. This means comprehension strategy instruction may produce systematically different results across readers with different suppression efficiency profiles.

**Text-type specificity**: Much of the empirical foundation for situation model theory comes from narrative texts. The five Event Indexing dimensions were articulated in a narrative context, and their operationalization for expository, argumentative, and technical texts — the text types most relevant to academic learning — requires theoretical adaptation that has been less empirically specified.

> [!reflection]
> 1. Given the domain knowledge dependence limitation, what is the appropriate strategy for reading texts in completely unfamiliar domains? How should reading goals and annotation strategies be adjusted when situation model construction will necessarily be limited by absent prior knowledge?
> 2. The argument has been made that active reading strategies produce better situation models. But active strategies take more time. How should a reader balance the depth-speed tradeoff across different reading purposes (studying for mastery vs. skimming for relevance vs. reading for enjoyment)?
> 3. The annotation threshold framework prescribes annotating at situation model junctions. But how would a reader who does not yet have strong metacognitive monitoring skills recognize when they are at such a junction? What does this imply about the relationship between situation model theory and [[Metacognitive-Monitoring|metacognitive monitoring skill]]?

## Phase VI: Synthesis & Integration

### The Settled Core and the Productive Frontier

The theoretical landscape surveyed across the preceding phases reveals a convergent picture. Across the C-I model, the Event Indexing Model, the Structure Building Framework, and Constructionist Theory, the following propositions receive broad cross-theoretical support and constitute the settled core of situation model theory:

Text comprehension targets the [[Situation-Model|situation model]], not the [[Textbase|textbase]]. The textbase is an intermediate representation — necessary but not sufficient for genuine comprehension. The situation model is a multidimensional mental simulation constructed through a combination of text-driven and knowledge-driven processing, organized primarily around causal structure. Inference generation is the mechanism of model construction: bridging inferences are obligatory and automatic; elaborative inferences require deliberate strategy activation. [[Working-Memory-Capacity|Working memory capacity]] constrains model quality, but this constraint is partially offset by prior knowledge that provides organizational schemas. Active reading strategies that explicitly prompt elaborative inference generation, causal chain tracking, and explicit coherence monitoring produce better situation models than passive reading, even in skilled readers.

The productive frontier — where important questions remain open — is centered on the format of the situation model's implementation. The evidence over the past 20 years has moved strongly toward embodied simulation accounts: situation models appear to be implemented in perceptual and motor simulation systems, not only in propositional memory structures. This has implications for reading strategy (active visualization and motor imagery may specifically support situation model quality) and for understanding what "understanding" a text at the situation model level actually means neurobiologically.

### What This Gives the Practitioner

The situation model framework transforms reading from an opaque phenomenological activity into a cognitively tractable process. The practitioner who understands this framework has a specific vocabulary for diagnosing comprehension failures (coherence failure vs. overspecification vs. integration failure), a specific set of prescriptions for addressing them (scenario priming, causal annotation, elaborative interrogation, compression testing), and a principled understanding of why those prescriptions work — they target the specific cognitive mechanisms (elaborative inference generation, causal structure building, working memory management) that differentiate deep from shallow comprehension.

Perhaps most importantly, the framework provides a quality criterion for comprehension that is independent of subjective confidence. The integration test — can you generate correct answers to questions the text never posed? — is an objective behavioral measure of situation model quality that correlates poorly with the felt sense of understanding that passive reading produces. Applying this test systematically to one's own reading practice is the most reliable single mechanism for detecting and correcting the shallow-comprehension illusions that accompany textbase-level reading.

### Unresolved Questions

Several important questions remain contested or underspecified. The precise threshold for predictive inference generation — when does the reader anticipate upcoming content, and when does anticipation remain implicit — yields inconsistent results across studies. The relationship between [[Situation-Model|situation model quality]] and [[Transfer-of-Learning|transfer of learning]] is theoretically central but insufficiently studied empirically; the intuition that better situation models produce better transfer is well-motivated but not as well-evidenced as it should be. The neural correlates of situation model representations are being actively investigated with increasingly consistent evidence for somatosensory and motor cortex activation during comprehension of action and event language, but the full architecture of neural implementation remains to be characterized.

> [!connections-and-links]
> **Internal PKB Connections:**
>
> **[[Schema-Theory]]** — Schema theory provides the prior knowledge architecture that situation model construction draws upon. The C-I model's construction phase is the mechanism by which schema-based activation occurs; the integration phase is the mechanism by which schema content is assimilated into the emerging situation model. A reader's existing schemas determine which elaborative inferences are possible and which Event Indexing dimensions can be rapidly populated. Schema theory specifies *from what* situation models are built; situation model theory specifies *what* is built.
>
> **[[Mental-Models-Theory]]** — Johnson-Laird's mental models (1983) and van Dijk & Kintsch's situation models are closely related, near-contemporaneous constructs arising independently in reasoning and discourse processing research respectively. Both posit that comprehension produces analogical simulations rather than propositional copies of the described domain. Mental Models Theory was developed primarily in the context of deductive reasoning; Situation Model Theory in the context of text comprehension. Understanding both yields a unified account of how analogical representation supports both reasoning and reading — and explains why the same reader who constructs a good situation model from a text also tends to reason better about the domain the text describes.
>
> **[[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]** — The working memory constraints literature maps directly onto situation model construction: capacity predicts comprehension quality because the maintenance of the current-state situation model representation and the generation of elaborative inferences are both working memory-intensive operations. The [[Working-Memory-—-Baddeley-Model|Baddeley model]]'s episodic buffer is the most plausible locus for situation model maintenance, though this remains theoretically contested.
>
> **[[Advance-Organizers]]** — Pre-reading situation model seeding operationalizes advance organizer theory at the representational level. The advance organizer provides a high-level organizational framework that facilitates assimilation of subsequent content; the mechanism through which it operates is the reduction of working memory demand for situation model construction — the framework already exists, so incoming information can be mapped onto it rather than requiring construction from scratch.
>
> **[[Self-Explanation-Effect]]** — Self-explanation is the most empirically robust reading strategy that situation model theory predicts should work. The mechanism is direct: self-explanation prompts elaborative causal inference generation (the reader must explain *why*, not just encode *what*), directly instantiating the processes most critical for situation model construction. Chi et al.'s (1994) finding that self-explainers significantly outperform re-readers on inference questions provides the primary empirical validation of situation model theory's prescriptive claims.
>
> **[[Metacognitive-Monitoring]]** — Situation model construction requires continuous comprehension monitoring. The metacognitive monitoring literature specifies the detection mechanisms (comprehension monitoring signals, feelings of knowing); situation model theory specifies what is being monitored — the coherence and dimensional completeness of the emerging simulation. The DIME monitoring protocol developed in this report is the application of metacognitive monitoring theory to situation model-specific tracking.
>
> **[[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]]** — Situation model construction is, functionally, the deepest available level of processing — it requires integrating new information with prior knowledge at the mechanism and causal level, producing elaborated, distinctively encoded memory traces. The levels of processing framework explains *why* situation model construction produces better retention; situation model theory explains *what* deep processing actually consists of in the reading context.

### Appendix §1 — Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Situation Model (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)**
> A multidimensional mental simulation of the situation, event sequence, or abstract domain described in a text. The situation model represents what the text is *about* — the referents, causal structures, agents, goals, and mechanisms of the described world — rather than the text's propositional content. It integrates text-derived propositions with prior world knowledge through elaborative inference generation.
>
> **Boundary:** Not identical to prior knowledge, not identical to the text's propositions. A newly constructed, text-specific representation synthesizing both. Two readers with different prior knowledge will construct different situation models from the same text.
>
> **Common Misconception:** That comprehension is demonstrated by ability to paraphrase the text's content. Paraphrase demonstrates textbase access; situation model quality is indexed by the ability to correctly answer inference questions about content the text did not explicitly state.
>
> **Research Status:** Among the most extensively validated constructs in cognitive psychology; supported by reading time, recall, recognition, inference probing, and neuroimaging paradigms.
>
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Mental-Models]], [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Schema-Theory]]

> [!definition] **Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)**
> A network of propositions representing the semantic content of a text, abstracted from surface linguistic form. Each proposition is a predicate-argument structure capturing a minimal unit of assertable meaning. The textbase represents *what the text asserted*, organized by coherence relations.
>
> **Boundary:** Textbase construction is necessary but not sufficient for genuine comprehension. A complete textbase supports text-explicit recall but not text-implicit inference generation.
>
> **Common Misconception:** That fluent paraphrase of a text's content demonstrates deep understanding. Paraphrase is a textbase-level achievement.
>
> **See also:** [[Propositional-Representation]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

> [!definition] **Construction-Integration Model (Kintsch, 1988, 1998)**
> A computational model of text comprehension proposing two processing phases: (1) a construction phase in which text propositions and weakly associated prior knowledge elements are rapidly and non-selectively activated; and (2) an integration phase in which constraint-satisfaction spreading activation strengthens coherent elements and suppresses incoherent ones, producing a coherent interpretation. The two phases operate cyclically at each reading unit.
>
> **Boundary:** Primarily a process model of *how* situation models are built. Its structural account of *what* they contain is supplemented by the Event Indexing Model.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Textbase]], [[Working-Memory]], [[Schema-Theory]]

> [!definition] **Event Indexing Model (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser, 1995)**
> A structural model of situation models proposing that readers track information simultaneously across five situational dimensions: temporal (when), spatial (where), causal (why/how events connect), intentional (agent goals and plans), and protagonist/entity (who is present and in what state). Situational discontinuities on any dimension produce reading time increases, with causal discontinuities producing the largest effects.
>
> **Boundary:** Originally specified for narrative texts; application to expository texts requires adaptation — "intentionality" maps onto authorial argumentative goals; "causality" maps onto logical and explanatory connections.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]]

> [!definition] **Bridging Inference**
> An inference generated obligatorily to establish local coherence between adjacent propositions — to bridge a referential or causal gap that would otherwise produce a textbase-level coherence failure. Generated automatically during skilled reading; indexed by increased reading times at gap-requiring locations.
>
> **Boundary:** Bridging inferences establish textbase coherence, not situation model depth. Their automatic generation does not constitute situation model construction.
>
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]]

> [!definition] **Elaborative Inference**
> An inference generated to enrich the situation model beyond minimal local coherence — adding mechanistic depth, causal explanations, spatial structure, predictive content, or prior knowledge integration. Not generated automatically; requires deliberate strategy activation and is strongly modulated by prior knowledge availability.
>
> **Common Misconception:** That elaborative inference generation is a sign of unfocused reading. In fact, it is the primary mechanism of deep comprehension.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]]

> [!definition] **Local vs. Global Coherence**
> **Local coherence**: the connectedness of adjacent text units — whether each sentence links to its immediate neighbors. A textbase-level property. **Global coherence**: the unity and consistency of the situation model as a whole — whether all representation elements integrate into a non-contradictory simulation. A situation-model-level property. A text can be locally coherent throughout while failing to produce global coherence.
>
> **See also:** [[Textbase]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Schema-Theory]]

> [!definition] **Suppression (Gernsbacher, 1990)**
> The active inhibition of initially activated but ultimately context-inappropriate information during comprehension. The mechanism underlying the C-I model's integration phase. Individual differences in suppression efficiency reliably predict comprehension skill independently of vocabulary and decoding ability.
>
> **Boundary:** Suppression is active and measurable in real time (through primed recognition paradigms showing reduced accessibility of suppressed content); it is distinct from passive forgetting.
>
> **See also:** [[Working-Memory]], [[Automaticity]], [[Metacognitive-Regulation]]

> [!definition] **Constructionist Theory (Graesser, Singer & Trabasso, 1994)**
> A theory of inference generation specifying that causal antecedent inferences (explaining why events occurred) and goal-motive inferences are generated automatically during comprehension, while predictive and elaborative inferences are not. Based on three assumptions: reader goal (inference generation is goal-modulated), coherence (readers default to seeking coherent representations), and explanation (readers automatically seek causal explanations).
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]]

> [!definition] **Foregrounding Effect**
> The empirical finding that information causally relevant to current events and protagonist goals is more accessible in working memory during reading than other text content, even when the other content is equally important by global coherence standards. Reflects working memory's selective maintenance of the current-state situation model representation.
>
> **Research Status:** Well-established through multiple experimental paradigms; consistent with both the Landscape Model and Event Indexing Model predictions.
>
> **See also:** [[Working-Memory-in-Reading]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Working-Memory-Capacity]]

> [!definition] **DIME Annotation Protocol (original synthesis)**
> A reading annotation system derived from the Event Indexing Model's five situational dimensions, designed to externalize the situation model rather than the textbase. Annotations are organized around five types: Causal chain annotations (→ markers for causal relationships); Temporal gap annotations (T? for unclear sequence); Spatial annotations (S: for spatial configuration); Intentional annotations (I: for agent goals); Entity annotations (E: for state tracking). Includes compression annotations at section ends as quality checks.
>
> **Boundary:** Original synthesis by this report. Well-motivated by Event Indexing theory but not directly validated by annotation-specific empirical research.
>
> **Epistemic Status:** Well-motivated synthesis requiring empirical validation.
>
> **See also:** [[Situation-Model]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]], [[Writing-to-Learn]], [[Self-Explanation-Effect]]

### Appendix §3 — Conceptual Tensions & Open Questions

> [!tension] **Propositional vs. Analogical/Embodied Representation**
>
> **The Tension:** Whether situation models are propositional structures (enriched networks of propositions with situational specifications) or genuinely analogical/simulation-based representations implemented in perceptual and motor systems.
>
> **Position A — Propositional (Kintsch, 1998):** The C-I model treats situation models as formally propositional. "Simulation" is a useful metaphor, not a literal description of the representational format.
>
> **Position B — Analogical/Embodied (Zwaan, 2004; Barsalou, 1999):** The Event Indexing Model and embodied simulation accounts propose that situation models are genuinely simulation-based, activating perceptual and motor systems. Neuroimaging evidence (somatosensory cortex activation during comprehension of action sentences) supports the analogical position.
>
> **Current Evidence:** The embodied simulation account has gained substantially more support over the past 20 years. The scientific debate has shifted from "propositional vs. analogical" to "how much is simulation, and across which domains?"
>
> **Why It Matters:** The representational format has direct implications for optimal reading strategies. Analogical accounts suggest that active visualization and motor imagery may specifically enhance situation model quality.
>
> **See also:** [[Mental-Models]], [[Mental-Simulation]], [[Propositional-Representation]]

> [!tension] **Automatic vs. Strategic Situation Model Construction**
>
> **The Tension:** Whether situation model construction is primarily automatic (driven by default processes in skilled reading) or primarily strategic (requiring deliberate metacognitive intervention).
>
> **Position A — Primarily Automatic (Constructionist Theory):** Skilled readers automatically generate causal coherence-preserving inferences as a default; situation model construction is a by-product of skilled fluent reading.
>
> **Position B — Primarily Strategic (Self-explanation literature):** Chi et al. (1994) and related research demonstrate that even skilled readers construct substantially better situation models when using deliberate self-explanation strategies, indicating that automatic processes are not sufficient for deep comprehension.
>
> **Current Evidence:** A middle position is best supported: automatic processes produce a minimally coherent situation model; strategic processes are necessary to extend it to the depth required for inferential transfer.
>
> **This Report's Position:** Strategic processes are necessary for situation model depth that supports transfer; automatic processes establish minimum causal coherence but not explanation-level depth.
>
> **See also:** [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]], [[Automaticity]]

> [!tension] **Working Memory Capacity as Fixed vs. Functionally Expandable**
>
> **The Tension:** Whether WMC constraints on situation model construction are stable individual difference parameters or are effectively expandable through strategy development and domain knowledge acquisition.
>
> **Position A — Fixed:** The WMC literature has historically treated reading span as stable. Low-WMC readers are structurally disadvantaged.
>
> **Position B — Functionally Expandable:** Cognitive load theory's schema automation account and [[Verbal-Efficiency-Theory|Verbal Efficiency Theory]] suggest that increasing fluency and domain knowledge expands working memory *available* for situation model construction by reducing lower-level processing demands.
>
> **Current Evidence:** WMC itself shows limited trainability, but functional WMC available for situation model construction can be expanded through strategy instruction and domain knowledge development.
>
> **See also:** [[Working-Memory-Capacity]], [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]], [[Reading-Fluency]], [[Automaticity]]

> [!open-question] **Which Annotation Features Best Predict Situation Model Quality?**
>
> **The Question:** Is there empirical evidence establishing which specific annotation behaviors — causal marking, dimensional tracking, question generation, compression — most reliably predict situation model quality on inference testing and transfer tasks?
>
> **What We Know:** Self-explanation (a form of elaborative annotation) robustly improves situation model quality. Elaborative interrogation improves retention relative to re-reading.
>
> **What We Don't Know:** No study has directly compared annotation types (causal chains vs. dimensional flags vs. question annotations vs. compression annotations) for their specific contributions to situation model quality, controlling for time on task.
>
> **Why It Matters:** The DIME Annotation Protocol in this report is theory-derived and well-motivated but awaits direct empirical validation.
>
> **Research Direction:** A within-subjects reading study comparing annotation type conditions on inference posttests and transfer tasks would directly test the DIME protocol.
>
> **See also:** [[Self-Explanation-Effect]], [[Metacognitive-Scaffolding]], [[Writing-to-Learn]]

### Appendix §7 — Practical Application Protocols

> [!protocol] **Pre-Reading Situation Model Seeding Protocol (derived from C-I Model + Advance Organizer theory)**
>
> *Purpose: construct a low-resolution situation model before full-text reading, reducing working memory demands during the reading itself.*
>
> **Step 1 — Scenario Priming (2-3 min):**
> Write one sentence: *What situation, mechanism, event sequence, or domain is this text going to help me understand?* This is a scenario question, not a topic question. It should name the referent world, not the text's subject heading.
>
> **Step 2 — Structural Survey (5-10 min):**
> Read, in order: abstract or introduction, all section headings and subheadings, conclusion. Do not read body paragraphs. Goal: extract the causal skeleton — the main claims and the logical connectives between them.
>
> **Step 3 — Prior Knowledge Activation (2-3 min):**
> Write 3-5 sentences: what you already know about the described situation or domain, including what causes what, what the main mechanisms are, and what the main controversies are. This is your initial situation model.
>
> **Step 4 — Prediction Generation:**
> Write two specific predictions about what the text will argue, demonstrate, or claim. Predictions commit you to an existing situation model that the text will confirm, disconfirm, or complicate.

> [!protocol] **The DIME Annotation Protocol (derived from Event Indexing Model — original synthesis)**
>
> *Purpose: externalize the situation model across all five situational dimensions through targeted annotation of structure rather than content.*
>
> **The Five Annotation Types:**
>
> **C — Causal annotations (highest priority):** Use → to mark causal relationships. When the text implies causation without stating it, write the causal annotation as the generated inference: "[A] → [B] because [your causal explanation]." This is the most critical annotation type.
>
> **T — Temporal annotations:** Mark when the temporal sequence is unclear: "T?: unclear when this follows [prior event]." Or confirm sequence: "T✓: this follows [timeframe] after [prior event]."
>
> **S — Spatial annotations:** For texts describing physical mechanisms, spatial configurations, or structural relationships: "S: [brief spatial description or diagram]."
>
> **I — Intentional annotations:** Mark the goals and motivations of agents (narrative) or the argumentative purposes of authors/theorists (expository): "I: [Agent/author] is trying to [goal] because [your explanation]."
>
> **E — Entity annotations:** Track key actors' state changes: "E[Name]: [current state, changed from prior state]."
>
> **Quality check:** After each section, verify all five dimensions are represented (or deliberately absent for good reason). Unmonitored dimensions are the typical location of situation model gaps.

> [!protocol] **The Annotation Threshold Decision Framework**
>
> *Purpose: determine when annotation is worth interrupting reading flow.*
>
> **Stop and annotate when ANY of the following:**
> 1. A causal mechanism has just been explained — mark with C-annotation
> 2. A foundational distinction has been drawn that will bear weight throughout the text
> 3. A coherence failure has been detected — flag the dimensional gap explicitly
> 4. An integration opportunity has been recognized — annotate the connection immediately
> 5. A claim has been made that requires justification the text has not provided — mark "WHY?"
>
> **Do not annotate for:** Interesting but structurally peripheral content; examples and illustrations unless the example IS the argument; material already integrated from prior knowledge.
>
> **Target density:** 10-15% of text content annotated. If exceeding this, you are annotating propositions, not architecture.

> [!protocol] **The Section-Ending Compression Protocol**
>
> *Purpose: convert annotations into situation model elements; diagnose model quality at section boundaries.*
>
> **After each major section:**
>
> **Step 1:** Close the text.
>
> **Step 2 — Produce the three-part narrative:** "This section is about [X], because [Y], which means [Z]." X = topic; Y = causal grounding (why X is so, or why the author claims what they claim); Z = implication (what follows for the broader argument or for your understanding).
>
> **Step 3 — Diagnose:**
> - All three parts: situation model functioning for this section.
> - X but not Y/Z: textbase, not situation model. Reread targeting causal structure.
> - Cannot produce X clearly: coherence failure. Return to text; targeted rereading at section opening and closing.
>
> **Step 4:** Write the compression as a margin annotation at the section end. This is the highest-level situation model summary for this section.
