# Attribution, Self-Efficacy, and Motivational Inner Speech Vocabulary

## Phase I: Orientation & Context Setting

There is a conversation happening inside you right now. Not the one you are having with this text — a quieter, faster, far more consequential one that runs beneath conscious deliberation like a river beneath a frozen surface. When you encounter a sentence you don't immediately understand, a voice informs you of what that failure means. When a theorem resists comprehension, something narrates the resistance — and the narration, not merely the resistance itself, determines what happens next. This inner voice, drawing on a vocabulary assembled over years of experience, attribution, and self-conception, constitutes what [[Lev-Vygotsky|Lev Vygotsky]] called the operational medium of self-regulation: [[inner speech]].

The stakes of this inner conversation are not modest. [[Bernard Weiner]]'s forty years of research on [[Attribution-Theory|Attribution Theory]] demonstrate that the causal stories people tell themselves about outcomes — especially failure — cascade with mathematical predictability into emotional responses, expectancies, and effort decisions. [[Albert-Bandura|Albert Bandura]]'s equally extensive research on [[Self-Efficacy]] shows that the specific words learners use to appraise their capability do not merely describe an underlying belief; they partly constitute it, shaping the physiological arousal, attentional deployment, and persistence that determine whether difficult learning actually occurs. [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]], developed by [[Edward-Deci|Edward Deci]] and [[Richard-Ryan|Richard Ryan]], reveals that the regulatory language embedded in inner speech — the difference between *I choose to engage with this* and *I have to get through this* — shifts the entire motivational profile of a learning episode, affecting not just effort but curiosity, depth of processing, and long-term retention.

What unites these three research traditions is precisely the focus of this report: **vocabulary**. Not vocabulary as mere labels applied to pre-formed mental states, but vocabulary as a constitutive element of motivational cognition — words and constructions that shape the inferences drawn, the emotions generated, and the goals pursued, particularly in the context of reading and engaging with academically demanding texts such as those generated in this PKB's Foundational Reports.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Before You Begin**
> Before reading further, take a moment to notice what you say to yourself when you encounter a passage in an academic text that doesn't immediately make sense. What is the precise language of that moment? Do you reach for an ability word ("I don't understand this"), an effort word ("I didn't try hard enough"), a strategy word ("I need a different approach"), or something more global and dismissive ("this is too hard")? Your starting vocabulary is the object of this entire report.

**Scope of This Report.** This report covers: (1) the nature and regulatory function of [[inner speech]] during silent academic reading; (2) [[Attribution-Theory|Attribution Theory]] as a framework for understanding how causal vocabulary shapes motivation; (3) [[Self-Efficacy]] theory and the relationship between efficacy language and capability beliefs; (4) the integration of [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]] and [[Achievement-Goal-Theory|Achievement Goal Theory]] into a vocabulary of autonomous, mastery-oriented learning; (5) hierarchical goal language and its role in SRL's Forethought Phase; and (6) practical vocabulary resources, prompts, and templates for developing a more constructive inner dialogue during reading. The report does not cover general reading comprehension instruction, phonological processing in reading, or the broader topic of dialogue (social or internal) in non-academic contexts.

**Roadmap.** Phase II establishes the conceptual bedrock — what inner speech is, what attribution dimensions are, what self-efficacy is, and how the field distinguishes these. Phase III maps the theoretical landscape of motivational vocabulary: SDT's regulatory language, Achievement Goal Theory's framing effects, and Growth Mindset's vocabulary of expandable intelligence. Phase IV reveals the mechanisms — the causal chains through which vocabulary choices cascade into comprehension outcomes. Phase V translates all of this into practical strategies, while honestly mapping the limitations of the current evidence base. Phase VI synthesizes and integrates with your PKB.

## Phase II: Conceptual Foundations

### Inner Speech: The Self-Regulatory Medium

> [!definition] **Inner Speech (Vygotsky, 1934/1986)**
> The internalized form of private (social) speech that functions as a cognitive and self-regulatory tool; characterized by a condensed, predicative structure in which the subject is typically omitted and only the predicate — the dynamic element of the thought — is retained. Inner speech is not silent talking to oneself in full sentences; it is the cognitive residue of social language, compressed and reorganized for internal cognitive function.
>
> **Boundary:** Inner speech is distinct from *subvocalization* (the phonological activation of words during reading, which serves word recognition) and from *verbal thought* in its broadest sense. Inner speech specifically refers to the self-directed regulatory dialogue that accompanies deliberate cognitive work — planning, monitoring, evaluating. It is also distinct from mind-wandering or unbidden intrusive thought, though these may share neural substrates.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** Inner speech during reading typically manifests as evaluative commentary ("this is hard"), planning language ("I should re-read this"), emotional labeling ("I'm confused"), and self-efficacy appraisals ("I think I'm following this") — recognizable as a running metacognitive narration accompanying text processing.
>
> **See also:** [[Vygotsky]], [[Metacognition]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Self-Regulation]], [[Private-Speech]]

Vygotsky's account of inner speech places it at the center of the development of voluntary, self-directed behavior. In childhood, speech that begins as social communication — directed to others, structurally full — gradually becomes "private speech" (spoken aloud to oneself) and eventually inner speech (entirely internal). This developmental trajectory is not merely a change in channel; it represents a functional transformation. The speech that once coordinated activity with others now coordinates the individual's own cognitive processes. For the adult academic reader, inner speech is the medium through which metacognitive monitoring and regulation occur: the stream of interpretive commentary accompanying text processing.

Contemporary researchers, particularly [[Charles Fernyhough]], have extended Vygotsky's account. Fernyhough (2016) distinguishes between *condensed inner speech* (Vygotsky's predicative form — fast, abbreviated, not fully formed) and *expanded inner speech* (more sentence-like, deliberate, occurring during effortful problem-solving). For academic reading, both forms are relevant: condensed inner speech operates continuously as rapid self-monitoring, while expanded inner speech emerges during active problem-solving — when you deliberately work through a difficult passage or construct a summary. Crucially, even the condensed form carries motivational vocabulary — the abbreviated impressions that flash through awareness during reading ("makes sense," "lost," "boring," "I knew this") carry attribution and efficacy content, even when they do not surface as full propositions.

> [!insight] **The Predicative Compression Problem**
> Because inner speech in its natural form is compressed and predicative — retaining only the dynamic element of the thought — it tends to strip away context and nuance. When an academic reader encounters difficulty, condensed inner speech may register simply as "can't" or "lost" rather than the more differentiated "using the wrong schema for this argument structure" or "this concept requires prior knowledge I don't yet have." The motivational significance of this compression is enormous: a single predicate like "can't" bundles together what are, in Attribution Theory terms, radically different causal inferences — ability failures, effort failures, strategy failures, and task difficulty effects. Vocabulary development for academic reading is partly the project of *un-compressing* these predicates into their attribution-relevant components.

### Attribution Theory: The Causal Architecture of Motivation

> [!definition] **Causal Attribution (Weiner, 1972, 1985)**
> The process by which individuals assign causes to outcomes, especially unexpected or important ones. In Weiner's attributional framework for achievement contexts, the primary attributional targets are ability, effort, strategy use, task difficulty, luck, and teacher/peer assistance. The motivational consequences of attribution are not determined by the specific cause assigned but by that cause's location on three structural dimensions: locus of causality, stability, and controllability.
>
> **Boundary:** Attribution in this framework is not a post-hoc rationalization of behavior but a cognitive process that mediates the relationship between outcomes and subsequent motivation. It is distinct from simple self-concept (which is a generalized belief about the self) in that attribution is situationally triggered by specific outcomes and operates through specific causal narratives.
>
> **See also:** [[Attribution-Style]], [[Attribution-Retraining]], [[Bernard-Weiner]], [[Locus-of-Control]], [[Self-Efficacy]]

Weiner's three-dimensional taxonomy of causal attributions is the analytical core around which this report's vocabulary framework is organized. The first dimension, **locus of causality**, distinguishes internal causes (ability, effort, mood) from external causes (task difficulty, luck, teacher quality). The second, **stability**, distinguishes stable causes (stable ability, characteristic effort level) from unstable causes (fluctuating effort, transient mood, luck). The third, **controllability**, distinguishes causes the individual can control (effort, strategy use) from those they cannot (aptitude, task features set by others). These three dimensions, not the causal categories themselves, determine the emotional and behavioral consequences of an attribution.

> [!key-claim] **Weiner's Core Prediction: Attribution Dimensions Determine Motivational Outcomes**
> - **Locus** → determines *pride/shame* following success/failure. Internal attribution for success generates pride; internal attribution for failure generates shame.
> - **Stability** → determines *expectancy* for future outcomes. Stable attribution for failure predicts continued failure; unstable attribution for failure leaves future success possible.
> - **Controllability** → determines *guilt/anger* and the *judgments others make*. Controllable attribution for failure generates guilt (self-directed) and sympathy from others; uncontrollable attribution generates shame (self) and pity from others.

The motivationally most damaging attribution pattern in academic reading is the convergence of **internal + stable + uncontrollable** attributions for failure: "I can't understand this because I lack the ability for this kind of material, and ability doesn't change." This pattern is what Carol Dweck's [[Helpless-Response-Pattern]] research describes: it generates shame, low expectancy for future success, and withdrawal from effortful engagement. The vocabular signature of this pattern is the ability sentence: *"I'm not smart enough for this," "This is beyond me," "I've never been able to understand [X]."*

By contrast, **internal + unstable + controllable** attributions for failure are motivationally productive: "I didn't yet use the right strategy for this type of argument, and I can change my strategy." This generates neither shame nor learned helplessness — it generates guilt (in Weiner's technical sense: productive self-directed responsibility), maintained expectancy, and renewed effort.

> [!definition] **Attribution Style (Peterson & Seligman, 1984)**
> The tendency to consistently favor particular attribution dimensions across different situations; a relatively stable individual difference variable. A *pessimistic attribution style* — characterized by internal, stable, global attributions for failure — is strongly associated with learned helplessness, depression, and academic underperformance. An *optimistic attribution style* — characterized by external, unstable, specific attributions for failure and internal, stable, global attributions for success — is associated with resilience, persistence, and academic achievement.
>
> **Boundary:** Attribution style is a statistical tendency across situations, not a fixed trait. It is modifiable through [[Attribution-Retraining-Interventions]] and through deliberate vocabulary practice. It is also distinct from [[Locus-of-Control]] (Rotter), which is a unidimensional construct; attribution style encompasses multiple dimensions.
>
> **See also:** [[Learned-Helplessness]], [[Attribution-Retraining]], [[Growth-Mindset]], [[Optimism]]

### Self-Efficacy: Domain-Specific Capability Beliefs

> [!definition] **Self-Efficacy (Bandura, 1977, 1997)**
> The individual's judgment of their capability to execute a specific behavior or attain a specific level of performance in a given domain under given conditions. Self-efficacy is not a general trait (like global self-esteem) but a domain- and task-specific judgment that varies with context. Bandura identifies four primary sources: *mastery experience* (prior successful performance), *vicarious experience* (observation of similar others succeeding), *verbal persuasion* (encouragement and feedback from credible others), and *physiological/affective states* (bodily and emotional arousal interpreted as relevant to capability).
>
> **Boundary:** Self-efficacy is often confused with self-esteem (one's general sense of worth) and self-concept (one's descriptive beliefs about the self in a domain). Self-efficacy is strictly a *future-oriented capability judgment* — "can I do this?" — not a description of what one is or a global evaluation of worth. High self-efficacy does not guarantee performance; it regulates the motivation, effort, and persistence that make performance possible.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** In inner speech, self-efficacy manifests as future-oriented capability statements: "I can figure this out," "I don't think I can handle this level of abstraction," "If I take this step by step I'll get it." The temporal orientation (future/present) and the domain-specificity distinguish efficacy inner speech from attributional inner speech (which is retrospective and causal).
>
> **See also:** [[Albert-Bandura]], [[Mastery-Experience]], [[Self-Efficacy-Theory]], [[Social-Cognitive-Theory-—-Bandura]], [[Self-Reflection-Phase]]

Bandura's theory locates self-efficacy at the center of the self-regulation loop: efficacy beliefs determine whether an individual initiates a challenging task, how much effort they invest, how long they persist in the face of difficulty, and how they recover from setbacks. For academic reading, this means that the efficacy judgment brought to a dense theoretical text determines, before a single word is read, how much cognitive effort will be allocated, how difficulty will be interpreted, and whether comprehension monitoring will trigger re-reading or withdrawal.

The crucial mechanism for this report is the **verbal persuasion** source of self-efficacy. When inner speech functions as self-directed verbal persuasion — *"I've understood difficult material like this before; I can do this systematically"* — it constitutes a genuine efficacy-building input, not merely a motivational decoration. Conversely, when inner speech functions as negative persuasion — *"I'm losing the thread; this is just too dense"* — it contributes directly to efficacy erosion. The words are not epiphenomenal; they are functional inputs into the efficacy estimation system.

> [!reflection] **Deepening Your Understanding**
> 1. Consider the last time you encountered a genuinely difficult academic text. In retrospect, which attribution dimension did your inner speech primarily engage — locus (was this about *me* or *the text*?), stability (did this feel permanent or temporary?), or controllability (did you feel you had tools to address the difficulty)? How did that framing shape what you did next?
> 2. The distinction between attribution (retrospective causal inference) and self-efficacy (prospective capability judgment) is subtle but important. Can you articulate in your own words what different cognitive work each does in the moment of encountering difficulty?
> 3. If inner speech is predicative and compressed by default, what would it mean to *expand* your inner speech deliberately? What would need to change for that to become habitual?

## Phase III: Theoretical Landscape

### The SDT Lens: Autonomous vs. Controlled Regulatory Language

[[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]] offers a framework that is, in its practical application, fundamentally a theory of the vocabulary of self-regulation. The [[Internalization-Continuum]] — from external regulation through introjection, identification, and integration to intrinsic motivation — is not merely a taxonomy of motivational states. It is a taxonomy of the *language* through which regulation is experienced and expressed. Each position on the continuum has a characteristic inner speech register, and the register used during self-directed learning is not merely a symptom of motivational quality — it influences motivational quality through the mechanisms SDT articulates.

> [!key-claim] **SDT's Regulatory Language Taxonomy in Inner Speech**
> - **External regulation inner speech**: "I have to read this. I'll be behind if I don't. I should just get it done." — The regulation is experienced as wholly external; the words express compliance, obligation, and contingency on external outcomes.
> - **Introjected regulation inner speech**: "I should understand this; I'll feel stupid if I don't. I can't let myself fall behind. I need to prove I can handle this." — The regulation has been partially internalized but as ego-involvement and shame-avoidance; the characteristic words are *should, must, ought to, have to*, and self-critical appraisals.
> - **Identified regulation inner speech**: "I want to understand this because it connects to my SRL project. This matters for what I'm trying to build." — Chosen, instrumentally valued; the characteristic words are *want to, choosing to, because it matters for [goal].*
> - **Integrated regulation inner speech**: "Understanding how attribution works is part of who I am as a deliberate learner. This is exactly the kind of thinking I do." — The activity is experienced as an expression of core identity; the characteristic words invoke identity, values, and intrinsic coherence.
> - **Intrinsic motivation inner speech**: "This is genuinely interesting. I want to follow this argument wherever it goes." — Pure interest and enjoyment; minimal regulatory language because the activity generates its own momentum.

The critical SDT insight for this report is that introjection — the most common form of "self-motivation" in academic contexts — is **motivationally toxic in the way it resembles external control**. The learner who reads because they'll feel stupid if they don't has not achieved autonomous motivation; they have installed an internal controller that punishes non-compliance with shame. The inner speech of introjection is recognizable by its *should/must* constructions, its self-critical commentary, and its pressure-based urgency. Vansteenkiste, Lens, and Deci (2006) have demonstrated that controlled motivation, even when internalized as introjection, undermines [[Deep-Processing]], creative thinking, and long-term retention — precisely the outcomes that matter most for the kind of academic reading this PKB is built around.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Identifying Your Regulatory Register**
> Before continuing, spend sixty seconds reading the most difficult section of this report, attending not to comprehension but to the motivational register of your inner speech. What proportion of your self-directed commentary takes a *should/must/have to* form (introjection)? What proportion takes a *want to/choosing to/because it matters* form (identification)? What proportion is pure content-engagement with no regulatory commentary at all (intrinsic)? This diagnostic is worth more than any external assessment of your motivation.

### Achievement Goal Theory: The Grammar of Goal Pursuit

[[Achievement-Goal-Theory]], developed across the foundational contributions of [[John-G.-Nicholls]], [[Carol-Dweck]], and [[Carole-Ames]], distinguishes primarily between [[Mastery-Goal-Orientation]] (learning goals; the goal is to develop competence, understand deeply, and improve) and [[Performance-Goal-Orientation]] (ego goals; the goal is to demonstrate competence relative to others or avoid demonstrating incompetence). In [[Elliot and McGregor]]'s 2×2 refinement, the performance dimension is further divided into approach (demonstrate high ability) and avoidance (avoid appearing incompetent), and the mastery dimension into mastery-approach (develop competence) and mastery-avoidance (avoid losing competence).

The goal orientation literature is directly relevant to inner speech vocabulary because goal orientations function partly *through* the language used to construct and pursue goals. A [[Mastery-Goal]] generates one characteristic inner speech register during difficulty; a [[Performance-Avoidance-Goal]] generates another. The difference is not merely in how difficulty *feels* but in what difficulty *means* in each register — which in turn determines what self-regulatory action is taken.

> [!key-claim] **Goal Orientation and Difficulty Interpretation**
> When difficulty is encountered during reading:
> - **Mastery-approach inner speech**: "I don't understand this yet. What is it about this argument that's resisting my current schema? What do I need to learn to follow this?" — Difficulty is information about what remains to be learned.
> - **Performance-approach inner speech**: "I should be able to get this. Am I understanding this as well as I should? Would someone reading this alongside me understand it faster?" — Difficulty is a potential competence signal; triggers normative comparison.
> - **Performance-avoidance inner speech**: "If I don't understand this, it means I'm not smart enough for this kind of work. I shouldn't be engaging with this if I can't handle it." — Difficulty is evidence of inadequacy; triggers withdrawal or surface processing.
> - **Mastery-avoidance inner speech**: "I understood this kind of argument better before. I'm losing my grip on this. I must not let my comprehension slip." — Difficulty signals regression; triggers anxiety-based effort.

The implication is that the same objective difficulty — an opaque sentence, an unfamiliar theoretical construction, a dense argument — is *motivationally metabolized* differently depending on the goal orientation active during reading, and goal orientation is partly constructed through inner speech vocabulary. The reader who has learned to reach for mastery-approach language during difficulty is not merely interpreting difficulty differently — they are also, over time, building a different attribution pattern, a different efficacy profile, and a different set of SRL strategies in response to difficulty.

### Growth Mindset and the Language of Expandable Intelligence

[[Growth-Mindset]] theory — [[Carol-Dweck]]'s account of implicit theories of intelligence — is best understood, from the perspective of this report, as a theory about what language is available to the learner when processing performance feedback. The *fixed mindset* vocabulary treats intelligence as a noun — a fixed quantity whose adequacy is revealed by performance: "I'm not a math person," "I'm not good at abstract reasoning," "I don't have the kind of mind for formal theory." The *growth mindset* vocabulary treats intelligence as a verb — a process that changes through effort and good strategy: "I haven't developed the abstract reasoning skills for this yet," "This kind of formal argument is new to me and will take practice," "I can build the schema I need."

> [!insight] **The Temporal Grammar of Growth Mindset Language**
> Growth mindset language has a distinctive temporal structure: it places current incapacity in a developmental timeline that opens onto future capability. The fixed mindset collapses time — "I can't" implicitly means "I never will" because the cause (ability) is treated as static. The growth mindset explicitly marks temporality: "I can't *yet*," "This is hard *now* but won't always be," "I'm at an early stage of learning this." The single word "yet" — Dweck's famous minimal growth-mindset intervention — functions by grammatically reopening the timeline that fixed-mindset language forecloses.

The relationship between [[Growth-Mindset]] and [[Attribution-Theory]] is direct: growth mindset is, in attribution terms, the belief that ability is an internal, unstable, controllable cause — that it can be changed by effort and strategy. Fixed mindset is the belief that ability is internal, stable, and uncontrollable. Growth mindset thus changes the attributional meaning of effort: in a fixed mindset, effort is a threat to self-worth (if I try hard and still fail, I have confirmed low ability); in a growth mindset, effort is the mechanism of ability growth (effort is the pathway, not the evidence of inadequacy).

### Hierarchical Goal Language and the Forethought Phase

[[Zimmerman's-Cyclical-Model-of-Self-Regulated-Learning]] places the Forethought Phase upstream of all learning performance. Within the Forethought Phase, *task analysis* (including goal setting and strategic planning) and *self-motivation beliefs* (including self-efficacy, outcome expectations, intrinsic interest, and goal orientation) determine the motivational and cognitive resources brought to the learning task. The vocabulary used to construct goals and appraise the task during the Forethought Phase thus functions as the cognitive architecture that the entire subsequent learning episode runs on.

Goal-setting research (Locke & Latham, 1990, 2002) has consistently demonstrated that specific, challenging, self-chosen goals outperform vague or "do your best" goals on virtually all performance and persistence measures. But the goal-setting literature has been less attentive to the *vocabulary* through which goals are formulated, which is where the present synthesis adds value. A goal stated in mastery-approach, identified-regulatory, internally-attributed, growth-mindset-consistent language performs very differently from an otherwise comparable goal stated in performance-avoidance, introjected, ability-focused language.

> [!example] **Goal Language Comparison: The Same Objective, Multiple Vocabularies**
> Consider a reader who intends to understand Weiner's three attribution dimensions while reading this report. Here are five different ways to formulate that goal, each encoding a different motivational vocabulary:
>
> 1. *"I need to memorize the three dimensions so I can use them."* — **Performance-approach, controlled, surface.** The goal is reproduction and instrumental use; the operative word is *need* (mild introjection); the criterion is recall.
>
> 2. *"I should be able to understand this by the end — it's not supposed to be that hard."* — **Performance-avoidance, introjected.** The goal is competence demonstration; the criterion is normative sufficiency; failure will generate shame.
>
> 3. *"I want to genuinely understand how locus, stability, and controllability each differentially affect motivation — not just as labels but as mechanisms."* — **Mastery-approach, identified, deep.** The goal is mechanistic understanding; the criterion is self-set and substantive; failure generates curiosity, not shame.
>
> 4. *"I'm building schema for a framework I'll apply to my own inner speech. I want to understand this well enough to recognize attribution patterns in real-time."* — **Mastery-approach, integrated, applied.** The goal is connected to identity and practical application; the criterion is transfer.
>
> 5. *"How does this work? I want to follow the argument."* — **Intrinsic.** No explicit goal construction; engagement is pure curiosity-driven; the criterion is felt comprehension.

The practical import is that even when the learning objective is fixed — understand Weiner's attribution dimensions — the *vocabulary* of the goal formulation encodes assumptions about what success means, what failure means, and what effort is for, and these assumptions shape the entire motivational profile of the reading episode.

> 2. Goal orientation theory and SDT are related but distinct frameworks. Can you articulate how they relate? Is a mastery-approach goal always autonomously regulated, or could it be introjected?
> 3. The "yet" intervention in growth mindset is elegantly simple — one word reopens a foreclosed timeline. Can you identify analogous minimal vocabulary interventions in the SDT or Attribution Theory frameworks?

## Phase IV: Mechanisms & Processes

### The Attribution-Efficacy Spiral During Reading

The core mechanism this report is built around is a **recursive loop** in which inner speech vocabulary activates attribution patterns, which generate efficacy estimates, which shape the motivational resources brought to the next cognitive challenge, which in turn triggers new inner speech. This loop can be either virtuous or vicious, and the direction it takes is substantially influenced by vocabulary choices that occur in milliseconds during reading.

> [!key-claim] **The Attribution-Efficacy Cascade: Step-by-Step**
> When a reader encounters comprehension difficulty (the trigger event):
> 1. **Automatic attribution search**: Inner speech rapidly generates a causal explanation. The vocabulary available determines the attribution made: "I can't follow this" (ability, internal, stable, uncontrollable) vs. "I'm not using the right approach here" (strategy, internal, unstable, controllable).
> 2. **Dimensional processing**: The attribution is implicitly assigned to Weiner's dimensions. Stable attributions generate low expectancy for future comprehension; controllable attributions preserve agency.
> 3. **Efficacy update**: The attribution modifies the current self-efficacy estimate for the reading task. Stable/uncontrollable attributions reduce efficacy; unstable/controllable attributions maintain it.
> 4. **Motivational consequence**: Reduced efficacy generates decreased effort allocation, cognitive withdrawal, shallower processing, and reduced persistence. Maintained efficacy generates continued engagement, strategic adaptation, and deeper processing.
> 5. **Comprehension outcome**: The motivated/demotivated reading generates a comprehension outcome that becomes the input for the next attribution search.
> 6. **Cycle**: The loop re-initiates.

This cascade can operate at the level of a single sentence (when a sentence is syntactically or conceptually challenging) or at the level of an entire text (when a reader evaluates their comprehension after a section). The crucial intervention point is step 1: the vocabulary of the initial attribution. Because attribution is partly habitual — people have practiced causal narrative templates over thousands of learning episodes — the vocabulary that surfaces in step 1 reflects learned patterns more than deliberate choice. But because inner speech is accessible to metacognitive monitoring, these patterns can be observed, recognized, and redirected.

> [!example] **The Cascade in Action: Two Readers, One Dense Paragraph**
> Consider two readers encountering a particularly abstract passage in a Foundational Report. Both fail to construct a coherent situation model on the first pass. Their inner speech diverges immediately:
>
> **Reader A**: *"I'm losing the thread again. I've never been good at this level of abstraction. Maybe this is just too advanced for me right now."* → **Internal, stable, uncontrollable** attribution → efficacy erosion → reduced effort allocation → skimming → shallow encoding → confirmation of initial attribution → vicious spiral.
>
> **Reader B**: *"I'm not tracking the argument here. Let me see where I lost the thread — it was probably at the point where the author moved from describing the phenomenon to explaining the mechanism. I need to re-read that transition."* → **Internal, unstable, controllable** (strategy) attribution → maintained efficacy → strategic re-reading → deeper processing → comprehension improvement → efficacy building → virtuous spiral.
>
> The difference between these two readers is not, at this moment, one of intelligence, prior knowledge, or reading skill. It is the vocabulary of step 1.

### How Inner Speech Vocabulary Affects Working Memory and Comprehension

The connection between motivational inner speech and [[Working-Memory]] in reading operates through two distinct channels. The first is **attentional**: negative, ruminative inner speech (especially the shame and self-evaluative content characteristic of ability attributions and introjected regulation) competes with content processing for [[Working-Memory]] capacity. If a reader is running a parallel commentary on their own inadequacy while processing an academic argument, working memory is divided — some resources are allocated to comprehension monitoring and processing, others to the self-evaluative narrative. The result is reduced capacity for the generative processing ([[Elaborative-Encoding]], [[Situation-Models-van-Dijk-&-Kintsch]]) that produces genuine comprehension.

The second channel is **motivational**: efficacy-eroding inner speech reduces the *effort* allocated to working memory-intensive processes. Deep comprehension of academic texts requires sustained engagement with demanding cognitive operations — inferential bridging, [[Situation-Model]] construction, integration with prior knowledge, evaluation of argument validity. These operations are effortful and can be truncated by low-effort reading that produces only surface-level text comprehension. When efficacy is high and goals are mastery-oriented, readers are more likely to sustain the effortful processing that comprehension requires; when efficacy is low or goals are performance-avoidant, surface processing is reinforced as risk-minimizing behavior.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Examining the Cognitive Cost of Self-Evaluative Inner Speech**
> This is a moment to pause and notice something about your current reading. Is there any proportion of your working memory currently engaged with evaluating *yourself* rather than *the content* — whether you're understanding well enough, whether you're reading at the right speed, whether someone with your background should already know this? That self-evaluative allocation has a real cognitive cost. The goal of the vocabulary framework in this report is partly to *redirect* that evaluative energy from self-assessment to content-engagement.

### Vocabulary, Goal Frames, and Comprehension Monitoring

The vocabulary of the Forethought Phase doesn't only affect effort allocation; it affects the *criteria* applied during comprehension monitoring. This is a subtle but important mechanism. A reader whose inner speech has framed the goal in mastery-approach, mechanistic terms — "I want to understand how these three attribution dimensions *interact* to produce motivation outcomes" — will apply a demanding comprehension criterion: have I understood the interactions, not just the labels? A reader whose inner speech has framed the goal in surface, performance-avoidant terms — "I need to get through this so I don't fall behind" — will apply a much weaker criterion: have I read the words?

The monitoring criterion matters because [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]] is the mechanism by which readers detect comprehension failures and initiate repair strategies. If the monitoring criterion is weak — if "having read the words" counts as comprehension — then genuine failures to understand will go undetected, and the strategic re-reading, re-elaboration, or prior-knowledge activation that would produce genuine comprehension will never be triggered. This is the cognitive pathway through which motivational vocabulary affects comprehension: not only by changing effort, but by changing what counts as success, which changes whether corrective action is initiated.

> 2. The two-channel account (attentional competition + effort allocation) of how inner speech affects working memory suggests two different intervention strategies. What would each intervention look like in practice?
> 3. The comprehension monitoring mechanism — whereby goal framing changes what counts as comprehension — is particularly important for PKB practice. What vocabulary change would most reliably elevate the criterion applied during your own monitoring?

## Phase V: Applications, Implications & Limitations

### Practical Vocabulary Architecture for Academic Reading

The practical implication of the preceding analysis is that constructive academic inner speech can be cultivated through deliberate vocabulary practice — specifically, by building a vocabulary that encodes growth-oriented attributions, maintained self-efficacy, autonomous regulation, and mastery-approach goal framing. This section presents a structured vocabulary architecture organized around the key intervention points identified in Phases II–IV.

> [!best-practice] **The Four-Domain Vocabulary Architecture**
>
> **Domain 1: Attribution Vocabulary**
> Replace ability-based, stable, uncontrollable language with strategy-based, unstable, controllable language:
> - *Instead of*: "I can't understand this" → *Use*: "I haven't found the right approach to this yet"
> - *Instead of*: "This is too hard for me" → *Use*: "This requires knowledge/skills I'm still developing"
> - *Instead of*: "I'm not good at abstract reasoning" → *Use*: "Abstract reasoning is an area where I need more practice and examples"
> - *Instead of*: "I keep losing the thread" → *Use*: "My current schema for this argument structure isn't working — I need to reconstruct the argument structure explicitly"
>
> **Domain 2: Self-Efficacy Vocabulary**
> Use future-oriented, domain-specific, process-anchored language:
> - *Instead of*: "I'll never understand this material" → *Use*: "I can understand this if I work through it systematically"
> - *Instead of*: "I don't think I'm smart enough for this level" → *Use*: "I've understood similarly difficult material before by taking it step-by-step"
> - *Instead of*: "I give up on this section" → *Use*: "I'm not yet understanding this — let me identify specifically where I lost comprehension"
>
> **Domain 3: Regulatory Vocabulary (SDT)**
> Replace controlled/introjected language with identified/integrated language:
> - *Instead of*: "I have to read this" → *Use*: "I'm choosing to read this because [specific reason]"
> - *Instead of*: "I should understand this by now" → *Use*: "I'm interested in understanding this because it connects to [specific goal]"
> - *Instead of*: "I'll feel stupid if I can't follow this" → *Use*: "Understanding this will help me [specific application]"
>
> **Domain 4: Goal Construction Vocabulary**
> Use mastery-approach, mechanistic, specific goal language:
> - *Instead of*: "Understand this report" → *Use*: "Understand the *mechanism* by which attribution dimensions cascade into self-efficacy"
> - *Instead of*: "Read through this material" → *Use*: "Construct a situation model of the argument that I can paraphrase in my own words"
> - *Instead of*: "Get through this section" → *Use*: "Identify the two or three claims in this section that I can connect to [existing PKB node]"

### Hierarchical Goal Construction Template for SRL Forethought

The Forethought Phase of [[Zimmerman's-Cyclical-Model-of-Self-Regulated-Learning]] involves goal setting at multiple levels: the overarching purpose of the learning episode, the specific comprehension objectives for the current text, and the moment-to-moment process goals that guide reading behavior. Each level benefits from vocabulary that encodes the motivational architecture described in this report.

> [!protocol] **Hierarchical Goal Construction Template for Academic Reading (Forethought Phase)**
>
> **Level 1 — Purpose Goal (Why am I reading this?)**
> Frame in identified or integrated regulatory language; connect to values or long-term goals:
> *"I'm reading this because [specific connection to PKB project / intellectual goal / application context]. I choose this."*
> Template: "I am reading [text] because it will help me [specific intellectual/practical outcome], which matters to me because [connection to broader goal]."
>
> **Level 2 — Comprehension Goal (What do I want to understand?)**
> Frame in mastery-approach, mechanistic language; specify criterion:
> *"By the end of this reading session, I want to understand [specific mechanism / relationship / argument structure] well enough to [specific performance criterion: explain it, connect it to X, apply it to Y]."*
> Template: "I want to understand the **mechanism** by which [A] causes/produces/relates to [B], with enough depth to [apply/explain/connect] it to [specific context]."
>
> **Level 3 — Process Goal (How will I read?)**
> Frame in strategy language; specify monitoring criteria:
> *"I will [specific reading strategy] and monitor comprehension by [specific criterion]. If I lose the thread, I will [specific recovery strategy] rather than continuing."*
> Template: "I will [read actively / annotate / paraphrase each section] and notice when I can't paraphrase a claim in my own words [monitoring criterion]. When that happens, I will [re-read / look up prerequisite / construct explicit argument structure] before moving on."
>
> **Level 4 — Attribution Pre-Commitment (How will I interpret difficulty?)**
> Explicitly pre-commit to attribution pattern before encountering difficulty:
> *"If I encounter difficulty in this text, I will interpret it as [strategy/schema mismatch / need for elaboration / unfamiliar vocabulary] rather than [ability inadequacy]. Difficulty is information, not verdict."*

### Monitoring Inner Speech: The Self-Reflection Phase

[[Zimmerman's-Cyclical-Model-of-Self-Regulated-Learning]]'s Self-Reflection Phase — the phase you know well from your SRL project — is the moment where attribution is most consequential. This is when the learner evaluates what happened, assigns causes, and draws conclusions that will feed the Forethought Phase of the next learning episode. The vocabulary used during self-reflection thus has temporal leverage: it shapes not only the current episode but the prior knowledge and motivational resources brought to future episodes.

> [!protocol] **Self-Reflection Phase Inner Speech Audit**
>
> After completing a reading session, spend 3–5 minutes with these prompts, attending to the vocabulary you reach for:
>
> **Causal Attribution Audit:**
> "What explains how this session went?" — Notice whether your explanation reaches for ability, effort, strategy, task difficulty, or context. Specifically: is the cause you named stable or unstable? Controllable or uncontrollable?
>
> **Efficacy Update Audit:**
> "What am I taking away about my capability for this kind of material?" — Notice whether your efficacy estimate has changed and why. Is the update warranted by actual performance data, or is it driven by the *emotional valence* of the session?
>
> **Regulatory Register Audit:**
> "What was the dominant motivational register of this session?" — Was it introjected (should/must), identified (want to/because), or intrinsic? What would it take to shift the balance toward the more autonomous end?
>
> **Vocabulary Replacement:**
> For each maladaptive vocabulary item identified, deliberately formulate a replacement using the four-domain vocabulary architecture above. This is not positive thinking; it is accurate attribution reframing.

### Limitations and Epistemic Honesty

> [!important] **Methodological Limitations of the Inner Speech Research Base**
> The inner speech literature, despite its theoretical richness, faces serious measurement challenges. Most studies of inner speech content rely on self-report (retrospective thought-listing or experience-sampling) or verbal protocols (think-aloud), neither of which captures the condensed, predicative form of natural inner speech with fidelity. Thought-listing protocols prompt for verbalized versions of inner speech that are likely more expanded and articulate than the original cognitive events. This means that much of what we know about inner speech *content* is actually about its verbalized reconstruction, which may differ systematically from the inner speech that occurs during reading.

> [!warning] **The Causality Problem: Vocabulary as Symptom vs. Driver**
> A central limitation of the vocabulary-focused approach in this report is the difficulty of establishing whether changing vocabulary *causes* changes in attribution, efficacy, and motivation, or whether vocabulary merely *reflects* underlying motivational states that change through other mechanisms. Attribution retraining interventions (Försterling, 1985; Perry et al., 1993) provide the strongest evidence that explicitly reframing causal language produces genuine motivational change — but these are structured interventions, not naturalistic vocabulary practice. The claim that deliberate inner speech vocabulary practice produces analogous effects rests on reasonable inference from the intervention literature, not direct evidence. This is worth holding in mind as you work with the practical protocols above.

> [!warning] **WEIRD Sample and Cross-Cultural Limitations**
> The foundational studies in Attribution Theory, Self-Efficacy Theory, and Achievement Goal Theory are predominantly conducted with Western (primarily American), Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) samples. Cross-cultural research suggests that the meaning of internal attribution for failure varies substantially across cultural contexts — in collectivist contexts, internal attribution for failure may be less damaging to motivation because it does not carry the same self-defining weight that it does in individualist contexts. The vocabulary prescriptions in this report should be applied with awareness that they reflect cultural assumptions about individual agency, the meaning of effort, and the relationship between self and performance that may not generalize universally.

## Phase VI: Synthesis & Integration

The analysis across Phases II–V converges on a single, architecturally important insight: **inner speech vocabulary is not merely a reporting medium for motivational states — it is a constitutive element of motivational cognition that can be deliberately designed.** This is a claim with significant practical leverage because vocabulary is, unlike many cognitive variables, directly accessible and modifiable through deliberate practice.

The convergence of [[Attribution-Theory|Attribution Theory]], [[Self-Efficacy]] theory, [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]], [[Achievement-Goal-Theory]], and [[Growth-Mindset]] around the question of vocabulary is striking. Each framework, developed largely independently, points to the same intervention site: the language used to interpret outcomes, appraise capability, frame goals, and regulate engagement. Weiner's attribution dimensions identify the *semantic content* of the most consequential vocabulary choices. Bandura's verbal persuasion source of efficacy explains *how* vocabulary functions mechanistically. SDT's regulatory continuum identifies the *syntactic markers* (must/should vs. want/choose) that encode motivational quality. Achievement Goal Theory identifies the *criteria* that goal vocabulary establishes. Growth Mindset identifies the *temporal grammar* (stable capability vs. developing capability) that forecloses or reopens motivational options.

What makes this synthesis especially relevant to your PKB practice is that the texts you are reading — Foundational Reports covering advanced theoretical content in motivational psychology, cognitive science, and pedagogy — are precisely the class of texts that activate all four of these motivational systems simultaneously. They are difficult enough to trigger attribution processes, novel enough to challenge efficacy, complex enough to reward mastery-approach goals, and dense enough to expose the comprehension cost of controlled, introjected motivation. They are also, by design, tools for building the very knowledge base that enables more sophisticated self-regulation — which means the quality of your inner dialogue while reading them has leverage on the entire project.

The "So What?" is this: **you now have a vocabulary for the vocabulary.** You can recognize an ability attribution and name it as such. You can detect introjected regulation in the *should* constructions of your inner speech and understand what it costs you cognitively and motivationally. You can formulate goals in the mastery-approach register and understand why that formulation performs better than alternatives. You can pre-commit to strategy attributions for difficulty and understand the mechanism by which that pre-commitment maintains efficacy through challenging reading. This meta-vocabulary — the capacity to observe and describe your own inner speech in theoretically articulate terms — is itself a powerful metacognitive tool.

## Phase VII: Appendix

### Appendix 1: Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Inner Speech (Vygotsky, 1934/1986; Fernyhough, 2016)**
> The internalized, self-directed form of language that functions as a cognitive and self-regulatory tool; characterized by condensed, predicative structure in which the subject is typically omitted. Distinct from silent reading subvocalization (phonological activation for word recognition) and from mind-wandering.
>
> **Boundary:** Inner speech is not equivalent to "thinking in words" in the broad sense; it specifically refers to the self-directed regulatory dialogue that accompanies deliberate cognitive work. Unstructured verbal association or unbidden thought is not inner speech in Vygotsky's technical sense.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** Evaluative commentary, planning language, monitoring statements, and self-efficacy appraisals during deliberate cognitive activity.
>
> **See also:** [[Vygotsky]], [[Metacognition]], [[Self-Regulation]], [[Stream-of-Consciousness]]

> [!definition] **Locus of Causality (Weiner, 1972)**
> The attribution dimension distinguishing whether a cause is perceived as residing within the individual (internal: ability, effort, mood, strategy) or outside the individual (external: task difficulty, luck, teacher quality). Internal attributions for failure generate shame and self-directed affect; external attributions for failure generate anger or context-directed affect.
>
> **Boundary:** Locus alone does not determine motivational outcome — it must be considered alongside stability and controllability. A failure attributed to internal, unstable, controllable causes (e.g., insufficient strategy use) has very different motivational consequences than one attributed to internal, stable, uncontrollable causes (e.g., fixed ability).
>
> **See also:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Bernard-Weiner]], [[Controllability]], [[Stability-Dimension]]

> [!definition] **Stability Dimension (Weiner, 1972)**
> The attribution dimension distinguishing whether a cause is perceived as fixed over time (stable: chronic low ability, consistent task difficulty) or variable over time (unstable: effort on this occasion, luck, current mood). Stable attributions for failure predict low expectancy for future success; unstable attributions for failure preserve expectancy.
>
> **Boundary:** Stability is the *most consequential* dimension for expectancy effects, and thus for persistent motivation. The fixed/growth mindset distinction maps most directly onto this dimension: growth mindset reframes ability as unstable (developable), while fixed mindset treats it as stable.
>
> **See also:** [[Growth-Mindset]], [[Learned-Helplessness]], [[Implicit-Theory-of-Intelligence]], [[Attribution-Style]]

> [!definition] **Controllability (Weiner, 1985)**
> The attribution dimension distinguishing whether a cause is perceived as within the individual's volitional control (controllable: effort, strategy use, preparation) or outside it (uncontrollable: inherent ability, luck, others' actions). Controllable attributions for failure generate guilt (self-directed responsibility); uncontrollable attributions for failure generate shame (self-concept threat).
>
> **Boundary:** Controllability and locus are correlated but distinct. Mood is internal but relatively uncontrollable; task difficulty is external and uncontrollable; effort is internal and controllable; social support is external but partially controllable. Interventions should target controllability as their primary lever for building agency.
>
> **See also:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Self-Regulation]], [[Volitional-Congruence]]

> [!definition] **Self-Efficacy (Bandura, 1977, 1997)**
> The individual's judgment of their capability to execute specific behaviors or attain specific performance levels in a given domain under given conditions; domain-specific, task-specific, and future-oriented. Four sources: mastery experience, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological/affective states.
>
> **Boundary:** Self-efficacy is not global self-esteem (general worth), not self-concept (descriptive beliefs about the self), and not outcome expectancy (belief that performing behavior will lead to desired outcomes). High self-efficacy does not guarantee performance — it regulates the motivational resources brought to performance attempts.
>
> **See also:** [[Albert-Bandura]], [[Self-Efficacy-Theory]], [[Mastery-Experience]], [[Self-Concept]]

> [!definition] **Introjected Regulation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> A form of partially internalized motivation in which behavior is regulated by internally administered approval, guilt, shame, or pride contingencies; the regulatory language is characterized by *should, must, ought to, have to* constructions. Though internal, introjection is not autonomous — it represents an inner controller that produces pressure and conditional self-worth rather than genuine endorsement.
>
> **Boundary:** Introjection is often misidentified as autonomous motivation because it involves self-generated pressure rather than external constraint. The diagnostic is whether the individual experiences themselves as freely choosing (autonomous) or as pressured — even by themselves — to comply. The emotional register of introjection includes anxiety, guilt, shame, and conditional self-worth, unlike identified or integrated regulation which generates interest and volitional engagement.
>
> **See also:** [[Self-Determination-Theory]], [[Internalization-Continuum]], [[Autonomous-Motivation]], [[Controlled-Motivation]]

> [!definition] **Mastery Goal Orientation (Dweck, 1986; Ames, 1992)**
> A motivational orientation in which the individual's standard of success is self-referential (learning, improvement, understanding), the purpose is developing competence, and difficulty is interpreted as information about what remains to be learned. Distinguished from performance goal orientations in which the standard is normative (comparison with others) and the purpose is demonstrating competence (approach) or avoiding displaying incompetence (avoidance).
>
> **Boundary:** Mastery goal orientation is not equivalent to "trying hard" — it is an orientation toward the *quality* of understanding rather than the *quantity* of effort, and toward learning standards rather than performance standards. Research suggests mastery-approach goals generally produce deeper processing, greater intrinsic motivation, and more adaptive responses to difficulty than performance goals, though the evidence for mastery-avoidance goals is more complex.
>
> **See also:** [[Achievement-Goal-Theory]], [[Carol-Dweck]], [[Carole-Ames]], [[Mastery-Goal]], [[Deep-Processing]]

> [!definition] **Verbal Persuasion (Bandura, 1977, 1997)**
> One of the four sources of self-efficacy: the impact of others' (or one's own) evaluative statements on efficacy beliefs. Verbal persuasion is weaker than mastery experience as an efficacy source but is more immediately accessible; crucially, *self-directed verbal persuasion* — the inner speech one deploys when appraising one's own capability — functions through the same mechanism as external persuasion. Credibility, specificity, and process-focus (rather than outcome-focus) enhance the efficacy impact of verbal persuasion.
>
> **Boundary:** Verbal persuasion operates most effectively when it is specific (domain- and task-relevant), process-anchored (focused on what the individual can *do* rather than what they *are*), and delivered by credible sources. Generic praise or unfounded encouragement has weak or even negative efficacy effects because it is inconsistent with the individual's performance record.
>
> **See also:** [[Self-Efficacy]], [[Process-Praise]], [[Mastery-Experience]], [[Attribution-Retraining]]

### Appendix 3: Conceptual Tensions & Open Questions

> [!tension] **The Vocabulary-Causality Problem: Does Word Choice Drive Motivation or Merely Label It?**
>
> **The Tension:** The central practical claim of this report — that deliberately changing inner speech vocabulary produces genuine motivational change — rests on an assumption that vocabulary is causally upstream of motivational states, not merely downstream of them. This is contested.
>
> **Position A — Language Constitutes Motivation (Wittgenstein, Vygotsky, Constructionist):** Language is not a passive reporter of pre-linguistic states; it organizes and partly constitutes cognitive and motivational experience. Changing the vocabulary of inner speech does not merely rename existing states — it genuinely reorganizes the cognitive content that language both expresses and structures. Evidence: attribution retraining interventions, which change both causal language and subsequent motivation and performance; theory of constructed emotion (Barrett), which suggests emotional categories are constructed through language-mediated conceptualization.
>
> **Position B — Motivation is Primary, Language Derivative (Traditional Representationalism):** Motivational states are non-linguistic mental events that are subsequently labeled by language. Changing vocabulary may produce momentary relabeling without changing the underlying state; the observable effects of attribution retraining may work through mechanisms other than language change.
>
> **Current Evidence:** Attribution retraining intervention studies (Perry et al., 1993; Haynes et al., 2006) do support causal effects of reframing on performance, but the mechanism is not definitively established as linguistic. The most honest position is that language and motivation are mutually constitutive, with neither unambiguously primary.
>
> **Why It Matters:** If language is only derivative, the vocabulary protocols in this report are misdirected — they address the symptom rather than the cause. If language is constitutive, they address the mechanism directly.
>
> **See also:** [[Attribution-Theory]], [[Theory-of-Constructed-Emotion]], [[Language-and-Thought]]

> [!open-question] **What is the Natural Ecological Validity of Inner Speech Vocabulary Research?**
>
> **The Question:** Nearly all research on inner speech in academic contexts uses verbal protocols (think-aloud) or retrospective self-report, which may not capture the condensed, predicative character of natural inner speech. Is the vocabulary that appears in verbal protocols representative of the vocabulary that actually operates during reading?
>
> **What We Know:** Fernyhough and colleagues have documented that inner speech in naturalistic settings (experience sampling methodology) often takes non-verbal or partially verbal forms — visual imagery, emotional tone, fragmentary language — rather than full propositions. The rich attribution and efficacy language in this report may correspond more to *potential* inner speech (what could be verbalized if prompted) than to *occurring* inner speech.
>
> **What We Don't Know:** Whether natural inner speech during academic reading is predominantly verbal (and thus vocabulary-modifiable), whether the non-verbal components carry the same motivational content as their verbal counterparts, and whether deliberate verbal practice generalizes to condensed inner speech.
>
> **Research Direction:** Experience-sampling studies of inner speech during authentic academic reading, combined with concurrent comprehension measures, would provide important data.
>
> **See also:** [[Inner-Speech]], [[Metacognitive-Monitoring]], [[Silent-Reading-Fluency]]

### Appendix 7: Practical Application Protocols

> [!protocol] **The Pre-Reading Motivational Architecture Protocol (Forethought Phase)**
>
> Apply this 5-minute protocol before beginning any Foundational Report or challenging academic text.
>
> **Step 1 — Regulatory Register Check (1 min):**
> Formulate in one sentence why you are reading this text. Listen to the sentence. Does it contain *have to, should, must, need to* (controlled), or *want to, choosing to, because it matters for* (autonomous)? If controlled: replace with an identified/integrated formulation before proceeding. Do not skip this — the register of the first sentence shapes the entire session.
>
> **Step 2 — Comprehension Goal (1 min):**
> State the mechanistic goal: "I want to understand the *mechanism* by which [A] produces [B]." Specify the criterion: "Well enough to [paraphrase/connect/apply]." Avoid surface goals ("understand this section") or performance goals ("prove I can handle this material").
>
> **Step 3 — Attribution Pre-Commitment (1 min):**
> State explicitly: "If I encounter difficulty in this text, I will interpret it as [a strategy/schema mismatch, an unfamiliar concept, a need for more prior knowledge] rather than as evidence of inadequate ability." Pre-commit to this interpretation before the difficulty occurs — post-hoc reframing is harder than prospective commitment.
>
> **Step 4 — Efficacy Anchoring (1 min):**
> Recall a specific prior instance of successfully engaging with similarly difficult material. This is not positive thinking — it is deliberate activation of the mastery experience source of efficacy. Be specific: "I successfully understood [X difficult material] by [specific strategy]. I can apply the same approach here."
>
> **Step 5 — Goal Review (1 min):**
> Re-read the comprehension goal. Commit to monitoring comprehension against that criterion (not against "reading the words"). If you cannot paraphrase a claim in your own words, that is a comprehension failure requiring repair strategy — not a signal to continue.

> [!protocol] **The Difficulty-Response Protocol (In-Reading)**
>
> Apply this protocol at the moment of encountering comprehension difficulty.
>
> **Step 1 — Attribution Triage (10 seconds):**
> Name the difficulty without evaluating the self: "I've lost the thread here." (Not: "I can't follow this.") This step prevents the automatic ability attribution.
>
> **Step 2 — Attribution Classification (20 seconds):**
> Apply Weiner's dimensions: Is the difficulty due to [strategy mismatch / unfamiliar vocabulary / missing prior knowledge / text structure / insufficient effort on this passage]? Select the most accurate and most controllable attribution.
>
> **Step 3 — Strategy Response (variable):**
> Select a recovery strategy matched to the attribution: vocabulary gap → consult glossary/PKB; schema mismatch → reconstruct argument structure explicitly; missing prior knowledge → identify and fill prerequisite; insufficient attention → re-read with active note-making.
>
> **Step 4 — Efficacy Maintenance:**
> After applying the strategy, note any improvement in comprehension. Use this as mastery experience input: "I recovered comprehension by [strategy]. That approach works."

> [!checklist] **Self-Reflection Phase Vocabulary Audit Checklist**
>
> After each reading session, review the following:
>
> - [ ] What was the dominant inner speech vocabulary during this session? (ability-focused? effort-focused? strategy-focused?)
> - [ ] Did any stable, uncontrollable attributions occur? If so: can I reframe them as unstable and controllable?
> - [ ] Was my regulatory register primarily controlled (should/must) or autonomous (want/choosing)? What would shift it toward autonomy?
> - [ ] Did I apply my comprehension goal as the monitoring criterion, or did I use a weaker standard (reading the words)?
> - [ ] What is my efficacy estimate for this domain after this session? Is it accurate, or is it contaminated by emotional tone rather than actual performance?
> - [ ] One attribution reframe to carry forward: "Instead of [maladaptive attribution], I will say [reconstructed attribution]."
