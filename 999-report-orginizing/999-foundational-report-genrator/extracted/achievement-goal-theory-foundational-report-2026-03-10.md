# Achievement Goal Theory: A Foundational Report

## Phase I: Orientation & Context Setting

### Why the Goals We Pursue Define How We Learn

Consider two students preparing for the same difficult examination. The first approaches the material with a question that quietly organises all her studying: *How much can I actually learn here? What will I understand that I didn't understand before?* The second student is preoccupied with a different question: *Will I do better than my classmates? Will this grade make me look competent?* Both students study hard. Both care about their performance. But the quality of their engagement, their response to difficulty, and their long-term relationship with the subject matter will differ in systematic, predictable, and consequential ways. [[Achievement-Goal-Theory|Achievement Goal Theory]] — the framework examined in this report — exists to explain exactly why.

[[Achievement-Goal-Theory|Achievement Goal Theory]] (AGT) is one of the most empirically productive frameworks in [[Educational-Psychology|Educational Psychology]] and [[Motivational-Psychology|Motivational Psychology]]. Originating in the early 1980s through the work of [[John-G.-Nicholls|John G. Nicholls]], [[Carol-Ames|Carol Ames]], and [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]], it proposes that much of the motivational variation we observe in learning environments can be explained by the *type* of goal a person is pursuing — specifically, the standard of competence against which they are measuring themselves. Its central claim is deceptively simple: not all goals to achieve are alike, and the differences between them shape cognition, affect, behaviour, and long-term outcomes in deep and lasting ways.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Before You Begin**
> Before reading further, take a moment to reflect on a learning context that matters to you — a skill you are developing, a course you are taking, a project you are working on. What is the implicit question you are asking yourself as you engage with it? Are you asking *Am I getting better?* or are you asking *Am I doing better than others?* Or perhaps something more fearful: *Am I avoiding looking stupid?* Holding your own motivational profile in mind as you read will make the theory far more personally legible.

### Scope and Reader Positioning

This report provides a comprehensive foundational treatment of Achievement Goal Theory. It traces the theory's intellectual origins, maps the major theoretical models and their evolution, explains the psychological mechanisms through which goals shape learning, reviews the evidence base, and examines practical applications and limitations. The report assumes no prior background in educational psychology, but treats you as a capable adult who can handle technical distinctions and nuanced empirical claims without oversimplification.

The report does not attempt an exhaustive review of every study in the field — that would require a textbook. Instead, it establishes the conceptual architecture you need to engage productively with any subsequent study of motivation, [[Fading-—-Instructional-Design-Collins,-Brown-&-Newman|Instructional Design]], [[Assessment-Design|Assessment Design]], or [[Self-Regulation]]. It connects AGT to adjacent frameworks already in your PKB, particularly [[Metacognition-&-Motivation|Metacognition & Motivation]] and [[Growth-Mindset-Implicit-Theories-of-Intelligence|Growth Mindset]], and identifies the most important expansion topics for future inquiry.

### Why This Matters

A practitioner who understands AGT is equipped to do several things that a practitioner without this framework cannot do as well. They can diagnose the motivational climate of a learning environment and identify the goal signals it inadvertently transmits to learners. They can design tasks, feedback structures, and assessment systems that preferentially elicit goal orientations associated with deep learning and persistence. They can interpret student behaviour — avoidance of challenge, strategic effort withdrawal, a preference for easy tasks — not as character flaws but as rational adaptations to the goal structure the environment has created. And they can understand their own motivational tendencies, including the ways in which performance concerns can quietly undermine the quality of their own learning even when effort remains high.

Beyond the practical payoff, AGT sits at the conceptual centre of a much broader network of ideas about how cognition and motivation interact. Understanding it sheds light on [[Growth-Mindset-Implicit-Theories-of-Intelligence|Growth Mindset]], [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]], [[Attribution-Theory|Attribution Theory]], and the psychology of [[Intrinsic-Motivation|Intrinsic Motivation]] in ways that make each of those frameworks richer and more precisely understood.

## Phase II: Conceptual Foundations

### The Central Question: What Counts as Success?

At the heart of Achievement Goal Theory lies a question that sounds obvious until you think about it carefully: when a person "tries to achieve," what exactly are they trying to achieve? The behaviourist answer — reward, or the avoidance of punishment — proved insufficient to explain the enormous variation in how people respond to challenge, failure, and evaluation. Two students can receive identical grades and respond entirely differently, one becoming more motivated and one retreating. Two athletes can lose the same match, one walking away energised and one devastated. The explanation, AGT proposes, lies not in the outcome itself but in the *meaning* the person attributes to it — and that meaning is determined by the standard against which they are measuring themselves.

> [!definition] **Achievement Goal**
> An achievement goal is a cognitive representation of the purpose a person is pursuing in an achievement context. It specifies what "success" means to the person, which in turn determines what counts as a competent performance, which tasks feel threatening or inviting, and which behaviours are rational responses to difficulty. Achievement goals are not merely preferences or wishes; they are organising frameworks that structure perception, attention, affect, and effort. The concept should not be confused with specific performance targets (e.g., "I want to score 90 on this test") — AGT goals are about the *type* of competence being sought, not the *level* of performance being targeted.

The theory emerged from a fundamental observation about [[Conceptions-of-Ability|Conceptions of Ability]] — the implicit theories people hold about what ability is and how it works. [[John-G.-Nicholls|John G. Nicholls]] (1978, 1984) was the first to systematically distinguish two radically different ways people conceptualise ability, and to show that these conceptions generate different motivational orientations.

### Nicholls's Founding Distinction: Differentiated vs. Undifferentiated Ability

[[John-G.-Nicholls|John G. Nicholls]]'s intellectual contribution was to notice that the concept of "ability" is not psychologically uniform. Children and adults can use the word "ability" — or "intelligence," or "skill" — to refer to very different psychological constructs, and these differences have cascading motivational consequences.

> [!definition] **Undifferentiated Conception of Ability**
> In an undifferentiated conception of ability, effort and ability are treated as the same thing — or at minimum, as inseparable. A person who works harder is understood to be more able, and ability is seen as something that grows through effort. Under this conception, success means improving and learning, and failure means simply "I haven't learned this yet." This conception is developmentally earlier (young children tend to operate under it) and is characteristic of what Nicholls called *task involvement*. It corresponds roughly to what [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]] would later call the [[Growth-Mindset-Implicit-Theories-of-Intelligence|Growth Mindset]].

> [!definition] **Differentiated Conception of Ability**
> In a differentiated conception of ability, effort and ability are understood as *separate* entities that can partially cancel each other out. If I have to try very hard to accomplish something that others accomplish easily, this is evidence that my ability is lower, not higher. Under this conception, demonstrating high ability means achieving more with less effort (or the same outcomes with less effort) relative to others. This is the conception of ability operative in *ego involvement*, and it is cognitively demanding — children typically do not fully develop it until late childhood or early adolescence. It corresponds to what [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]] calls the [[Fixed-Mindset|Fixed Mindset]] or [[Entity-Theory-of-Intelligence|Entity Theory of Intelligence]].

> [!key-claim] **The Foundational Proposition of AGT**
> The standard of competence a person is using to evaluate their performance determines the psychological meaning of effort, difficulty, and failure — and therefore determines the motivational consequences of those experiences. Achievement Goal Theory proposes that much of the variability in adaptive and maladaptive motivation in achievement contexts can be traced to which standard of competence is operative at a given moment.

This is a profound insight because it shifts the unit of analysis from the person's trait-level "motivation" to the *interaction* between person and context. Goal orientation is not simply a fixed personality trait — it is a dynamic state shaped both by relatively stable individual tendencies and by contextual signals about what success means in this particular environment.

### Task Involvement and Ego Involvement

Nicholls formalised his insights through the concepts of *task involvement* and *ego involvement*, which serve as the psychological states corresponding to the two goal orientations.

> [!definition] **Task Involvement**
> Task involvement is a motivational state in which the person's primary concern is mastering the task itself — understanding it, improving at it, making progress. In this state, the person is using their own prior performance or the inherent demands of the task as the standard of reference. Success is defined as learning, understanding, or improving, and effort is seen as the natural means of achieving these things. Task involvement is associated with deep engagement, challenge-seeking, and resilience in the face of failure.

> [!definition] **Ego Involvement**
> Ego involvement is a motivational state in which the person's primary concern is demonstrating superior ability — or, critically, avoiding the demonstration of inferior ability — relative to others. In this state, normative social comparison is the operative standard. Effort becomes a double-edged sword: it is necessary for success, but high effort combined with poor relative performance is damning evidence of low ability. Ego involvement is associated with defensive strategies, challenge avoidance (because failure is more costly), and vulnerability when outcomes are unfavourable.

### Ames's Contribution: Mastery and Performance Goal Constructs

While Nicholls's framework emerged primarily from a developmental psychology perspective, [[Carol-Ames|Carol Ames]] translated these ideas into the language of educational motivation that became the dominant terminology in the field. Ames (1984, 1992) introduced the terms *mastery goals* and *performance goals* to describe the motivational orientations that correspond to task and ego involvement, respectively.

> [!definition] **Mastery Goal (Learning Goal, Task Goal)**
> A mastery goal is an achievement goal in which the person defines success as developing competence — acquiring new skills, deepening understanding, or improving relative to one's own prior performance. The standard of reference is intrapersonal and/or task-inherent. Mastery goals are sometimes called *learning goals* (Dweck's terminology) or *task goals* (Nicholls's terminology). They are associated with the adoption of deep cognitive strategies, help-seeking when confused, persistence in the face of difficulty, positive affect during challenge, and long-term retention. Under mastery-oriented conditions, effort is unambiguously a sign of engagement and is positively valued.

> [!definition] **Performance Goal (Ego Goal)**
> A performance goal is an achievement goal in which the person defines success as outperforming others or appearing more competent than others. The standard of reference is normative — anchored in social comparison. Performance goals are sometimes called *ego goals* or *ability goals*. The motivational consequences of performance goals are, as we will see, more complex than early theorists recognised, and they have been significantly refined by subsequent work distinguishing approach from avoidance orientations.

Ames also made a crucial contribution to the study of [[Motivational-Climate|Motivational Climate]] — the idea that environments, not just individuals, have goal orientations, and that these environmental goal structures powerfully shape the goals individuals adopt. This contribution is examined in Phase III.

### The Intellectual Genealogy

AGT did not emerge from a vacuum. It developed in dialogue with several prior intellectual traditions. [[Attribution-Theory|Attribution Theory]], developed by [[Bernard-Weiner|Bernard Weiner]] (1972), had already established that the causes students attribute to their successes and failures (ability, effort, task difficulty, luck) shape their subsequent motivation. AGT deepened this by explaining why students make the attributions they do — their conception of ability determines what counts as evidence about the underlying causes of performance. [[Intrinsic-Motivation|Intrinsic Motivation]] research, particularly the work of [[Edward-Deci|Edward Deci]] and [[Richard-Ryan|Richard Ryan]] that would culminate in [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]], had established that not all motivation is equivalent: autonomous, self-determined motivation sustains learning better than controlled, external motivation. AGT contributed a more granular account of how the *content* of a person's goals — the specific standard of competence they are pursuing — mediates these motivational processes. And [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]]'s work on [[Growth-Mindset-Implicit-Theories-of-Intelligence|Implicit Theories of Intelligence]] (entity vs. incremental theories) provided the cognitive substrate for understanding why individuals differ in their goal orientation tendencies — a thread we will follow in Phase IV.

> 2. Can you identify a learning context in your own life where you have clearly operated in task-involved mode, and another where ego involvement was dominant? What was different about those experiences?
> 3. Why might the differentiated conception of ability be developmentally *later* than the undifferentiated conception? What cognitive capacities does it require?

## Phase III: Theoretical Landscape

### The Dichotomous Model and Its Limitations

The original AGT framework — what scholars now call the *dichotomous model* — posited two and only two achievement goal orientations: mastery and performance. For roughly a decade, this dichotomy was the dominant organising framework, and it generated a clear and internally consistent set of predictions. Mastery goals were associated with adaptive outcomes: the use of deep processing strategies, persistence, positive affect, intrinsic interest. Performance goals were associated with maladaptive outcomes: surface processing, avoidance of challenge, vulnerability to failure, and the strategic management of one's appearance rather than one's actual competence.

This neat picture was, however, too neat. By the early 1990s, empirical anomalies had accumulated. Studies were finding that performance goals were sometimes associated with positive outcomes — high grades, strong academic engagement, and even intrinsic interest in some contexts. The performance-goal-as-bad-news narrative was too simple. The theoretical resolution came through the incorporation of the approach-avoidance distinction.

> [!counter-argument] **The Performance Goal Anomaly**
> Harackiewicz, Barron, and Elliot (1998) and subsequent longitudinal studies demonstrated that performance-approach goals — goals focused on outperforming others — were positively associated with course grades in college settings, even while mastery goals better predicted long-term interest and intrinsic motivation. This finding created a genuine theoretical problem for the dichotomous model and motivated the development of more differentiated frameworks.

### The Trichotomous Model

[[Andrew-J.-Elliot|Andrew J. Elliot]] and [[Judith Harackiewicz]] (1996) proposed a solution: split the performance goal category into two distinct orientations based on whether the person's valence is positive (approaching success, outperforming others) or negative (avoiding failure, avoiding appearing incompetent). This gave rise to the *trichotomous model* of achievement goals.

> [!definition] **Performance-Approach Goal**
> A performance-approach goal is oriented toward the positive possibility of normative competence — demonstrating that one is more competent than others. The motivational focus is appetitive: moving toward success in the normative sense. Under this orientation, competition and social comparison are energising rather than threatening, and success in outperforming others is the primary desired outcome.

> [!definition] **Performance-Avoidance Goal**
> A performance-avoidance goal is oriented toward the negative possibility of normative incompetence — avoiding demonstrating that one is less competent than others. The motivational focus is aversive: moving away from the threat of looking stupid or inferior. Under this orientation, the primary motivation is fear of failure and social humiliation rather than desire for success. This goal type is consistently associated with the most maladaptive outcomes in the research literature: surface processing, avoidance of challenging tasks, high anxiety, poor long-term learning, and burnout.

The trichotomous model preserved the undivided mastery goal and divided the performance goal into approach and avoidance variants, yielding three orientations: (1) mastery goals, (2) performance-approach goals, and (3) performance-avoidance goals. This model clarified the empirical landscape considerably. The maladaptive consequences previously attributed to performance goals generally turned out to be specific to performance-avoidance goals. Performance-approach goals, by contrast, were associated with higher grades and competitive performance in certain contexts — though typically not with deep engagement or sustained intrinsic interest.

### The 2×2 Framework

[[Andrew-J.-Elliot|Andrew J. Elliot]] and [[Marcy Church]] McGregor (2001) extended the approach-avoidance distinction to mastery goals as well, completing the logic that had motivated the trichotomous model. If approach and avoidance are fundamental motivational valences that can apply to any goal content, then there should also be a *mastery-avoidance* goal: the goal of avoiding doing worse than one has done before, or failing to live up to one's own past performance standards.

The resulting *2×2 framework* crossed two dimensions — goal content (mastery vs. performance) with motivational valence (approach vs. avoidance) — to yield four goal types:

> [!definition] **Mastery-Approach Goal**
> Oriented toward the positive possibility of intrapersonal competence: developing one's skills, deepening understanding, mastering the task. The person asks "Am I learning as much as possible?" This is the goal orientation most consistently associated with adaptive outcomes: deep processing, intrinsic motivation, positive affect, and genuine intellectual engagement.

> [!definition] **Mastery-Avoidance Goal**
> Oriented toward the negative possibility of intrapersonal incompetence: failing to learn, declining in ability, not living up to one's prior standard. The person asks "Am I avoiding regression?" This goal type is theoretically interesting but practically less common and somewhat harder to study. It may be common among experts, high-performing athletes, or older adults who are concerned with avoiding cognitive decline. It is associated with worry and exhaustion even when performance remains high.

> [!definition] **Performance-Approach Goal**
> Oriented toward the positive possibility of normative competence: outperforming others, winning, appearing highly able. The person asks "Am I better than others?" Associated with competitive engagement, higher grades in evaluative settings, but typically not with deep intrinsic interest or durable learning.

> [!definition] **Performance-Avoidance Goal**
> Oriented toward the negative possibility of normative incompetence: avoiding appearing stupid or inferior relative to others. The person asks "Am I avoiding looking worse than everyone else?" Consistently associated with the most maladaptive outcomes in the research: surface processing, challenge avoidance, high anxiety, poor intrinsic motivation, and diminished learning.

> [!evidence] **2×2 Model Validation**
> Elliot and McGregor (2001) validated the 2×2 model across two studies using confirmatory factor analysis, demonstrating that the four goal types are empirically distinct (not merely conceptually separable) and have different patterns of relationships to cognitive strategies, exam anxiety, and academic performance. Their results showed: mastery-approach goals predicted deep processing and positive test affect; performance-approach goals predicted surface processing but also higher exam performance; performance-avoidance goals predicted surface processing, disorganisation, and test anxiety; mastery-avoidance goals predicted disorganisation and worry despite strong GPA association.

### The 3×2 Model and Beyond

[[Andrew-J.-Elliot|Andrew J. Elliot]], [[Kenji Murayama]], and [[Reinhard Pekrun]] (2011) proposed a further refinement: the *3×2 model*, which distinguishes not just mastery-based and performance-based competence standards but introduces a third category — *task-based* standards — yielding six goal types (task-approach, task-avoidance, self-approach, self-avoidance, other-approach, other-avoidance). The 3×2 model offers conceptual precision but has been less extensively validated and is not yet the dominant framework in the field.

> [!insight] **The Pattern Across Frameworks**
> Looking across the dichotomous, trichotomous, 2×2, and 3×2 models, a clear pattern emerges: theoretical progress in AGT has consistently been driven by two moves — finer-grained differentiation of the competence standard (what counts as "doing well") and the incorporation of fundamental motivational distinctions (approach vs. avoidance, task vs. self vs. other). Each refinement has clarified the empirical landscape by separating what earlier models conflated.

### Ames's Motivational Climate Theory

Running parallel to the debates about individual goal orientations is [[Carol-Ames|Carol Ames]]'s highly influential work on *motivational climate* — the extent to which a learning environment (a classroom, a sports team, a workplace) signals to participants that success means mastering tasks (a *mastery climate*) or that success means outperforming others (a *performance climate*).

> [!definition] **Motivational Climate**
> Motivational climate refers to the achievement goal structure perceived to be operative in a particular setting. It is determined by the structural features of the environment — how tasks are designed, how authority is distributed, how recognition is given, how students are grouped, how evaluation is conducted, and how time is managed. Ames (1992) used the acronym TARGET (Task, Authority, Recognition, Grouping, Evaluation, Time) to identify the key structural dimensions that shape perceived motivational climate. Crucially, motivational climate influences which goals individuals *adopt* in that setting — independently of their personal goal orientation tendencies.

Motivational climate research has generated one of the most practically important bodies of evidence in [[Educational-Psychology|Educational Psychology]]: that the structural design of a learning environment can systematically shift learners toward mastery or performance orientations, with corresponding effects on engagement, learning strategies, and well-being. This finding has significant implications for [[Fading-—-Instructional-Design-Collins,-Brown-&-Newman|Instructional Design]] and [[Assessment-Design|Assessment Design]].

## Phase IV: Mechanisms & Processes

### How Goals Actually Work: The Cognitive-Motivational Architecture

Understanding *what* the different goal orientations are is less than half the story. The deeper explanatory work of Achievement Goal Theory lies in specifying *how* goal orientation shapes learning — the psychological mechanisms through which a cognitive representation of "what success means" propagates through cognition, affect, and behaviour to produce different learning outcomes. This section examines those mechanisms in depth.

### Mechanism 1: Competence Valuation and Its Implications for Effort

One of the most important — and often misunderstood — mechanisms concerns how different goal orientations change the meaning and value of effort. Under a mastery orientation, effort is instrumentally and intrinsically valuable: it is the means by which competence (defined as learning) is achieved, and it is also a direct expression of engagement with the material. Working hard on something difficult feels like the right thing to be doing, because difficulty is evidence of a worthwhile challenge.

Under a performance-avoidance orientation, the relationship between effort and competence is fundamentally different. Recall that in a differentiated conception of ability, high effort plus poor relative performance is damning evidence of low ability. But there is a corollary: high effort plus good performance is ambiguous evidence about ability, because you might only be performing well *because* you worked so hard. The truly able person performs brilliantly with minimal effort. This logic creates a perverse motivational structure: for students operating under performance-avoidance goals, *not trying* — or appearing not to try — becomes a self-protective strategy. If you fail without trying hard, you have a face-saving excuse. If you succeed without trying hard, you have evidence of high ability. The strategic withdrawal of effort is therefore a rational adaptation to the goal structure, not a character defect.

> [!key-insight] **Self-Handicapping as a Motivational Adaptation**
> The phenomenon of self-handicapping — creating obstacles to one's own performance in advance, so that failure is attributable to the obstacle rather than to low ability — is a direct prediction of AGT under performance-avoidance conditions. Students who stay up all night before an exam, who avoid studying a subject they fear they may be bad at, or who procrastinate until productive work becomes impossible are often engaging in self-handicapping. From the outside, these behaviours look like laziness or poor self-regulation. From the inside, they are rational strategies for protecting the self-concept in an environment where failure signals low ability.

### Mechanism 2: Attention Allocation and Information Processing

Goal orientation shapes not just how much cognitive effort a person deploys but where that effort is directed. This is a crucial mechanism because the *type* of cognitive processing — not just the quantity — determines learning quality.

Under mastery goals, the operative question is "What am I learning? How does this connect to what I already know? Where is my understanding incomplete?" This question directs attention toward meaning, toward connections between ideas, toward the detection of one's own gaps in understanding. It generates what cognitive psychologists call *deep processing* or *elaborative encoding* — the kind of engagement that produces durable, transferable knowledge.

Under performance goals, the operative question is "What do I need to do to perform well on this evaluation?" This question directs attention toward what will be tested, toward surface patterns and correct answers, and away from genuine understanding. It tends to generate *surface processing* or *reproductive encoding* — the kind of engagement that produces temporary retrieval-ready knowledge that quickly decays and does not transfer to novel contexts.

> [!example] **The Study Strategy Divergence**
> Research by [[Ames-and-Archer|Ames and Archer]] (1988) and numerous subsequent studies has documented systematic differences in reported learning strategies between students in mastery-oriented versus performance-oriented conditions. Mastery-oriented students more frequently report using tactics associated with deep processing: self-testing for understanding, connecting new material to prior knowledge, seeking to understand the logic behind procedures rather than merely memorising them, and identifying areas of confusion and seeking help. Performance-oriented students (particularly those high on performance-avoidance) more frequently report surface strategies: rote memorisation, selective attention to likely test items, and avoidance of difficult material that might undermine confidence.

### Mechanism 3: The Response to Failure and the Allocation of Attributions

Perhaps the most consequential mechanism in Achievement Goal Theory concerns how different goal orientations shape the interpretation of — and response to — failure and difficulty. This is where the theory's practical importance is perhaps clearest.

For a person operating under mastery goals, difficulty and even failure are informationally rich and motivationally manageable. Difficulty means the material is genuinely challenging — which means it is worth learning. Failure means "I haven't learned this yet, and here is specific information about what I need to work on." The attribution pattern under mastery orientations tends to emphasise effort and strategy as the primary controllable causes of performance — which means that poor performance can be addressed by adjusting effort and strategy. This produces what [[Carol-S.-Dweck|Carol S. Dweck]] has called a *mastery-oriented response pattern*: increased effort and strategy-seeking after failure, maintained or increased intrinsic interest, and emotional equanimity.

For a person operating under performance-avoidance goals, failure is not informationally useful — it is *threatening*. Because the operative standard is normative competence, poor performance is evidence of low ability, which is precisely the thing they are trying to avoid demonstrating. The attribution pattern under performance-avoidance conditions tends to gravitate toward ability attributions for failure — "I'm just not good at this" — because ability, under the differentiated conception, is the thing most at stake. This produces what Dweck called a *helpless response pattern*: decreased effort after failure, withdrawal from challenge, negative affect, and ultimately disengagement from the domain.

> [!thought-experiment] **The Diagnostic Power of a Failure**
> Imagine two students, both of whom fail the same difficult exam. Student A, operating under mastery goals, asks: "What did I misunderstand? What do I need to study differently?" She schedules more practice, seeks a study partner, asks the instructor about the concepts she missed, and returns to the next exam with stronger understanding. Student B, operating under performance-avoidance goals, asks: "What does this say about my ability?" He concludes that he is not a "maths person," avoids asking for help (which would reveal his confusion publicly), reduces the effort he invests in subsequent assessments (offering himself the excuse of "not really trying"), and eventually withdraws from maths-related coursework. These divergent responses are not determined by the failure itself — they are determined by the goal framework through which the failure is interpreted.

### Mechanism 4: Motivational Climate and Goal Adoption

A crucial and sometimes underappreciated mechanism concerns how goal orientations are not simply brought to learning contexts by individuals but are actively shaped — and sometimes dramatically overridden — by environmental signals. [[Carol-Ames|Carol Ames]]'s [[Motivational-Climate|Motivational Climate]] framework specifies how structural features of learning environments influence the goals individuals adopt.

The TARGET dimensions operate through a common mechanism: they signal to participants what success means in this environment. A classroom in which tasks are varied and offer genuine intellectual challenge, in which students have some authority over their learning choices, in which recognition is given for improvement and effort rather than only for top performance, in which assessment emphasises individual progress rather than normative ranking, and in which sufficient time is given for mastery before evaluation — such a classroom transmits strong mastery-climate signals. Students in such environments, regardless of their prior goal orientation tendencies, tend to adopt mastery goals more readily.

Conversely, a classroom in which tasks are uniform and emphasise correct answers, in which the teacher maintains all authority, in which recognition is given primarily to the highest performers, in which students are sorted by ability and publicly ranked, and in which time pressure is constant — such a classroom transmits performance-climate signals. These structural features activate performance concerns even in students who would prefer to focus on learning.

> [!observation] **The Power of Perceived Climate**
> Studies by Ames and Archer (1988), Midgley and colleagues, and Ntoumanis and Biddle (1999) in sports contexts have consistently shown that *perceived* motivational climate — how students perceive the goal structure of their environment — predicts their goal adoption, strategy use, and affect more strongly than their measured individual goal orientation tendencies. This is a remarkable finding with strong practical implications: environment matters enormously. The goal orientation a person endorses in a research questionnaire may not be the goal orientation they actually pursue in a particular classroom.

### Mechanism 5: Interaction with Competence Beliefs

Achievement Goal Theory does not operate in isolation from the learner's beliefs about their own competence. There is a well-documented interaction between goal orientation and [[Self-Efficacy]] beliefs that shapes the motivational consequences of goal adoption. Specifically, the maladaptive effects of performance-approach goals are most pronounced when a person holds low confidence in their own ability — because in that case, the performance-approach goal is likely to shade into performance-avoidance, as the realistic assessment is that one will not outperform others. Conversely, high self-efficacy can partially buffer against the negative effects of performance-goal conditions, because the person's confidence makes the prospect of favourable social comparison realistic.

## Phase V: Applications, Implications & Limitations

### Applications in Educational and Instructional Design

The practical implications of Achievement Goal Theory for [[Fading-—-Instructional-Design-Collins,-Brown-&-Newman|Instructional Design]] and classroom practice are among the most robustly supported in [[Educational-Psychology|Educational Psychology]]. Ames's TARGET framework provides educators with an actionable framework for auditing and redesigning the motivational climate of their learning environments.

**Task design** is perhaps the most powerful lever. Tasks that present genuine intellectual challenge, that allow for multiple approaches, and that emphasise the process of inquiry rather than the production of a single correct answer transmit mastery climate signals. Research suggests that tasks with what Deweck calls "growth features" — tasks for which effort and strategy genuinely improve outcomes, and where improvement is discernible — are particularly effective at supporting mastery motivation. By contrast, tasks that are uniform, that have single correct answers achievable by anyone who follows the right procedure, or that emphasise speed over depth tend to activate performance concerns.

> [!best-practice] **Task Design for Mastery Climate**
> When designing learning tasks, include features that make growth visible and valued: open-ended components that reward elaborated thinking, opportunities for revision and improvement, problems that can be approached multiple ways, and explicit framing that positions difficulty as an expected and valuable part of learning rather than a signal of failure. Avoid designs that make ability rankings immediately obvious (e.g., public performance boards, visibly stratified task sheets) unless the competitive signal is intentional and the context supports it.

**Feedback and assessment practices** are the second most powerful TARGET dimension. Feedback that is descriptive and growth-informative — telling learners specifically what they have learned, what they have not yet mastered, and what strategies might help — transmits mastery signals. Feedback that is primarily normative — your score compared to the class average, a percentile ranking, a comparative grade — transmits performance signals. Formative assessment practices (assessment *for* learning rather than *of* learning) are more consistent with mastery climate than summative, high-stakes, normatively-benchmarked assessments.

**Recognition practices** matter significantly. When teachers, coaches, or managers publicly recognise only top performers — only the winner, only the person who gets the highest grade — they transmit powerful performance-climate signals to everyone in the group, including the recognised person. When recognition is also given for improvement, for the quality of effort and strategy, or for helping others, mastery-climate signals are stronger.

> [!best-practice] **Assessment Language for Mastery Orientation**
> Feedback that emphasises the learner's developing understanding ("You've clearly grasped the underlying principle here, and your next step might be to test this understanding by applying it to a novel problem") is more mastery-consistent than feedback that emphasises rank or comparison ("You scored in the top quartile"). This does not mean eliminating grading — it means attending carefully to what the *language* of evaluation signals about what matters.

### Applications in Sports, Workplace, and Beyond

AGT was developed primarily in educational settings but its applicability is broadly demonstrated. In [[Sports Psychology]], mastery-oriented athletes demonstrate higher intrinsic motivation, more adaptive responses to setbacks, greater sustained engagement with training, and — over long careers — often superior outcomes to performance-avoidance-oriented athletes, who may win early but burn out or disengage. Coaches who create mastery climates (emphasising skill development, personal improvement, and the intrinsic satisfactions of sport) reliably produce more durable athlete motivation than coaches who emphasise rankings, winning, and comparative performance.

In workplace learning and professional development, the same principles apply. Organisations whose learning cultures send strong mastery-climate signals — where effort and growth are recognised, where admitting uncertainty and asking for help is not penalised, where failure in service of learning is tolerated — tend to produce employees with stronger long-term skill development and higher intrinsic engagement. Performance-climate workplaces may produce short-term output, but often at the cost of learning, creativity, and resilience.

### Current Limitations and Honest Boundaries

Achievement Goal Theory has accumulated an impressive evidence base, but a rigorous treatment requires honest engagement with its limitations — several of which are substantive.

> [!important] **WEIRD Sample Problem**
> Much of the foundational AGT research was conducted with North American and Western European samples, predominantly college students. The generalisability of AGT predictions to non-Western cultures — where, for example, the relationship between social comparison and motivation may differ significantly, or where collective rather than individual competence standards are normative — is an area of ongoing investigation and legitimate uncertainty.

> [!important] **Measurement Inconsistency**
> The field has struggled with inconsistent measurement. Different research groups have used different questionnaire instruments to assess the same goal orientations, often with different item wording that creates subtle but consequential differences in what is actually being measured. Meta-analyses (notably Hulleman et al., 2010) have found that the specific wording of performance-goal items moderates the direction of their relationship to outcomes — performance goals assessed with "doing better than others" language have somewhat different relationships to outcomes than those assessed with "demonstrating ability" language. This measurement heterogeneity makes the integration of findings across studies more difficult than it might appear.

> [!important] **The Performance-Approach Controversy**
> The motivational status of performance-approach goals remains the most actively contested area in the field. Harackiewicz, Barron, and colleagues have defended the adaptive potential of performance-approach goals, particularly in competitive educational settings where high grades have real-world consequences. Elliot, Dweck, and others have argued that these effects are contextually limited and do not generalise to deep learning, creativity, or sustained motivation. A nuanced reading of the evidence suggests that performance-approach goals may be conditionally adaptive — beneficial for short-term competitive performance under high competence beliefs, but not beneficial for long-term intrinsic motivation or for learning that requires genuine understanding rather than strategic performance.

> [!warning] **Avoiding the Mastery-Goal Prescriptivism Trap**
> A common misreading of AGT — one worth flagging explicitly — is to conclude that mastery goals are simply "good" and performance goals are simply "bad," and that the practical implication is to lecture students or athletes into adopting mastery goals. The evidence does not support this prescriptive conclusion, for several reasons. First, the relationship between goal orientation and outcomes is moderated by multiple factors including competence beliefs, task type, and cultural context. Second, simply telling people to adopt mastery goals rarely works — goal orientation is shaped more strongly by environmental structures than by explicit exhortation. Third, there are genuine motivational functions served by performance goals in certain contexts. The appropriate practical response to AGT is to design environments that support mastery orientation, not to diagnose and correct individuals who are responding rationally to their environment.

> This report draws on foundational theoretical texts by Nicholls (1984), Ames (1992), and Elliot & McGregor (2001), as well as the extensive empirical literature generated by these frameworks over four decades. The treatment of mechanisms integrates findings from laboratory studies, classroom observational research, and longitudinal investigations. Meta-analytic evidence from Hulleman et al. (2010) and Murayama and Elliot (2012) is incorporated where it provides aggregated estimates of effect sizes. Claims about mechanism are generally well-supported by converging evidence from multiple methodologies; claims about practical applications are more conditional and context-dependent, as noted throughout. Speculative proposals (e.g., about the mechanisms underlying mastery-avoidance goals in expert populations) are marked as such.

## Phase VI: Synthesis & Integration

### The Deep Pattern: How Goals Create Self-Fulfilling Epistemic Environments

Stepping back from the individual mechanisms and evidence streams, Achievement Goal Theory reveals a deep and somewhat sobering pattern about how goals shape not just performance but the very quality of a person's relationship with knowledge. The goal orientation someone brings to a learning encounter does not merely influence how hard they work — it shapes what they attend to, what they retain, what they are willing to risk, and ultimately what they learn to value about the act of learning itself.

A person who consistently operates under mastery-approach orientation builds, over time, a kind of epistemic infrastructure: a well-developed set of deep processing strategies, a comfortable relationship with difficulty and confusion, a habit of self-monitoring for genuine understanding, and an identity as a learner who grows through effort. Each successful navigation of a difficult challenge reinforces all of these. The goal orientation becomes, in a real sense, self-fulfilling.

A person who consistently operates under performance-avoidance orientation builds a different and more precarious infrastructure: a set of impression-management strategies, a defensive relationship with difficulty, a habit of monitoring for threats to self-concept rather than gaps in understanding, and an identity anchored in demonstrated ability rather than developing competence. Each avoided challenge — each strategically chosen easy task — reinforces the avoidance pattern and, crucially, denies the person the practice at navigating difficulty that would actually build their competence. The irony is profound: performance-avoidance goals, which are driven by fear of appearing incompetent, tend over time to actually *undermine* competence development by discouraging the very behaviours — challenge-seeking, help-seeking, error-correction — through which competence is built.

### The "So What?": What This Understanding Enables

Understanding Achievement Goal Theory gives a practitioner or reflective learner something important: a causal framework for interpreting motivational phenomena that would otherwise look opaque or character-based. Student X avoids asking questions in class — not because she is lazy, but because in the perceived performance climate of her classroom, displaying confusion is evidence of low ability. Student Y works incredibly hard for weeks but shows no transfer to related problems — not because he lacks intelligence, but because his performance goals directed his processing toward what would be evaluated, not toward the underlying conceptual structure. Learner Z, upon receiving a critical piece of feedback, becomes defensive and dismissive — not because she lacks self-awareness, but because her goal orientation frames critical feedback as a threat to self-concept rather than information useful for growth.

This framework does not excuse these behaviours, but it does *explain* them in ways that make intervention possible. And the interventions it suggests are structural — change the environment, change the signals, change the goal orientation — rather than purely exhortative. You cannot simply tell people to want the right things; you can design conditions that make it easier and more natural to want them.

> [!connections-and-links]
> **Internal PKB Connections**
>
> This report on [[Achievement-Goal-Theory|Achievement Goal Theory]] connects to existing knowledge in your PKB in the following ways:
>
> - **[[Metacognition-&-Motivation|Metacognition & Motivation]]** — The intersection is direct and rich. AGT predicts that mastery-oriented learners will engage in more sophisticated metacognitive monitoring — because their goal is to understand, not merely to perform, they have a stronger incentive to accurately detect their own comprehension gaps. Performance-avoidance learners, by contrast, may engage in motivated inaccuracy: deliberately *not* monitoring their understanding too carefully, because accurate self-assessment might reveal incompetence. This suggests that metacognitive strategy instruction will be most effective when embedded in mastery-climate environments.
>
> - **[[Growth-Mindset-Implicit-Theories-of-Intelligence|Growth Mindset]]** — Dweck's growth mindset framework is in many ways the upstream cognitive cause of mastery goal adoption. An entity theory of intelligence (fixed mindset) predicts performance goal adoption because, if ability is fixed, the rational goal is to demonstrate it rather than develop it. An incremental theory (growth mindset) predicts mastery goal adoption because, if ability can be developed, then development is the rational focus. The two frameworks are therefore deeply complementary, with mindset theory explaining *why* individuals differ in goal orientation tendencies, and AGT specifying the *mechanisms* through which those tendencies translate into learning behaviours.
>
> - **[[Metacognition-&-Critical-Thinking|Metacognition & Critical Thinking]]** — Critical thinking requires intellectual risk-taking: the willingness to question assumptions, to entertain uncomfortable possibilities, and to revise beliefs in light of evidence. All of these behaviours are more natural under mastery orientation than under performance orientation. In a performance-oriented environment, intellectual risk-taking is potentially costly (you might be wrong, publicly), while in a mastery-oriented environment it is potentially rewarding (you might learn something new).
>
> - **[[Schema-Theory|Schema Theory]]** — Schema formation and elaboration depend on the kind of deep processing that mastery goals preferentially support. When learners are focused on understanding rather than performance, they are more likely to engage in the effortful assimilation and accommodation that schema development requires. This connection suggests that schema formation is not merely a cognitive process but a motivationally conditioned one — the conditions under which deep learning occurs are also the conditions that AGT identifies as mastery-conducive.
>
> - **[[Critical-Thinking-in-Education|Critical Thinking in Education]]** — Instructional approaches that aim to develop critical thinking are unlikely to succeed in performance-climate environments, because critical thinking requires the very intellectual behaviours — challenge engagement, error tolerance, cognitive risk — that performance-avoidance goals suppress. This suggests that critical thinking pedagogy and motivational climate design are not separate problems but deeply interconnected aspects of a single design challenge.
>
> The pattern these connections reveal is significant: Achievement Goal Theory is not merely a theory about motivation. It is a theory about the motivational conditions under which deep cognitive engagement becomes possible. It connects to virtually every aspect of your PKB focused on how learning actually works — because goal orientation shapes not just how much people learn, but what kind of learning they are capable of in a given moment.

## Phase VII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Achievement Goal (Nicholls, 1984; Ames, 1992)**
> A cognitive representation of the purpose an individual pursues in an achievement context. It specifies the standard of competence against which the person evaluates their performance, determines what counts as success or failure, and organises attention, affect, and behaviour accordingly. Not to be confused with a performance target (a desired level of achievement); achievement goals are about the *type* of competence sought, not the *level*.

> [!definition] **Mastery Goal (Ames, 1992; Dweck's "Learning Goal")**
> An achievement goal in which success is defined as developing competence — learning, improving, or mastering the task. The standard of reference is intrapersonal or task-inherent. Associated with deep cognitive processing, persistence in the face of difficulty, intrinsic motivation, help-seeking, and long-term learning. Also known as learning goals (Dweck), task goals (Nicholls), or task-involvement goals.

> [!definition] **Performance Goal (Ames, 1992; Nicholls's "Ego Goal")**
> An achievement goal in which success is defined as demonstrating superior competence relative to others. The standard of reference is normative. In later frameworks, distinguished as performance-approach (approaching normative superiority) and performance-avoidance (avoiding normative inferiority). Associated with surface cognitive processing, vulnerability to failure, and contextually variable outcomes.

> [!definition] **Performance-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & Harackiewicz, 1996)**
> An achievement goal oriented toward avoiding the demonstration of normative incompetence — the goal of not looking worse than others. Associated with the most consistently maladaptive outcomes in the research literature: surface processing, challenge avoidance, high anxiety, disorganised study, and diminished intrinsic motivation. Arises most naturally when both competence concerns are salient and self-efficacy is low.

> [!definition] **Mastery-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & McGregor, 2001)**
> An achievement goal oriented toward avoiding intrapersonal incompetence — not failing to learn, not declining in ability relative to one's own prior standard. Theoretically predicted by the 2×2 framework and empirically supported in expert and high-performer populations. Associated with worry and perfectionism-related anxiety even at high performance levels.

> [!definition] **Ego Involvement (Nicholls, 1984)**
> A motivational state in which ability concerns are salient and the person is preoccupied with demonstrating (or protecting) their ability relative to others. Ego involvement is the psychological state corresponding to performance goal adoption, particularly performance-avoidance. It activates the differentiated conception of ability, making effort a double-edged sword.

> [!definition] **Motivational Climate (Ames, 1992)**
> The goal structure perceived to be operative in a particular achievement environment, shaped by structural features including task design, authority distribution, recognition practices, grouping, evaluation methods, and time management (TARGET). Motivational climate is distinct from individual goal orientation — it is an environmental variable that powerfully shapes which goals individuals adopt in a given setting.

> [!definition] **2×2 Framework (Elliot & McGregor, 2001)**
> A theoretical model of achievement goals that crosses two dimensions — goal content (mastery vs. performance) with motivational valence (approach vs. avoidance) — to yield four distinct goal types: mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance. Currently the dominant framework in the field, though contested regarding the adaptive potential of performance-approach goals.

> [!definition] **TARGET Framework (Ames, 1992)**
> An acronym identifying the key structural dimensions of learning environments that shape motivational climate: Task (design and complexity), Authority (distribution of decision-making power), Recognition (how achievement is acknowledged), Grouping (how learners are sorted and structured), Evaluation (how competence is assessed), and Time (how temporal pressure is structured). Used as an audit and design tool for motivational climate engineering.

> [!definition] **Differentiated Conception of Ability (Nicholls, 1984)**
> The implicit theory of ability in which effort and ability are understood as separate entities: high effort combined with poor performance implies low ability, while high performance with low effort implies high ability. This conception is cognitively more complex than the undifferentiated conception, emerges later developmentally, and is the basis of ego involvement and performance goal orientation.

> [!cite] **Hulleman, C. S., Schrager, S. M., Bodmann, S. M., & Harackiewicz, J. M. (2010). A meta-analytic review of achievement goal measures: Different labels for the same constructs or different constructs with similar labels? *Psychological Bulletin, 136*(3), 422–449.**
> A critical meta-analysis demonstrating that measurement inconsistency — specifically, differences in how performance goal items are worded — significantly moderates the effects attributed to performance goals. An essential corrective to naive readings of the empirical literature and a key text for understanding the limitations discussed in Phase V.

> [!cite] **Ames, C., & Archer, J. (1988). Achievement goals in the classroom: Students' learning strategies and motivation processes. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 80*(3), 260–267.**
> An influential classroom study documenting systematic differences in reported learning strategies between students in mastery-oriented versus performance-oriented classes. One of the clearest empirical demonstrations of the link between motivational climate and cognitive strategy use. Relevant to Phase IV and Phase V.

> [!cite] **Elliot, A. J., Murayama, K., & Pekrun, R. (2011). A 3×2 achievement goal model. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 103*(3), 632–648.**
> Introduces the 3×2 model, which distinguishes task-based, self-based, and other-based standards of competence crossed with approach-avoidance valence to yield six goal types. Represents the current frontier of AGT theoretical development. Relevant as an expansion topic and as context for Phase III's discussion of theoretical evolution.

> [!cite] **Murayama, K., & Elliot, A. J. (2012). The competition–performance relation: A meta-analytic review and test of the opposing processes model of competition and performance. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(6), 1035–1070.**
> A meta-analysis examining the relationship between competitive contexts, goal adoption, and performance. Relevant to the contested question of when performance-approach goals may be adaptive. Discussed in the limitations section of Phase V.

### C. Methodology and Sources Note

> [!methodology-and-sources] **Research Grounding for This Report**
> This report synthesises approximately four decades of theoretical development and empirical research in Achievement Goal Theory. The theoretical narrative draws primarily on original theoretical papers (Nicholls, 1984; Ames, 1992; Elliot, 1997; Elliot & McGregor, 2001) rather than textbook summaries, ensuring fidelity to the primary intellectual contributions. Empirical claims are grounded in peer-reviewed research, with preference given to findings replicated across multiple studies or supported by meta-analytic evidence. Where the evidence is mixed or contested — most significantly regarding performance-approach goals — this is noted explicitly with reference to the relevant evidence streams rather than resolved artificially. The mechanisms discussed in Phase IV integrate findings from correlational classroom studies, experimental manipulations, and longitudinal investigations; their causal status varies, and this variation is implicitly marked in the language used ("is associated with" vs. "causes"). Developmental claims about the differentiated/undifferentiated ability distinction draw on Nicholls's original developmental work. Cultural generalisability claims are deliberately hedged, reflecting the WEIRD-sample limitation of the primary evidence base. The practical applications in Phase V are supported by the evidence base but presented with appropriate conditionality, given that the path from laboratory findings to classroom implementation involves contextual factors not fully captured in the research literature.

### D. Expansion Topics

> [!further-exploration] **Deepening Your Practice**

> [!topic-idea] [[Self-Determination-Theory-—-Foundational-Report|Self-Determination Theory — Foundational Report]]
> Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000) is the dominant motivational framework in contemporary educational psychology and represents the most important theoretical complement and counterpoint to AGT. Where AGT focuses on the *content* of goals (what standard of competence is operative), SDT focuses on the *regulatory style* of motivation (whether motivation is autonomous/intrinsic or controlled/extrinsic). A comparative treatment would clarify the significant areas of convergence (both frameworks identify conditions that support intrinsic motivation and deep learning) and important areas of distinction (SDT's autonomy construct maps onto mastery climate but is not identical to it; AGT's approach-avoidance dimension has no direct SDT parallel). Priority: **High** — SDT is encountered throughout the learning sciences literature and fluency in both frameworks is close to essential.

> [!topic-idea] [[Motivational Climate Design — Applied Guide]]
> A deep dive into the practical engineering of mastery-oriented learning environments, using Ames's TARGET framework as the primary organisational lens. This report would move from the abstract dimensions (Task, Authority, Recognition, Grouping, Evaluation, Time) to specific, evidence-based design decisions at each level — covering task design principles, feedback language, grouping structures, assessment practices, and the temporal architecture of learning sequences. Would draw on both AGT-derived research and complementary work from [[Universal Design for Learning]], [[Formative-Assessment|Formative Assessment]], and [[Project-Based Learning]]. Priority: **High** — directly actionable from this foundational treatment.

> [!topic-idea] [[Implicit Theories of Intelligence & Growth Mindset — Foundational Report]]
> Dweck's framework on entity vs. incremental theories of intelligence is the cognitive-developmental upstream cause of goal orientation differences. Understanding *why* people adopt different goal orientations requires understanding the implicit beliefs about the nature of ability that drive those orientations. This report would cover the experimental evidence base for mindset interventions, the theoretical relationship between mindset and AGT, and the important recent replication debates that have complicated the field's earlier enthusiasm for brief mindset interventions. Priority: **High** — directly linked to this report and already referenced in the PKB.

> [!topic-idea] [[Achievement-Emotions-—-Pekrun's-Control-Value-Theory|Achievement Emotions — Pekrun's Control-Value Theory]]
> Reinhard Pekrun's Control-Value Theory of achievement emotions provides a rich account of the affective consequences of achievement goal orientations, including enjoyment, boredom, anxiety, pride, shame, hopelessness, and relief. Because emotional experience is a major outcome variable in AGT research, and because emotions in turn influence cognitive processing and motivation, this framework deepens the AGT account significantly. The connection between performance-avoidance goals and test anxiety, and between mastery goals and epistemic enjoyment, are particularly well-developed in Pekrun's work. Priority: **Medium** — enriches AGT but is not necessary for applying it.

> [!topic-idea] [[Assessment-Design-&-Goal-Orientation|Assessment Design & Goal Orientation]]
> Assessment is the most powerful climate-signalling dimension in most formal educational settings, and yet it is also the dimension most resistant to change — external pressures (standardised testing, grading systems, institutional accountability) heavily constrain what educators can do. This report would examine the evidence on how different assessment practices (formative vs. summative, criterion-referenced vs. norm-referenced, portfolio vs. examination, self-assessment vs. external evaluation) signal different goal orientations, with attention to both the design of individual assessments and the systemic constraints that shape what is possible. Priority: **High** — assessment design is a critical application domain.

> [!topic-idea] [[Epistemic Curiosity and the Intrinsic Pleasures of Inquiry]]
> This expansion would explore the motivational states that are most fully aligned with mastery-approach orientation: curiosity, wonder, the intrinsic satisfactions of understanding, and what Csikszentmihalyi calls [[Flow]] in intellectual work. It would connect the AGT prediction that mastery goals support intrinsic motivation to the broader literature on curiosity-driven learning, inquiry-based pedagogy, and the conditions under which learners become genuinely self-directed. Priority: **Medium** — enriches and extends the mastery-orientation picture into positive motivational territory.
