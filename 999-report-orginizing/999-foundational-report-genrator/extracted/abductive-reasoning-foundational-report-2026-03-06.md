# Abductive Reasoning and Its Role in Learning

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Context Setting

Every scientist who has ever looked at a puzzling datum and thought "that's strange — what if...?" has performed an act of abduction. Every clinician who narrows a list of differential diagnoses has done the same. Every student who notices something odd in an experiment and ventures a tentative explanation is engaging in the most creative of the three classical forms of inference. And yet, of the three — deduction, induction, and abduction — abduction is the form that receives the least formal instruction, the least philosophical attention in most curricula, and the least recognition as a teachable skill.

This asymmetry is strange when you consider what abduction actually does. [[Deductive-Reasoning|Deduction]] takes a rule and a case and tells you the result. [[Inductive-Reasoning|Induction]] takes many cases and results and works toward a general rule. But [[Abduction|abduction]] works backward from a surprising result to the best available explanation — and in doing so, it is the only one of the three that generates genuinely new hypotheses. Deduction derives what must follow; induction generalizes what tends to occur; abduction creates what might explain. It is, in a precise philosophical sense, the logic of discovery.

**Why this matters for learning** is not merely a pedagogical footnote. If the goal of education extends beyond the transmission of settled knowledge toward the development of thinkers capable of formulating original hypotheses, tolerating ambiguity, and navigating genuine uncertainty, then abductive reasoning belongs at the center of the curriculum — not as a special topic in philosophy, but as a foundational cognitive skill woven through every domain.

This report establishes the conceptual foundations of abductive reasoning, maps the major theoretical frameworks that have been developed to understand it, examines the cognitive mechanisms through which it operates, and explores what its centrality in learning science, [[Scientific Reasoning in Education|scientific reasoning]], and [[Design Thinking Pedagogy|design thinking]] means for educational practice. It is written for educators, instructional designers, and intellectually curious learners who sense that something important is happening when students venture hypotheses under conditions of uncertainty — and who want a precise vocabulary and theoretical map to understand what that something is.

**Scope.** This report covers abductive reasoning as a logical, cognitive, and pedagogical phenomenon. It addresses the philosophical genealogy from [[Charles Sanders Peirce|Peirce]] through contemporary analytic philosophy, cognitive science accounts of hypothesis formation, and the implications for instructional design and classroom practice. It does not provide an exhaustive survey of every domain in which abductive reasoning has been theorized (medical diagnosis, legal argumentation, and artificial intelligence each warrant their own treatments), though each is introduced as illustrative context. It does not presuppose familiarity with formal logic, though engagement with the report will significantly deepen the reader's logical literacy.

**Roadmap.** Phase II excavates the definitional bedrock — what abduction is, where the concept came from, and the critical distinctions that prevent common confusions. Phase III surveys the major theoretical frameworks — [[Inference-to-the-Best-Explanation|IBE]], eco-cognitive abduction, naturalized epistemology, and design thinking — that have developed the concept beyond its Peircean origins. Phase IV descends to the mechanism: how does abductive reasoning actually work in a thinking mind, step by step? Phase V examines real-world applications in education, science, medicine, and design, alongside a candid account of what we do not yet know. Phase VI draws the threads together. The appendix provides a lexicon, references, and expansion topics for ongoing inquiry.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Before You Begin**
> Before reading further, pause and recall a time you formed a hypothesis — about why a machine wasn't working, why a student seemed disengaged, why a historical event unfolded as it did, or why an experiment produced an unexpected result. What moved you from noticing the surprising fact to proposing an explanation? Was it logical necessity? Statistical regularity? Or something more like an imaginative leap? Hold that experience in mind as you read — you will encounter its theoretical description within the next several thousand words.

## Phase II: Conceptual Foundations

### The Intellectual Genealogy: From Aristotle to Peirce

The story of abductive reasoning as a distinct logical category begins — as so many modern ideas do — with a misreading made productive. The term *abduction* is a translation of Aristotle's *apagoge*, which he used in the *Prior Analytics* to describe a kind of syllogism in which the middle term is not known with certainty but is merely plausible. Aristotle's apagoge was less a theory of discovery than a classification of imperfect inference. The concept remained relatively dormant for two millennia, serving at most as a footnote in the history of logic.

It was [[Charles Sanders Peirce]] (1839–1914), the American philosopher, mathematician, and logician — arguably the most original philosopher the United States has produced — who transformed this footnote into a foundational principle. Peirce spent much of his career developing a comprehensive account of inference and semiotics, and within that project, abduction occupied a crucial position. He argued that the three forms of inference correspond to three distinct logical movements across the same syllogistic components, and that understanding their differences was essential for understanding how knowledge grows.

Peirce's mature formulation of abduction appears in his Harvard lectures of 1903 and in various manuscript fragments, but his most quoted articulation runs as follows: the discovery of a surprising fact, *C*, combined with the realization that *if H were true, C would be a matter of course*, licenses the conjecture that *H* might be true. This is a far cry from the iron necessity of deductive inference, and it is not the statistical generalization of induction. It is, rather, a creative movement from effect to possible cause — or, more precisely, from surprising observation to explanatory hypothesis.

> [!definition] **Abductive Reasoning (Peirce, 1903)**
> The logical form of inference that moves from a surprising or anomalous observation to the hypothesis that would, if true, render that observation intelligible or expected. Abduction does NOT establish that the hypothesis is true — only that it is worth investigating. It is the logic of hypothesis *generation*, not hypothesis *confirmation*. It is sometimes called *retroduction* in Peirce's earlier writings, and its more recent analytic reconstruction is known as [[Inference-to-the-Best-Explanation|Inference to the Best Explanation]].

> [!definition] **The Abductive Syllogism**
> Peirce's canonical form of the abductive syllogism:
> - The surprising fact *C* is observed.
> - But if *H* were true, *C* would be a matter of course.
> - Therefore, there is reason to suspect that *H* is true.
> This form is explicitly non-deductive: the conclusion does not follow necessarily from the premises. The warrant for the inference is not logical compulsion but *explanatory fit*. Importantly, *H* must be genuinely explanatory — not merely a restatement of *C*, and not an arbitrary conjecture, but a hypothesis that *makes* *C* intelligible.

### The Triadic Structure: Deduction, Induction, Abduction

To understand what makes abduction distinctive, it is essential to see it in contrast with its logical siblings. Peirce's framework organizes all three forms of inference around the same three elements: a **Rule** (a general principle), a **Case** (a particular instance or token), and a **Result** (an observable outcome or fact). What differs among the three forms is which element is inferred from the other two.

**Deduction** begins with the Rule and the Case and derives the Result. *All metals expand when heated* (Rule); *this sample is a metal* (Case); *therefore this sample will expand when heated* (Result). The movement is from general principle to particular prediction, and the conclusion follows necessarily if the premises are true. Deduction is the form of inference that *applies* established knowledge.

**Induction** begins with the Case and the Result and constructs the Rule. *This sample is a metal and expands when heated; that sample is a metal and expands when heated;...* (many Cases and Results); *therefore, probably, all metals expand when heated* (Rule). Induction generalizes from observed instances to probable regularities. It extends knowledge but does not guarantee it.

**Abduction** begins with the Rule and the Result and infers the Case. *All metals expand when heated* (Rule); *this sample is expanding when heated* (surprising Result); *therefore, this sample might be a metal* (Case as hypothesis). The movement here is from observation back to explanatory classification — from a phenomenon in need of explanation to a hypothesis that would explain it. Abduction is the form that *creates* new hypotheses worthy of testing.

> [!key-claim] **The Generative Asymmetry of Abduction**
> Among the three forms of inference, only abduction generates genuinely *new* content — hypotheses that were not already present in the premises. Deduction is truth-preserving but non-ampliative (it cannot tell you more than the premises already contain). Induction is ampliative but not creative (it generalizes from what has already been observed). Abduction is both ampliative and creative: it projects an explanatory structure that was not directly observed and was not logically necessitated. This is precisely why [[Peirce]] insisted that "all the ideas of science come to it by the way of Abduction" (CP 5.145).

### Foundational Distinctions: What Abduction Is Not

Several persistent confusions must be addressed before the theoretical landscape can be navigated productively.

**Abduction is not guessing.** Although abduction involves conjecture, it is constrained conjecture — constrained by the requirement that the proposed hypothesis would, if true, explain the observed data. An arbitrary guess about why the car won't start ("because leprechauns removed the engine") may technically satisfy the logical form, but it fails the requirement that the hypothesis be *plausible* — that it engage with what is known about the causal structure of the world. Peirce's later writings increasingly emphasized the role of *instinct*, *scientific sensibility*, and domain knowledge in constraining which hypotheses emerge as worthy candidates.

**Abduction is not a form of weak deduction.** One cannot strengthen an abductive inference by adding more premises until it becomes deductively valid without fundamentally changing what is being done. Abduction operates in a different inferential register — one governed by explanatory considerations rather than logical entailment. The epistemically appropriate response to an abductive conclusion is tentative acceptance *as a hypothesis for investigation*, not confident assertion as established fact.

**Abduction is not the same as analogical reasoning.** [[Analogical-Reasoning|Analogical inference]] reasons from the similarity between cases to probable shared properties: *A and B are similar in ways x, y, z; A has property q; therefore B probably has property q*. Abduction can *use* analogy — a doctor recognizing that a patient's presentation resembles a prior case may be abductively generating a hypothesis — but the logical form is distinct. Analogy operates by structural mapping; abduction operates by explanatory fitness.

> [!definition] **Context of Discovery vs. Context of Justification**
> A foundational distinction in [[Philosophy of Science|philosophy of science]], associated with [[Hans Reichenbach]] (1938), between the *context of discovery* (the psychological and heuristic process by which hypotheses are generated) and the *context of justification* (the logical process by which hypotheses are tested and confirmed or disconfirmed). Peirce's abduction belongs primarily to the context of discovery, though [[Inference-to-the-Best-Explanation|Inference to the Best Explanation]] attempts to give it a role in the context of justification as well. The distinction matters pedagogically: abductive reasoning instruction concerns different cognitive skills than hypothesis-testing instruction, and conflating the two leads to curricular design errors.

> [!definition] **Abductive Competence**
> The cluster of cognitive, epistemic, and motivational capacities that support effective abductive reasoning. These include: sensitivity to anomaly (noticing when observed data does not fit existing expectations); explanatory imagination (the ability to generate multiple candidate hypotheses); plausibility assessment (the ability to evaluate which hypotheses are more or less likely to explain the data given background knowledge); and tolerance for provisional commitment (the willingness to act on a hypothesis while holding it as revisable). Abductive competence is developed, not innate — it can be enhanced through instruction and practice.

> [!reflection] **Deepening Your Understanding**
> 1. The distinction between deduction, induction, and abduction is not merely a textbook taxonomy — it maps onto fundamentally different cognitive acts. Which of the three most resembles how you typically approach novel problems? What might this suggest about blind spots in your thinking?
> 2. Peirce's claim that science advances through abduction is both celebrated and contested. Can you think of a scientific discovery that appears to have followed an abductive pattern — where a surprising observation prompted a creative hypothesis?
> 3. The distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification has been criticized for being too clean. Do you think the process of generating a hypothesis is ever entirely separate from the process of evaluating it?

## Phase III: Theoretical Landscape

### The Analytic Reconstruction: Inference to the Best Explanation

Peirce's formulation of abduction, while philosophically rich, left open a critical problem: how do we determine which hypothesis is the *best* candidate when multiple hypotheses could explain the same observations? This question became the central concern for the analytic philosophy tradition's reconstruction of abduction, which crystallized under the name **Inference to the Best Explanation** (IBE).

The term itself was coined by [[Gilbert Harman]] in a landmark 1965 paper in *The Philosophical Review*. Harman argued that many ordinary and scientific inferences that appear to be inductive are better understood as inferences to the explanation that would best account for the data. When a doctor infers that a patient has pneumonia because pneumonia would best explain the symptoms, the inference is not a simple induction from observed frequencies but a holistic judgment about explanatory fit. Harman's IBE was deliberately informal — he was more concerned with identifying the pattern than with providing a precise algorithm.

The most systematic and influential philosophical treatment of IBE came from [[Peter Lipton]] in his landmark work *Inference to the Best Explanation* (first edition 1991, second edition 2004). Lipton introduced a crucial distinction between the **loveliest** explanation and the **likeliest** explanation. A lovely explanation is one that provides a deep, illuminating, and unifying account of the phenomena — what Lipton called an explanation that offers the most *understanding*. A likely explanation is simply the one most probably true given the evidence. Lipton's central thesis — and his central puzzle — is that we are often drawn toward lovely explanations, and this pull is not epistemically irrational: explanatory loveliness is a *guide* to probability, even if the two are not identical.

> [!key-claim] **Lipton's Contrastive Explanationism**
> Lipton argued that explanation is typically contrastive: we explain why *P* rather than *Q* (where *Q* is a foil — the alternative that didn't happen). The contrastive structure helps sharpen abductive inference by clarifying what a good explanation must account for. A hypothesis is good not merely if it explains the observed fact, but if it explains *why that fact rather than the obvious alternative*. This contrastive dimension has significant pedagogical implications: teaching students to ask "Why this, rather than that?" is teaching them to sharpen their abductive inferences.

> [!counter-argument] **Van Fraassen's Objection: The Bad Lot Problem**
> The philosopher [[Bas van Fraassen]] mounted the most incisive challenge to IBE: the *bad lot problem*. Even if one hypothesis explains the data better than all the alternatives considered, it does not follow that the hypothesis is probably true — we might simply be choosing the best of a bad lot, with the true explanation never having entered our consideration. IBE cannot guarantee that the best available explanation is good in any absolute sense. This objection does not refute IBE — inference under uncertainty always faces such limitations — but it underscores that abductive conclusions are genuinely provisional and that the quality of the *pool* of candidate hypotheses matters as much as the selection criteria.

### Eco-Cognitive and Model-Based Abduction

A significantly different theoretical tradition developed outside analytic philosophy, drawing on Peirce's semiotics more directly. [[Umberto Eco]], the Italian semiotician and novelist, contributed important refinements to abductive theory in *Semiotics and the Philosophy of Language* (1984) and *The Limits of Interpretation* (1990). Eco distinguished among **overcoded abduction** (where the rule to apply is well-known — a trivial case, as in recognizing a letter of the alphabet), **undercoded abduction** (where several alternative rules are possible and one must be selected — the typical case of diagnostic reasoning), and **creative abduction** (where the rule itself must be invented — the characteristic case of genuine scientific discovery).

Eco's taxonomy clarified an important point: abductive reasoning is not a single cognitive act but a family of related acts differing in how much creative work is required. Most educational discussions focus on the undercoded and creative ends of the spectrum — and these are the educationally most important.

[[Lorenzo Magnani|Lorenzo Magnani's]] research programme — developed across *Abduction, Reason, and Science* (2001) and *Abductive Cognition* (2009) — extended the concept further into cognitive science and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Magnani introduced the concept of **manipulative abduction**: hypothesis formation that occurs not in purely mental deliberation but through physical manipulation of the environment. When a scientist sets up an experiment, rearranges apparatus, builds a model, or sketches a diagram, the manipulation itself can generate abductive insights that would not arise from contemplation alone. This is a distributed, embodied conception of abduction that aligns with contemporary [[Embodied-and-Situated-Cognition-—-What-Text-Based-PKBs-Cannot-Capture|embodied and situated cognition]] research.

> [!insight] **Why Manipulative Abduction Matters for Learning Design**
> Magnani's concept of manipulative abduction reframes the role of physical experimentation, prototyping, and hands-on activity in education. From a purely representationalist viewpoint, such activities are vehicles for applying or testing antecedently formed hypotheses. From a manipulative abductive viewpoint, the physical activity *generates* hypotheses — the encounter with recalcitrant materials, unexpected model behaviors, or the physical act of building something creates the abductive occasion that cognitive deliberation alone would not produce. This is a strong theoretical warrant for hands-on learning environments that goes beyond the familiar argument about motivation or embodied encoding.

### Naturalized Accounts: Abduction in Cognitive Science

[[Norwood Russell Hanson]], writing in *Patterns of Discovery* (1958), offered the first sustained cognitive science framing of abduction before cognitive science had a name. Hanson argued that what a scientist *sees* when looking at data is always theory-laden — observations do not come to the scientist as raw, theory-neutral inputs, but already organized by conceptual frameworks. This has important consequences for abduction: the surprising fact that triggers abductive reasoning is not simply a datum but a datum *read through* prior understanding. This means that background knowledge is not merely a constraint on hypothesis generation — it is constitutive of what counts as surprising in the first place.

[[Jaakko Hintikka]], developing what he called the *interrogative model of inquiry*, argued that all scientific inquiry can be modeled as a structured question-answer game in which the scientist poses questions to Nature and Nature responds with observations. On this model, abduction is the strategic reasoning that guides which questions to pose next — it is inherently forward-looking as well as backward-looking. The interrogative model has been influential in science education for its emphasis on question quality as a proxy for reasoning quality.

> [!key-claim] **Theory-Ladenness and Abductive Sensitivity**
> Hanson's argument that observation is theory-laden means that developing abductive competence is inseparable from developing richer domain knowledge. A novice who lacks the conceptual framework to *recognize* an anomaly cannot initiate an abductive inference. A physicist trained in classical mechanics will not find quantum superposition initially surprising in the way a physicist fluent in classical mechanics will — because the surprise depends on knowing what the rule predicts and recognizing that the observed result deviates from it. This is one of the underappreciated reasons why [[Domain Knowledge]] acquisition and reasoning skill development are complementary rather than competing educational goals.

### The Design Thinking Connection

[[Roger Martin]], in *The Opposable Mind* (2007) and *The Design of Business* (2009), argued that the reasoning form characteristic of great designers and business innovators is precisely abduction — specifically, the ability to form *new hunches* based on limited evidence and to develop those hunches toward viable solutions. Martin contrasted abductive thinking with the reliability-oriented [[Deductive-Reasoning|deductive]] and [[Inductive-Reasoning|inductive]] thinking that dominates conventional management practice, arguing that organizations oriented exclusively toward "proof before commitment" systematically underinvest in abductive thinking and therefore in genuine innovation.

Martin's work connected abduction to [[Design Thinking|design thinking]] pedagogy more broadly — a pedagogical tradition associated with IDEO, Stanford's d.school, and curricula from design engineering to social innovation. In this tradition, the explicit cultivation of tolerance for ambiguity, the generation of multiple candidate hypotheses (prototypes), and the iteration between hypothesis and test are not merely good design practices but the outward expression of abductive cognition.

> [!reflection] **Engaging the Landscape**
> 1. IBE and eco-cognitive abduction represent quite different research programmes — one rooted in analytic philosophy and concerned with inference standards, the other rooted in semiotics and cognitive science and concerned with the variety of abductive acts. What would a synthesis of these perspectives add that neither alone provides?
> 2. Van Fraassen's bad lot problem feels like a devastating critique — but consider that all reasoning under uncertainty faces analogous problems. Does the bad lot problem give you a reason to distrust abduction specifically, or simply to be epistemically humble about all non-deductive inference?
> 3. Martin's claim that organizations systematically undervalue abductive thinking is an empirical claim about organizational cognition. What evidence would you want to see before accepting or rejecting it?

## Phase IV: Mechanisms & Processes

### The Cognitive Anatomy of Abductive Inference

Understanding how abductive reasoning actually works — the sequence of cognitive operations through which a thinker moves from surprising observation to explanatory hypothesis — requires attending to several distinct stages, each with its own cognitive demands and potential failure modes. The process is not simply "noticing something odd and guessing why." It is a structured cognitive operation with describable components.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Testing Your Understanding**
> Before reading the following account of the abductive process, try to describe in your own words the mental steps you take when you encounter something you can't explain and try to figure out what is going on. How many distinct moves can you identify? Write them down. Compare your account to what follows.

**Stage 1: Anomaly Detection.** Abductive reasoning is initiated by the perception of something anomalous — an observation that does not fit the expected pattern. This perception is far from automatic or passive. It requires an active background expectation against which the observed datum stands out as surprising. The detective notices that the dog did not bark in the night; the clinician notices that the expected improvement did not occur; the student notices that the chemical reaction produced the wrong color precipitate. Each of these is a recognition of a discrepancy between expectation and observation — and generating that expectation requires domain knowledge.

The cognitive demands of Stage 1 are therefore primarily knowledge-dependent: you must have a sufficiently rich and accurate model of how things normally go in order to recognize when something is going differently. Research on expert-novice differences consistently shows that experts are better at noticing anomalies not because they are more observant in a general sense but because their richer mental models make more things "surprising" that novices simply fail to register.

**Stage 2: Hypothesis Generation.** Once an anomaly is registered, the abductive process generates candidate hypotheses — potential explanations that would, if true, render the observed anomaly expected or intelligible. This is the most creative stage, and the one least susceptible to algorithmic description. [[Creative Cognition|Creative cognition]] research suggests that hypothesis generation involves the activation and combination of concepts from long-term memory, analogical mapping from structurally similar cases, and what Ohlsson (1992) called "representational change" — the mental restructuring that allows a problem to be seen in a new light.

Several cognitive strategies characteristically support hypothesis generation: **analogical transfer** (treating the current anomaly as similar in structure to a previously explained anomaly); **causal schema activation** (drawing on background causal knowledge to identify the kinds of factors that could produce the observed effect); **means-ends inversion** (thinking backward from the desired explanatory state to what would need to be true for it to obtain); and **category membership questioning** (asking whether the observed item might belong to a different category than initially assumed — as when the failure of a medical treatment prompts the hypothesis that the diagnosis was wrong).

> [!example] **The Abductive Process in a Chemistry Classroom**
> A student is performing a titration experiment and expects the indicator to change from colorless to pink at the endpoint. Instead, it turns a deep orange-yellow and stays there even as more base is added. The student experiences anomaly detection: "This isn't what should happen." Hypothesis generation begins: "Maybe I used the wrong indicator. Maybe the solution is contaminated. Maybe I made an arithmetic error in calculating the expected volume. Maybe the base solution is more concentrated than labeled." Each hypothesis is a candidate explanation. The student then evaluates these candidates against what they know about the chemistry, eliminates the least plausible, and pursues the most explanatorily powerful. This is abduction in real time, in a real classroom, requiring real domain knowledge.

**Stage 3: Plausibility Assessment and Candidate Selection.** Not all generated hypotheses are equally worthy of pursuit. After generation comes evaluation — assessing which candidate hypotheses are most plausible given background knowledge, which would most completely explain the anomaly, and which are most testable given available resources. This stage imports criteria from IBE: explanatory power, simplicity, fit with established knowledge, and novelty.

The cognitive operations here are simultaneously more analytical and more metacognitive than those of Stage 2. The thinker must step back from the generative process and evaluate its products. Research on [[Scientific Reasoning|scientific reasoning]] — particularly the work of [[David Klahr|David Klahr]] and colleagues on the dual-space model of scientific discovery — suggests that children and novices often fail at this stage not because they cannot generate hypotheses but because they lack effective strategies for evaluating and selecting among them. They generate freely but evaluate poorly.

**Stage 4: Provisional Commitment and Inquiry Direction.** The output of the abductive process is not a belief but a *research directive*: a hypothesis chosen as worth investigating, held provisionally, and used to guide subsequent inquiry. Peirce was emphatic on this point — abduction licenses conjecture and investigation, not assertion. The final epistemic act is not "I conclude that H" but "H is a reasonable hypothesis; I should now look for evidence that would confirm or disconfirm it."

This stage has a motivational as well as a cognitive dimension. The willingness to commit provisionally to a hypothesis — to invest effort in pursuing it while remaining genuinely open to its refutation — requires what might be called **epistemic courage**: the willingness to be wrong, to have invested in an hypothesis that turns out to be false. This is an affective and motivational dimension of abductive competence that is often overlooked in purely cognitive accounts.

> [!thought-experiment] **The Abductive Chain in Medical Diagnosis**
> Consider a physician seeing a patient with fatigue, cold intolerance, unexplained weight gain, and dry skin. Each symptom, taken alone, is consistent with many diagnoses. But taken together as a pattern, they form a gestalt that generates the hypothesis: hypothyroidism. This is abductive reasoning — the physician is not deducing hypothyroidism from the symptoms (deduction would require already knowing the symptoms entail the diagnosis) and not inducing it from frequency statistics (though base rates are relevant to plausibility). They are inferring it as the explanation that best makes sense of the entire pattern. If a thyroid panel comes back normal, the hypothesis is revised — perhaps Addison's disease, perhaps depression, perhaps anemia. Each revision is itself a new abductive inference triggered by the anomaly of the unexpected test result.

### Metacognitive Dimensions of Abductive Reasoning

Effective abductive reasoning is not merely a first-order cognitive process but one that recruits [[Metacognition|metacognitive]] monitoring throughout. A skilled abductive reasoner is aware that they are generating hypotheses, evaluates the quality of their hypothesis pool, monitors for confirmation bias (the tendency to stop searching once a plausible hypothesis is found), and remains sensitive to evidence that might disconfirm the currently held hypothesis even when it is not being actively sought.

This metacognitive dimension explains why abductive competence is deeply connected to the metacognitive capacities documented in [[Metacognition and Critical Thinking|critical thinking research]]. [[Donald-Schön|Schön's]] concept of reflection-in-action captures something of this: the reflective practitioner is not one who applies established rules but one who engages in ongoing, real-time abductive reasoning about their own practice, generating and testing hypotheses about what is happening and what would work.

> [!key-insight] **The Confirmation Bias Problem in Abduction**
> One of the most consistent findings in reasoning research is [[Confirmation-Bias-Myside-Bias|confirmation bias]] — the tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information that confirms an existing hypothesis. In the context of abduction, this means that once a plausible hypothesis is generated, the natural cognitive tendency is to pursue confirming evidence rather than disconfirming evidence. Sophisticated abductive reasoning requires actively counteracting this tendency — pursuing what Popper called "severe tests" that could falsify the hypothesis. The metacognitive skill of recognizing when you are doing this, and correcting for it, is a core component of what distinguishes expert from novice abductive reasoning.

> [!reflection] **Understanding the Machinery**
> 1. The four-stage model of abductive inference is an idealization. In practice, these stages are often rapid, partially unconscious, and not neatly sequential. Can you recall an instance of abductive reasoning in your own life where the stages were clearly distinguishable? Where they seemed to blur together?
> 2. The claim that anomaly detection requires background knowledge has an uncomfortable implication: it means that what counts as "surprising" or "interesting" depends on what you already know. Does this mean that broader and deeper knowledge always enhances abductive reasoning? Are there cases where it might constrain it?
> 3. Provisional commitment — accepting a hypothesis while holding it revisable — sounds simple but is psychologically demanding. What makes it difficult to remain genuinely open to revising a hypothesis you have invested intellectual effort in developing?

## Phase V: Applications, Implications & Limitations

### Abductive Reasoning in Educational Practice

The most direct application of abductive reasoning theory to education is in the design of learning environments that deliberately create and support abductive moments — encounters with the anomalous, the inexplicable, and the incomplete that invite students to generate, evaluate, and provisionally commit to explanatory hypotheses. This is the intellectual core of [[Inquiry-Based-Learning|inquiry-based learning]] (IBL), though IBL pedagogies have not always been explicitly theorized in abductive terms.

> [!best-practice] **The Productive Failure Model**
> Research by [[Manu Kapur]] and colleagues on *productive failure* provides strong empirical support for a specifically abductive pedagogical design. In productive failure studies, students are given complex problems without prior instruction, allowed to generate multiple (typically incorrect or incomplete) solution methods, and only then provided with direct instruction. The abductive process of hypothesis generation during the initial exploration phase, even when it produces wrong answers, significantly enhances subsequent learning from instruction compared to direct instruction from the start. The mechanism appears to be that struggling to generate hypotheses activates relevant prior knowledge, creates awareness of the knowledge gaps the instruction will address, and produces a richer schema for organizing the new information.

> [!best-practice] **Anomaly-Driven Instruction**
> Deliberately introducing anomalous data, counterintuitive demonstrations, or cases that confound prior expectations creates the abductive occasion. Science education research on *conceptual change* (Posner et al., 1982; Chi, 2008) converges on the finding that learners who encounter demonstrably anomalous data that contradicts their existing mental models are better motivated to engage in genuine conceptual restructuring. The pedagogical challenge is calibrating the anomaly: it must be surprising enough to motivate hypothesis generation but not so opaque as to be simply discouraging.

The design of abductive learning environments requires attending to several key elements. First, problems must be structured such that multiple candidate explanations are plausible — if the "right" answer is obvious, no abductive reasoning is triggered. Second, students need sufficient background knowledge to recognize the anomaly as anomalous and to generate at least some plausible candidate hypotheses — a complete absence of prior knowledge renders the task incomprehensible rather than challenging. Third, collaborative structures are valuable: in a classroom, the diversity of hypotheses generated by different students mirrors the epistemic diversity that makes collective abductive reasoning more powerful than individual reasoning.

### Abductive Reasoning and Scientific Inquiry

[[Scientific Reasoning in Education|Science education]] has the most developed tradition of attending to abductive reasoning, though often under adjacent labels. The standards-based science education movement's emphasis on *practices* — observing, questioning, planning investigations, analyzing data, constructing explanations — implicitly relies on abductive reasoning at several points. [[Next Generation Science Standards|NGSS]] in the United States explicitly foreground "constructing explanations" as a core scientific and engineering practice, which is precisely abductive reasoning operating in the context of scientific inquiry.

Research by Klahr and Dunbar's *Scientific Discovery as Dual Search* (SDDS) model (1988) demonstrated that both expert scientists and children engage in hypothesis-first searches when exploring novel phenomena — generating hypotheses that then guide experiment design. What distinguished experts was not a different *form* of reasoning but a more reliable and effective execution of the same abductive process: better anomaly detection, richer hypothesis generation, more rigorous hypothesis evaluation. This finding supports the view that abductive reasoning is a natural cognitive capacity that can be refined through education and practice rather than an exotic skill possessed only by geniuses.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **The Expert-Novice Gap**
> The research on expert-novice differences in scientific reasoning consistently shows that experts generate *fewer* hypotheses, not more — but higher-quality ones that are more precisely aligned with the experimental data. What does this suggest about what develops when someone becomes more skilled at abductive reasoning? Is it more practice at generating hypotheses, more knowledge to constrain which hypotheses are plausible, or something else?

### Domain-Specific Applications: Medicine, Law, and Design

In **medical education**, abductive reasoning has been explicitly theorized under the label of *clinical reasoning* and *diagnostic reasoning*. Medical diagnosis is, at its core, an abductive process: the presentation of a patient's symptoms is an anomaly with respect to the expected state of health, and the task of the clinician is to generate, evaluate, and provisionally select the hypothesis (diagnosis) that best explains the symptom pattern. Research on clinical reasoning distinguishes between *pattern recognition* (experienced clinicians recognizing a syndrome at a glance — fast, System 1 abduction) and *analytic reasoning* (systematic hypothesis generation and evaluation — slow, System 2 abduction). The development of dual-process competence in clinical reasoning is an important goal of medical education.

In **legal reasoning**, the inferential process by which a jury or judge decides what happened is fundamentally abductive. Wigmore's (1913) evidence charts and more recent story model theories of jury decision-making (Pennington & Hastie, 1992) both recognize that legal fact-finders construct narrative explanations of the evidence and select among competing narratives on the basis of their coherence, completeness, and fit with the evidence. This is IBE in a legal register. Legal education that focuses exclusively on precedent application (deduction) and neglects the abductive structure of fact-finding produces lawyers who are technically proficient but may be poor factual reasoners.

In **design education** and [[Design Thinking Pedagogy|design thinking]], abduction is operationalized in the concept of "framing" — the creative act of selecting which interpretation of a design problem to pursue. Design problems, unlike mathematics problems, are not well-defined: there is no agreed specification of what the solution must satisfy, and no algorithmic procedure for finding it. The designer's task is to abductively generate a framing (a hypothesis about what the problem really is and what a good solution would look like) and to iteratively refine this framing through cycles of making and testing. This process is explicitly taught in design schools and, increasingly, in curricula that have adopted design thinking methodologies.

### Limitations and Open Questions

Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging several significant limitations in the current state of abduction research and its educational applications.

> [!important] **The Operationalization Problem**
> The most significant limitation is the difficulty of operationalizing "abductive competence" in ways that are reliably measurable and transferable across domains. Unlike working memory capacity or fluid intelligence, there is no well-validated psychometric measure of abductive reasoning ability. Most empirical studies of abductive reasoning in educational contexts measure *domain-specific* hypothesis generation — which means that findings from, say, science education may not transfer to assessments in history or design. This limits the ability to design domain-general abductive reasoning curricula with confidence.

> [!important] **The Transfer Question**
> A related limitation is the question of whether abductive reasoning skills developed in one domain transfer to other domains. General reasoning interventions (such as teaching formal logic) show limited transfer outside the training context. If abductive competence is similarly domain-bound, then the educational goal should be developing it *within* disciplinary contexts rather than as a standalone metacognitive skill. The research here is genuinely incomplete.

> [!warning] **The Scaffolding Tension**
> Inquiry-based learning that emphasizes abductive hypothesis generation can produce confusion and discouragement when students lack the background knowledge to generate any plausible hypotheses or when the abductive task is calibrated beyond their current competence. The "productive failure" literature emphasizes that failure must be productive — which requires careful design of problems, appropriate background preparation, and timely instructional support. Poorly designed open inquiry can produce not abductive reasoning practice but simple frustration.

> [!warning] **Cultural and Epistemic Context**
> Most of the philosophical and psychological research on abductive reasoning has been conducted within Western scientific and analytic traditions. The extent to which the cognitive and epistemic structures described here are universal or are themselves culturally specific is a genuine open question. Cross-cultural research on reasoning styles suggests that there are meaningful differences in how individuals from different cultural backgrounds reason about categories, causality, and inference — and abductive reasoning, which depends on background knowledge and explanatory norms, is likely to manifest differently across cultural contexts.

> [!reflection] **From Understanding to Action**
> 1. Which limitation — the operationalization problem, the transfer question, or the scaffolding tension — seems most important to address if you were designing an abductive reasoning curriculum? Why?
> 2. The cultural limitation raises a genuinely difficult question: if explanatory norms vary across cultures, does "inference to the best explanation" have a culturally specific content? What would a culturally sensitive approach to teaching abductive reasoning look like?
> 3. Think of a specific learning context you know well (a course you teach or take, a professional training program, an informal learning environment). Where do students have opportunities to engage in abductive reasoning? Where are those opportunities missing?

## Phase VI: Synthesis & Integration

### Drawing the Threads Together

The conceptual journey across the preceding phases reveals abductive reasoning as a phenomenon with remarkable depth and range. At the philosophical level, it is the form of inference that distinguishes the generative from the demonstrative movements of thought — the logical structure through which novelty enters human knowledge. At the psychological level, it is a complex, multi-stage cognitive process that recruits domain knowledge, analogical mapping, metacognitive monitoring, and epistemic courage. At the educational level, it is both a target of development and a mechanism through which much genuine learning actually occurs.

The deepest synthetic insight is the relationship between abduction and [[Metacognition|metacognition]], which connects this report to adjacent nodes in the knowledge base. Effective abductive reasoning is not merely a cognitive act but a metacognitively guided one — the abductive reasoner must simultaneously generate hypotheses, monitor their own generation process for bias and incompleteness, evaluate candidates against criteria that are themselves subject to scrutiny, and maintain a disposition of provisional commitment that neither collapses into dogmatic assertion nor dissolves into paralytic uncertainty. This is precisely the profile of the [[Self-Regulated Learning|self-regulated learner]]: monitoring, evaluating, adjusting.

The relationship to [[Critical-Thinking|critical thinking]] is equally generative. [[Paul-Elder Critical Thinking Framework|Paul and Elder's]] critical thinking framework emphasizes the importance of disciplined hypothesis generation, evaluating evidence, and recognizing the difference between what is known and what is inferred. These are not just generic intellectual virtues but specifically abductive capacities. The critical thinker who asks "what assumptions am I making?" is, among other things, auditing the background knowledge that shapes which anomalies are registered and which hypotheses are generated.

The relationship to [[Transfer-of-Learning|transfer of learning]] is perhaps the most practically urgent. If the goal of education is for students to be able to use their knowledge in new situations — to transfer — then abductive competence is directly relevant. Transfer typically requires the learner to recognize a new problem as relevantly similar to previously encountered problems, generate a hypothesis about which prior knowledge applies, and test that hypothesis against the new context. This is an abductive process. Instruction that develops abductive competence through practice with genuine anomalies and genuine hypothesis generation is, indirectly, instruction that develops transfer.

### The "So What?"

What capability does understanding abductive reasoning give the educator, the learner, and the curriculum designer that they did not have before?

For the **educator**, it provides a principled account of what is cognitively and epistemically happening when students engage in inquiry, problem-solving, and genuine creative thinking — and it suggests which specific competencies (anomaly sensitivity, hypothesis generation, plausibility evaluation, provisional commitment) need development and how. It also explains why certain pedagogical moves work: why productive failure produces deeper learning, why anomalous demonstrations promote conceptual change, why collaborative hypothesis generation outperforms individual reasoning.

For the **learner**, it provides a vocabulary and a map for understanding their own reasoning process. The student who knows that they are engaged in abductive reasoning — that they are generating a hypothesis, not asserting a conclusion — is better positioned to hold that hypothesis with appropriate epistemic humility, to recognize when they are collecting confirming evidence and should instead be seeking disconfirmation, and to appreciate the difference between a compelling hypothesis and an established fact.

For the **curriculum designer**, it reframes the design question. Instead of asking "how do we transmit this content?", the abductive lens asks "what anomalies, surprises, and incompletely explained phenomena can we put students in contact with, and what scaffolding will help them develop and evaluate hypotheses?" This is a different design orientation, and it produces different curricular choices.

Does the availability of tools that generate hypotheses automatically develop or atrophy the capacity for human abductive reasoning?

## Phase VII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Abduction (Peirce, 1903)**
> The logical form of inference that generates a hypothesis to explain a surprising observation. The abductive syllogism: if *H* were true, *C* would be expected; *C* is observed and surprising; therefore *H* is a conjecture worth investigating. Abduction is non-deductive (the conclusion does not follow necessarily), non-inductive (it does not generalize from observed frequencies), and generative (it produces genuinely new hypotheses). Synonymous in some contexts with *retroduction* (Peirce's earlier term) and with *inference to the best explanation* (the analytic philosophy reconstruction). NOT synonymous with guessing or arbitrary conjecture — abduction requires that the hypothesis be genuinely explanatory.

> [!definition] **Inference to the Best Explanation (IBE) (Harman, 1965; Lipton, 2004)**
> The analytic philosophy reconstruction of Peircean abduction, understood as the inferential practice of selecting the hypothesis that, if true, would best explain the observed data according to criteria including explanatory power, fit with background knowledge, simplicity, and what Lipton calls "loveliness" (depth of illumination). IBE is simultaneously a description of how reasoners actually infer and a normative standard for how they should infer. Subject to the *bad lot problem* (van Fraassen): even the best available hypothesis may not be good in any absolute sense if the pool of candidates considered is poor.

> [!definition] **Anomaly (in the abductive context)**
> An observation or datum that does not fit the expectations generated by an agent's current knowledge or background theory. Anomalies are the triggers of abductive reasoning — they create the occasion for hypothesis generation. Importantly, what counts as anomalous is theory-dependent (Hanson's *theory-ladenness of observation*): the same datum that is anomalous for one thinker may be entirely expected for another with different background knowledge. Developing sensitivity to anomaly — a richer sense of what should be surprising — is a core component of abductive competence development.

> [!definition] **Abductive Competence**
> The cluster of cognitive and epistemic capacities that support effective abductive reasoning. Components include: (1) anomaly sensitivity — the capacity to detect when an observation deviates from expectation; (2) hypothesis generation — the capacity to generate multiple candidate explanations; (3) plausibility evaluation — the capacity to assess which hypotheses are more or less consistent with background knowledge and explanatory criteria; (4) provisional commitment — the epistemic disposition to hold the best available hypothesis as a guide to inquiry while remaining open to revision. Abductive competence is domain-sensitive (stronger background knowledge supports better abduction in that domain) and developable through deliberate instruction and practice.

> [!definition] **Manipulative Abduction (Magnani, 2001)**
> A form of abductive inference that occurs through and in the course of physical manipulation of the environment — experimentation, prototyping, tool use, diagrammatic reasoning. Distinct from *sentential abduction* (hypothesis formation through linguistic or logical deliberation) and *model-based abduction* (hypothesis formation through mental model manipulation). Manipulative abduction grounds the epistemological value of hands-on learning: physical interaction with materials can generate hypotheses that purely mental deliberation would not produce, because the material world offers unexpected resistances and affordances that the mental model does not.

> [!definition] **Theory-Ladenness of Observation (Hanson, 1958)**
> The philosophical thesis that observations are not theory-neutral sense-data but are already organized and interpreted by the theoretical frameworks of the observer. "Seeing" in a scientific context is always "seeing as" — as a metal, as a bacterium, as evidence for a hypothesis. The theory-ladenness thesis has direct consequences for abduction: what counts as an anomaly, and therefore what triggers abductive inference, is determined by the observer's background theory. This makes the development of richer and more accurate background knowledge a prerequisite for, not merely complementary to, the development of abductive reasoning skill.

> [!definition] **Productive Failure (Kapur, 2010, 2016)**
> A pedagogical design principle and empirical finding holding that student engagement with complex problems that they are not yet equipped to solve correctly can produce superior subsequent learning outcomes compared to direct instruction, when followed by instructor-provided explanation. The mechanism is partly abductive: the prior engagement with the problem activates prior knowledge, surfaces misconceptions, creates awareness of knowledge gaps, and generates a schema of failed attempts that structures the subsequent encoding of correct information. Productive failure is one of the best-empirically-supported specific implementations of abduction-centered pedagogy.

> [!definition] **Context of Discovery vs. Context of Justification (Reichenbach, 1938)**
> A foundational distinction in philosophy of science between the psychological and heuristic process through which hypotheses are generated (context of discovery) and the logical process through which hypotheses are tested, evaluated, and accepted or rejected (context of justification). Abduction operates primarily in the context of discovery, though IBE attempts to extend its normative role into the context of justification as well. The distinction has been criticized for being too clean — in practice, discovery and justification interact — but retains pedagogical utility for distinguishing the skill of hypothesis generation from the skill of hypothesis testing.

> [!definition] **Inquiry-Based Learning (IBL)**
> A family of pedagogical approaches in which students are the primary agents of inquiry: they formulate or are presented with questions, generate hypotheses, design or conduct investigations, and draw conclusions from evidence. IBL is grounded philosophically in Dewey's *How We Think* (1910) and developed empirically through decades of science education research. In its most abductive instantiation, IBL deliberately creates anomalies and withholds pre-formed explanations to ensure that students engage in genuine hypothesis generation rather than simply verifying known outcomes.

> The work that most influentially connected abductive reasoning to design thinking and business innovation. Martin's concept of "the knowledge funnel" and his argument that abductive thinking is systematically undervalued in algorithm-oriented organizations provides a powerful argument for abduction's relevance beyond academic learning contexts.

> [!cite] **Hmelo-Silver, C. E., Duncan, R. G., & Chinn, C. A. (2007). Scaffolding and achievement in problem-based and inquiry learning: A response to Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006). *Educational Psychologist, 42*(2), 99–107.**
> The key empirical response to critiques of inquiry-based learning, demonstrating that appropriately scaffolded IBL produces strong learning outcomes. Essential for any educator who must defend inquiry-oriented, abductively-structured pedagogy against efficiency-focused critiques. The paper's account of scaffolding provides practical guidance on supporting abductive reasoning without eliminating the productive struggle.

> [!cite] **van Fraassen, B. C. (1989). *Laws and Symmetry*. Oxford University Press.**
> Contains the most influential philosophical critique of IBE — the "bad lot problem." Understanding this objection is essential for a philosophically sophisticated engagement with abductive reasoning; it prevents naive overconfidence in abductive conclusions and supports the development of appropriate epistemic humility about hypotheses, even compelling ones.

### C. Methodology and Sources Note

> [!methodology-and-sources] **Research Grounding for This Report**
> This report draws on three intersecting intellectual traditions: (1) the philosophical tradition originating with Peirce and extending through analytic philosophy of science (Hanson, Harman, Lipton, van Fraassen), which provides the conceptual and normative framework for understanding what abductive inference is and when it is well-performed; (2) the cognitive science tradition (Klahr, Dunbar, Magnani), which provides empirical and theoretical accounts of how abductive reasoning actually operates in human minds and in extended cognitive systems; and (3) the educational research tradition (Dewey, Kapur, Hmelo-Silver), which provides empirical evidence on how abductive reasoning develops and how it can be fostered through instructional design. The philosophical claims in this report (the triadic structure of inference, the character of IBE) are established within their respective traditions. The cognitive science claims (the four-stage process, expert-novice differences) represent well-replicated empirical findings. The educational claims (productive failure, anomaly-driven instruction) represent research-supported best practices, though the evidence base is stronger in some domains (science education) than others. The claim that abductive reasoning is constitutive of transfer is a theoretical synthesis that goes beyond any individual source and represents this report's own integrative contribution. The limitations section identifies where the evidence is genuinely weak or contested.

### D. Expansion Topics

> [!further-exploration] **Deepening Your Practice**

> > [!topic-idea] [[Inference to the Best Explanation — Philosophical Foundations]]
> > The philosophical literature on IBE is extensive and technically demanding but directly relevant to anyone who wants to think carefully about what makes a hypothesis "best." A dedicated report would cover Harman's original formulation, Lipton's loveliness-vs.-likeliness distinction, van Fraassen's bad lot problem and epistemic voluntarism response, Bayesian reconstructions of IBE (particularly the debate about whether IBE can be absorbed into a Bayesian framework or whether it requires independent normative status), and the question of whether IBE is a description of actual inference or a prescription for ideal inference. Priority: high — this is the most technically developed adjacent topic and would provide the strongest philosophical underpinning for the claims in this report.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Inquiry-Based Learning — Empirical Evidence and Design Principles]]
> > While this report provides the theoretical foundation for inquiry-based learning from an abductive perspective, a dedicated report on IBL would systematically review the empirical evidence, examine the debate between minimally guided and heavily scaffolded inquiry, explore the productive failure literature in depth, and provide concrete design principles for structuring abductive learning environments across domains. The report would engage the Kirschner-Sweller-Clark (2006) critique of minimal guidance and the extensive responses to that critique. Priority: high — this is the most direct translation of abduction theory into educational practice.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Abductive Reasoning in Medical Education — Clinical Reasoning and Diagnosis]]
> > Medical education has the most developed domain-specific literature on abductive reasoning, under the labels of clinical reasoning, diagnostic reasoning, and differential diagnosis. A dedicated report would cover dual-process theory in clinical cognition (System 1 pattern recognition vs. System 2 analytical reasoning), the literature on diagnostic error as abductive failure (premature closure, confirmation bias, anchoring), the design of clinical reasoning curricula, and the use of case-based learning as an abductive pedagogy. Medical education's emphasis on evidence-based reasoning provides the most disciplined domain-specific instantiation of the concepts developed in this report. Priority: high.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Design Thinking as Abductive Pedagogy — Theory and Practice]]
> > Roger Martin's framework connects abduction to professional education in design, business, and engineering. A dedicated report would examine the history of design thinking pedagogy from the Ulm School through IDEO's human-centered design approach, the Stanford d.school model, and cross-disciplinary implementations. It would critically evaluate the evidence base for design thinking outcomes, address the critiques of design thinking as pedagogical fashion, and provide a theoretically grounded account of how the specific design thinking moves (empathizing, defining, ideating, prototyping, testing) instantiate the stages of abductive reasoning. Priority: medium.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Epistemic Calibration and Uncertainty Tolerance in Learners]]
> > Abductive reasoning requires holding hypotheses provisionally — a disposition that is psychologically demanding and that varies considerably among individuals and cultures. A dedicated report would examine what is known about epistemic calibration (the correspondence between confidence and accuracy), the development of uncertainty tolerance as a learnable disposition, the relationship between uncertainty tolerance and intellectual humility, and instructional strategies for developing these capacities. This report would provide the affective and motivational complement to the cognitive account of abductive reasoning. Priority: medium.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Scientific Discovery — Cognitive Models and Historical Case Studies]]
> > A report focused specifically on abduction in the history and philosophy of science would examine celebrated cases of scientific discovery (Kepler's planetary laws, Fleming's penicillin, Watson and Crick's double helix) as case studies in abductive reasoning, examine computational models of scientific discovery (Langley et al.'s BACON program, Simon's heuristics), and engage with the Kuhnian account of paradigm shifts as large-scale abductive restructuring. This report would provide the richest exemplification of abductive reasoning at the level of collective, historical inquiry. Priority: medium.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Analogical Reasoning and Abduction — Cognitive Intersections]]
> > The relationship between analogical and abductive inference is philosophically and cognitively subtle. Both involve moving from the known to the unknown; both draw on structural mapping; both can generate novel hypotheses. A dedicated report would examine the cognitive overlap and differences, drawing on Gentner's structure-mapping theory of analogy, the role of analogical reasoning in scientific discovery (Black's interaction theory of metaphor, Hesse's models and analogies in science), and the instructional implications of treating analogical reasoning as an abductive resource. Priority: low — this is the most technically specialized adjacent topic and presupposes familiarity with the cognitive science of analogy.
