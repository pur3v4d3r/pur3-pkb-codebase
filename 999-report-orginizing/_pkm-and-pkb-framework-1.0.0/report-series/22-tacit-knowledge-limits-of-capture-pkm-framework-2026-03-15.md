# Report 22: Tacit Knowledge and the Limits of Capture — What a PKB Cannot Store

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Synthesis Focus

There is something deeply strange about the knowledge of a chess grandmaster. Garry Kasparov, playing at his peak, could examine a complex middlegame position for a few seconds and announce that White is winning — without being able to say *why* with any precision that would actually help a weaker player replicate the judgment. The knowledge is real, demonstrably correct, and practically powerful. Yet it resists the standard instruments of knowledge capture: you cannot write it down, you cannot store it in a note, and you cannot transfer it to another person simply by documenting it. When Kasparov does articulate his reasoning, the articulation is always downstream from the insight — a post-hoc rationalization of a judgment that arrived whole, pre-verbally, from somewhere he cannot fully point to.

This is not a peculiarity of chess. The jazz musician who knows when to solo, the experienced nurse who recognizes a patient about to deteriorate before any vital sign confirms it, the master craftsperson who knows from the texture of wood grain how it will split, the veteran teacher who reads a classroom's energy and adjusts — all of these represent forms of knowledge that are real, earned through tremendous effort, and functionally decisive. And none of them can be fully captured in a note.

This report confronts a question that sits at the foundation of the entire PKM/PKB enterprise: *What is the proper relationship between a Personal Knowledge Base and the vast dimension of human knowing that fundamentally resists textual capture?* Getting this question right is not an edge case or a philosophical nicety — it is arguably *the* most important design question for a lifelong learner building a PKB. A learner who doesn't understand the limits of capture risks two equally damaging errors: either they fail to use their PKB at all (believing, correctly, that the most important things cannot be stored), or they over-invest in their PKB, believing that comprehensive note-taking is equivalent to comprehensive knowing — and missing the entire tacit dimension that separates novice performance from expert performance.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Before You Begin: A Diagnostic**
> Before engaging with the frameworks below, take a moment to probe your own relationship to tacit knowledge in your primary area of expertise. Think of something you do well — a skill, a judgment, a practice — that you would struggle to fully explain to a beginner. What *is* that knowing? Where does it live? When you try to articulate it, what happens to the articulation? Noting your intuitions here provides a personal anchor for the abstract frameworks ahead.

The synthesis question this report addresses is: *How do Michael Polanyi's philosophy of tacit knowledge, Hubert Dreyfus's phenomenological account of skill acquisition, Ikujiro Nonaka's SECI model of organizational knowledge creation, and the cognitive science of embodied and situated cognition combine to reveal what a text-based PKB cannot store — and to suggest what complementary practices should accompany it?*

This is not a comforting question for PKB enthusiasts. The answer involves acknowledging real, irreducible limits. But the answer also generates a more sophisticated and ultimately more powerful conception of what a PKB is *for* — one that positions the PKB not as a replacement for tacit knowing but as a carefully designed complement to it. The disciplines converging on this question are [[Knowledge Management]] (Nonaka, Polanyi), [[Philosophy of Mind]] and [[Phenomenology]] (Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus), [[Cognitive Science]] (procedural memory, embodied and situated cognition), and [[Educational Philosophy]] (craft learning, apprenticeship). Their intersection yields something that no single discipline articulates: a theory of the PKB as a **Tacit Knowledge Observatory** rather than a tacit knowledge repository.

Each phase builds on the last. Phase II establishes the key frameworks. Phase III examines the evidence for tacit knowledge's resistance to capture — and for what partial capture is possible. Phase IV analyzes the mechanisms that make tacit knowledge tacit, which is where the deepest synthesis occurs. Phase V translates these findings into PKB design. Phase VI produces the report's original synthesis contribution.

## Phase II: Analytical Framework — Cross-Domain Foundations

### The Tacit Dimension: Polanyi's Foundational Contribution

The starting point is Michael Polanyi's deceptively simple claim, first articulated in his 1958 masterwork *Personal Knowledge* and compressed to an aphorism in *The Tacit Dimension* (1966): **"We know more than we can tell."** This is not a claim about information we have forgotten or concepts we haven't yet verbalized. It is a structural claim about the architecture of knowing itself — that knowledge has a dimension that is constitutively resistant to explicit articulation, not merely contingently un-articulated.

> [!definition] **Tacit Knowledge (Polanyi, 1966 — Philosophy of Mind / Epistemology)**
> Knowledge that is functionally operative in skilled performance but cannot be fully articulated in explicit propositional form. Tacit knowledge is not simply knowledge that has not yet been written down; it is knowledge whose very functioning depends on its remaining pre-reflective. Distinguished from *explicit knowledge* (propositional, codifiable, transmissible by symbolic means). Key subtypes include tacit *knowing-how* (procedural) and tacit *judgment* (evaluative pattern recognition). Boundary condition: some tacit knowledge admits partial externalization (Nonaka); the deepest level of expert intuition does not.

Polanyi's technical machinery for explaining *why* tacit knowledge resists capture centers on his distinction between **focal** and **subsidiary** awareness:

> [!definition] **Focal / Subsidiary Attention Distinction (Polanyi, 1966 — Philosophy of Mind)**
> In any act of skilled perception or performance, attention is structured asymmetrically. *Focal awareness* is directed at the explicit object of concern (the nail being hammered, the patient's symptoms, the chess position). *Subsidiary awareness* operates in the background — attending to the tool, the bodily feelings, the pattern of previous positions — and these subsidiary elements are precisely what constitute tacit knowledge. The critical insight is that when subsidiary awareness is made focal (when you try to pay explicit attention to how your hand holds the hammer), the skill degrades or collapses. The tacit dimension functions through subsidiarity; articulation destroys its operative role.

This mechanism — that making tacit elements focal disrupts their function — is not merely philosophical speculation. It has well-documented empirical parallels. Research on *choking under pressure* (Beilock & Carr, 2001) demonstrates that expert athletes who are prompted to consciously attend to automated motor sequences perform significantly worse than those who are not. Verbal overshadowing (Schooler, 2002) demonstrates that asking people to verbally describe a face they have just seen impairs their subsequent ability to recognize it. The act of articulation does not merely fail to capture tacit knowledge — it can actively interfere with it.

> [!definition] **Explicit Knowledge (Polanyi, 1966 / Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995 — Knowledge Management)**
> Knowledge that can be articulated in formal language, documented symbolically, and transmitted systematically through propositional means. Explicit knowledge is the natural currency of a PKB: definitions, arguments, procedures, frameworks, facts, models. Distinguished from tacit knowledge by its independence from any particular knower's embodied experience. Boundary condition: what appears to be fully explicit knowledge is often underpinned by tacit knowledge required to interpret and apply it — the explicit rules of chess are not sufficient to play chess well.

The relationship between tacit and explicit knowledge is not binary but spectral. Some knowledge is largely explicit (the rules of chess), some is largely tacit (how it feels to play well), and much occupies intermediate territory where partial articulation is possible and useful while full articulation remains out of reach. This spectral model, rather than a sharp dichotomy, is essential for designing a PKB around these realities.

### Dreyfus's Skill Acquisition Model

Hubert Dreyfus, drawing on Heideggerian phenomenology and Merleau-Ponty's philosophy of embodiment, developed a five-stage model of skill acquisition that maps the trajectory from rule-following novice to intuitive expert — and reveals how this trajectory is also a trajectory *away from* explicit, capturable knowledge and *toward* tacit, embodied knowing.

> [!definition] **Dreyfus Skill Acquisition Model (Dreyfus & Dreyfus, 1980/1986 — Educational Philosophy / Phenomenology)**
> A five-stage model describing how skillful performance develops from deliberate rule-following to embodied intuition:
> 1. **Novice**: Follows explicit, context-free rules. Performance is effortful, halting, and dependent on verbal instruction.
> 2. **Advanced Beginner**: Recognizes situational features beyond formal rules but still treats elements analytically.
> 3. **Competent**: Selects among a large set of recognized situations, plans deliberately, feels responsibility for outcomes.
> 4. **Proficient**: Perceives situations holistically without decomposing them. Plans still deliberate but perception is intuitive.
> 5. **Expert**: No longer relies on rules, guidelines, or maxims. Perceives *what to do* directly through embodied understanding, without analytical decomposition. Performance is fluid, situationally responsive, and largely pre-reflective.
>
> Critical implication: expertise is not *more* explicit knowledge — it is a *transformation* of explicit knowledge into embodied, non-propositional knowing. Trying to make an expert function like a competent rule-follower degrades performance.

The Dreyfus model has a direct and somewhat alarming implication for PKBs: the more expert a person becomes at something, the *less* their performance is governed by the kind of explicit, rule-based knowledge that a PKB can store. A novice learning to write benefits enormously from explicit notes about grammar, sentence structure, and rhetorical strategy. A master writer's judgment about when a paragraph is right cannot be expressed in rules — it is felt, directly and immediately. If the PKB user's goal is genuine expertise, they should expect the PKB's contribution to their core performance to *decrease* as expertise increases, not because the PKB becomes less useful, but because its locus of usefulness shifts.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Dreyfus's Expert Stage and CLT's Automaticity**
> Dreyfus's description of the Expert stage — where rules give way to immediate, non-analytical recognition and response — is structurally isomorphic with Cognitive Load Theory's account of *automaticity* (established in Report 02). In CLT terms, as schemas become highly refined through extensive practice, their activation requires minimal working memory resources; the process becomes automatic. In Dreyfus's terms, the same trajectory is described phenomenologically: what was once effortful rule-following is now immediate intuitive grasp. These are the same developmental trajectory viewed from two different vantage points — cognitive architecture (CLT) and lived experience (phenomenology). The convergence matters for PKB design: both frameworks imply that the knowledge that *most drives* expert performance is the knowledge *least accessible to conscious report* — and therefore least available for PKB capture. This is not a failure of introspection but a feature of how expertise works.

### Nonaka's SECI Model

Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi, in their landmark 1995 work *The Knowledge-Creating Company*, developed the **SECI model** — a framework for understanding how organizations create knowledge through a dynamic spiral converting between tacit and explicit forms.

> [!definition] **SECI Model (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995 — Knowledge Management / Organizational Learning)**
> A four-process spiral model of knowledge creation through conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge:
> - **Socialization** (Tacit → Tacit): Direct transmission of tacit knowledge through shared experience, observation, and imitation. Apprenticeship is the paradigm case. No explicit articulation required.
> - **Externalization** (Tacit → Explicit): Converting tacit knowledge to explicit form through metaphor, analogy, hypotheses, and models. Partial and imperfect but enables broader sharing. This is where tacit knowledge becomes PKB-storable.
> - **Combination** (Explicit → Explicit): Systematizing explicit knowledge through documents, databases, and communication. Standard information management.
> - **Internalization** (Explicit → Tacit): Converting explicit knowledge back into tacit form through practice, embodied learning, and experiential application. "Learning by doing."
>
> Critical insight: no single conversion is sufficient. Knowledge creation requires the *spiral* — cycling through all four quadrants repeatedly. PKBs operate primarily in the Combination quadrant and partially support Externalization. They cannot directly instantiate Socialization or Internalization.

### Embodied and Situated Cognition

The fourth disciplinary contribution comes from cognitive science's [[Embodied Cognition]] tradition, associated primarily with Maurice Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology and its contemporary cognitive science developments.

> [!definition] **Embodied Cognition (Merleau-Ponty, 1945; Varela, Thompson & Rosch, 1991 — Cognitive Science / Philosophy of Mind)**
> The thesis that cognition is not merely *about* the body but *constituted by* the body and its sensorimotor engagement with the environment. Knowledge is not stored in a disembodied symbol system and applied through the body; it is enacted through patterns of bodily engagement that cannot be fully abstracted from those engagements. Expert knowledge in physical domains (crafts, sports, surgery, music) is particularly embodied — it consists substantially in refined sensorimotor contingencies that are inaccessible to propositional capture. Boundary condition: not all cognition is equally embodied; abstract reasoning may be more separable from specific bodily instantiation.

> [!definition] **Situated Cognition (Brown, Collins & Duguid, 1989; Lave & Wenger, 1991 — Cognitive Science / Educational Philosophy)**
> The thesis that knowledge is not a portable, decontextualized commodity but is always constituted in and by the activity, context, and culture in which it was developed and in which it is applied. Knowledge separated from its generating context loses properties essential to its practical function. Situated cognition theory predicts that much of what works as knowledge in practice will not transfer straightforwardly when abstracted into notes and stored in a different context. The PKB note is, by construction, decontextualized — which is part of why Report 11's Transfer Problem is so persistent.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Conceptual Checkpoint: The Spectrum**
> Before proceeding: can you place the following kinds of knowing on the tacit-explicit spectrum? (1) The definition of *photosynthesis*; (2) knowing how to ride a bicycle; (3) recognizing a good argument; (4) knowing how to write a clear sentence; (5) knowing when a piece of music is building toward a climax. What does your placement reveal about which aspects of your PKM practice are most at risk of confusing the map for the territory?

> [!reflection] **Integrating the Frameworks**
>
> **Comprehension**: Polanyi, Dreyfus, Nonaka, and the embodied cognition theorists are all circling a related insight, but from different angles. Can you articulate the shared core?
>
> **Application**: Looking at your PKB right now — what percentage of the notes you've made represent genuinely explicit knowledge versus partial externalizations of tacit knowledge? Does that ratio feel right?
>
> **Extension**: What is the tension between Nonaka's optimism (tacit knowledge can be converted through externalization) and Polanyi's and Dreyfus's less optimistic accounts of the deepest tacit knowledge? Why does this tension matter?

## Phase III: Critical Examination of Evidence

### What Cannot Be Captured — The Evidence Base

The claim that significant knowledge resists explicit capture is not merely philosophical — it has accumulated a substantial empirical base across several disciplines.

The most compelling evidence comes from **expert-novice research**. Pioneered by chess researchers de Groot (1946) and Chase and Simon (1973), this tradition established that what separates chess masters from novices is not superior analytical processing but vastly richer pattern recognition — the ability to perceive meaningful chunks of board position that novices see only as scattered pieces. Crucially, when grandmasters are asked to explain their evaluations, the articulations they produce are consistently insufficient to enable a novice to replicate the judgment. The articulation is accurate as far as it goes; it simply does not go far enough to capture what is actually functioning in the expert's perception. Similar findings have been replicated in medical diagnosis (Elstein et al., 1978; Norman et al., 1992), firefighting (Klein, 1998), and nursing clinical judgment (Benner, 1984).

Gary Klein's **Naturalistic Decision Making** (NDM) research is particularly relevant to PKB design. Klein studied expert decision-makers — fireground commanders, intensive care nurses, military commanders — in real-world conditions and found that they overwhelmingly do not deliberate between options. They recognize situations as instances of familiar patterns and immediately generate a plausible course of action. They do *not* reason from explicit rules or principles stored in external systems. The knowledge that drives their life-or-death decisions is embodied, situationally responsive, and largely pre-reflective.

> [!evidence] **The NDM Research Program (Klein, 1998)**
> Gary Klein's study of expert decision-making in naturalistic environments, documented in *Sources of Power* (1998), found that experienced practitioners in high-stakes domains rarely engage in formal analysis or option comparison. Instead, they recognize situations as familiar and immediately generate workable responses. The decision-making process is fast, intuitive, and pattern-driven. Experts report their reasoning retrospectively, but the retrospective account consistently underspecifies what actually drove the decision. This has been replicated across domains including firefighting, intensive care nursing, and military command. For PKB design: the kind of decision-making expertise that matters most in high-stakes domains is the *least* accessible to the kind of explicit documentation that a PKB can store.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **The Articulation Problem Is Not a Language Problem**
> A persistent misconception about tacit knowledge is that the problem is linguistic — that with better vocabulary or richer description, we could eventually capture what experts know. The evidence suggests this is wrong. Verbal overshadowing research (Schooler & Engstler-Schooler, 1990; Schooler, 2002) demonstrates that the very act of verbal articulation can impair recognition performance in areas where knowledge is primarily perceptual and procedural. This is not a failure of description but a structural feature: verbal encoding and perceptual/procedural encoding use different cognitive systems, and activating verbal encoding can interfere with perceptual access. If articulation could capture tacit knowledge without loss, verbal overshadowing would not exist. The tacit dimension is not merely pre-verbal; it is in some respects anti-verbal — it functions *by* remaining pre-reflective.

### What the SECI Model Suggests Can Be Captured

Nonaka's research in organizational knowledge creation provides a more optimistic counterpoint. Studying highly innovative Japanese firms, Nonaka and Takeuchi documented successful externalization — the conversion of tacit knowledge to explicit form — as a repeated phenomenon. The mechanisms they identified include: **metaphor and analogy** (where the tacit is approximated through comparison with something better understood), **dialog and reflection** (where the act of being asked to explain forces partial articulation), **prototyping and modeling** (where embodied knowledge is externalized into physical or diagrammatic form), and **narrative** (where the sequence of skilled action is captured in story form).

> [!evidence] **Nonaka and Takeuchi's Externalization Studies (1995)**
> Nonaka and Takeuchi's research in innovative Japanese firms (notably their case study of Honda's City car development) documented how tacit knowledge was successfully converted to explicit form through structured processes including brainstorming, dialogue, metaphor development, and prototype iteration. The "Tall Boy" concept for the Honda City emerged from the tacit aesthetic judgment of designers being progressively articulated through dialogue and prototype. Key finding: externalization is always *partial* — the explicit representation approximates but never fully captures the tacit original. The value of externalization is not complete capture but *sufficient capture for combination and communication*.

The crucial qualification from Nonaka's own account is that externalization always produces approximations, never exact transcriptions. The explicit model is useful precisely because it *approximates* the tacit original well enough for organizational purposes — combination, communication, refinement. But the tacit original cannot be fully reconstructed from the explicit approximation. Someone who reads Nonaka's account of Honda's design process has learned something real about how to externalize tacit design judgment — but they have not thereby acquired that judgment itself. Internalization (the fourth SECI quadrant) is still required.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **A Taxonomy of Tacit Knowledge Convertibility**
> Synthesizing the evidence across Polanyi, Dreyfus, and Nonaka suggests a rough taxonomy of tacit knowledge by convertibility. At the high-convertibility end sit: procedural knowledge with clear decision points (partially capturable as decision trees or flowcharts), pattern recognition judgments that can be broken into component features (partially capturable as checklists or rubrics), and craft knowledge with identifiable sub-skills (partially capturable through worked examples and annotated samples). At the low-convertibility end sit: holistic perceptual judgments (the master writer's sense of rightness in a sentence), fast intuitive recognition in complex dynamic situations (Klein's fireground commanders), and sensorimotor expertise requiring years of embodied practice (surgical technique, musical performance). PKB design should distinguish these zones, investing in externalization where convertibility is moderate and complementary practice where it is low.

### The Embodied Evidence

Cognitive science's [[Procedural Memory]] research provides a neurological angle on tacit knowledge. Procedural memory — the memory system responsible for skills and habits — operates through different neural architecture than declarative (explicitly accessible) memory. Long-term potentiation in motor and perceptual cortices underlies skilled performance; hippocampal encoding underlies propositional knowledge. The two systems interact but are genuinely dissociable: amnesic patients who cannot form new declarative memories can still acquire procedural skills. Conversely, explicit verbal instruction does not directly drive procedural learning — the body must practice (Willingham, 1998; Squire & Zola, 1996).

> [!tension-identified] **The Verbalization Paradox in Skill Instruction**
> A productive tension runs through the evidence on tacit knowledge and instruction. On one hand, research on deliberate practice (Ericsson et al., 1993) emphasizes the importance of immediate, specific feedback — which requires some explicit articulation of what went wrong and why. On the other hand, Dreyfus and the embodied cognition tradition emphasize that overloading learners with explicit rule-following degrades performance and slows the transition to intuitive expertise. Both are well-supported. The tension resolves conditionally: explicit instruction is most valuable at the novice and advanced beginner stages, where schemas are being formed and rules provide scaffolding. As competence develops into proficiency and expertise, explicit instruction should *fade* (as Report 10 established), and embodied practice should dominate. PKBs that continue to emphasize rule-collection and explicit frameworks past the competent stage may actively impede the development of expert intuition.

### The Situated Cognition Evidence

The situated cognition literature, particularly Lave and Wenger's (1991) studies of apprenticeship learning and Brown, Collins, and Duguid's (1989) analysis of how knowledge functions differently in authentic practice versus classroom abstraction, provides further evidence for the limits of decontextualized, explicit knowledge storage.

Lave and Wenger's concept of **legitimate peripheral participation** describes how novices in communities of practice learn primarily through situated engagement — working alongside experts, participating in authentic tasks at the periphery, gradually taking on greater responsibility. The knowledge being transmitted in this process is largely tacit: the norms, judgments, and habits of the practice community are absorbed through participation rather than instruction. A PKB can capture what a master says about their craft; it cannot replicate the months of peripheral participation through which a novice actually acquires the craft's tacit norms.

> [!reflection] **Integrating the Evidence**
>
> **Comprehension**: Three research traditions — NDM, SECI externalization studies, and situated cognition — converge on a shared finding with different emphasis. What is the shared finding? What does each tradition add?
>
> **Application**: If you were redesigning one aspect of your PKB based solely on this evidence, what would you change? What practice *outside* your PKB might become more important?
>
> **Extension**: Notice where the evidence makes you uncomfortable. Resistance to this evidence often signals a deep assumption about the nature of knowledge — one worth surfacing and examining.

## Phase IV: Mechanisms, Dynamics & Deep Synthesis

> [!important] **Complexity Transition**
> The analysis ahead moves from *what* tacit knowledge is and *that* it resists capture, to *why* — examining the precise mechanisms that make explicit capture insufficient. It then builds toward the report's most ambitious synthesis: a reconception of the PKB's proper role in relation to tacit knowing. This section directly integrates findings from Reports 08, 10, 11, and 17 in ways that were not fully possible before the tacit knowledge framework was in view.

### The Focal/Subsidiary Mechanism in Detail

Polanyi's focal/subsidiary distinction, introduced in Phase II, is not merely a descriptive taxonomy — it is a functional account of *how* tacit knowledge works. Understanding the mechanism illuminates why capture fails where it fails.

In any skilled performance, what the performer is focally attending to is *the objective* — the nail, the patient, the chess position, the musical phrase. The tools, the body, the previous experience, the cultural norms — these are subsidiarily present. They inform the focal performance *from* a background that the performer is not explicitly attending to. Critically, this subsidiary background is not unconscious in any deep neurological sense; it is simply structured differently. It is present as *felt sense*, as *orientation*, as *perceptual readiness* — and these ways of being present do not translate into propositional form without losing what makes them functional.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Tool Analogy as Design Principle**
> Polanyi uses the analogy of a tool: when you hammer a nail skillfully, your awareness *dwells in* the hammer — you feel the nail through the hammer, not in the hammer. The hammer has become an extension of your intentional body. If you shift your focal attention to the hammer itself — to the weight, the grip, the swing arc — you lose the fluid performance that the subsidiarity enables. This tool analogy has a direct and non-obvious implication for PKB design. A PKB is itself a tool in the sense Polanyi means. When used well, the PKB should enable you to *dwell through* your notes toward your intellectual objectives — the notes are subsidiary, the problem is focal. But when the PKB becomes the object of focal attention (elaborate tagging systems, endless organization, comprehensive capture anxiety), the subsidiary dwelling collapses and the tool stops functioning as a cognitive extension. This is an underappreciated explanation of why some PKB systems paradoxically *obstruct* thinking rather than support it.

### The Dreyfus Progression Mechanism: Rules Dissolving into Intuition

The Dreyfus model's most important contribution for PKB design is its account of *what happens to explicit knowledge over the course of expertise development*. At the novice stage, explicit rules *are* the knowledge — the player has no other resource. As expertise develops, those rules do not simply get supplemented with richer rules; they get *absorbed* into pattern recognition and eventually into bodily intuition. The rules do not disappear, exactly, but they become the substrate of a holistic recognition that no longer requires consulting them.

This means that for any skill domain, there is a lifecycle of explicit knowledge in relation to performance. Early in skill development, explicit notes are tightly coupled to performance — you use them directly. As competence develops, the notes become less consulted, not because they are wrong but because their content has been internalized. At the expert stage, the notes may remain useful for articulating the skill to novices or for diagnostics when something goes wrong — but they no longer represent the knowledge that is actually operating in expert performance.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Expertise Paradox for PKB Users**
> There is a genuine paradox in expert PKB users: the more deeply someone has developed expertise in a domain, the *less* the accumulated notes in that domain represent what they actually know. The notes capture earlier, more explicit stages of understanding; the current expertise lives in a different register entirely — in embodied recognition, in fast intuition, in a felt sense of rightness that cannot be articulated without distortion. This means that a PKB's historical notes about a domain are always, in a sense, *lagged* — they document the stages of explicit learning but not the tacit expertise that has accumulated alongside them. A PKB user who confuses their note collection with their knowledge state will systematically underestimate both what they now know tacitly and the degree to which their most valuable knowing is unrepresented in their system.

### The SECI Spiral and the PKB's Natural Habitat

> [!cross-domain-connection] **SECI Externalization and Dewey's Reflective Inquiry**
> Nonaka's Externalization quadrant — the conversion of tacit to explicit through metaphor, dialogue, and modeling — is structurally isomorphic with Dewey's account of reflective inquiry, introduced in Report 08. For Dewey, reflection is initiated by a "felt difficulty" — a pre-reflective sense that something is wrong or incomplete — and proceeds through inquiry toward explicit formulation of the problem and its resolution. For Nonaka, externalization begins with a tacit sensing that something important needs expression and proceeds through metaphor and dialogue toward explicit capture. Both describe the same conversion process: the pre-reflective becoming reflective, the felt becoming articulated. This convergence suggests that the same practices that support Deweyan reflection (reflective journals, dialogue with others, structured retrospectives) are also the practices that support SECI externalization — and therefore the practices that partially bridge the tacit-explicit gap in a PKB workflow.

The SECI model clarifies the PKB's natural habitat. The Combination quadrant — where explicit knowledge is systematized and combined — is where a PKB operates most naturally. The PKB is a combination machine: it takes explicit notes, links them, organizes them, and produces more complex explicit structures. But the SECI model also clarifies what must happen *before* the PKB can be fed useful explicit content (Externalization) and what must happen *after* explicit content has been stored for it to become genuinely useful performance (Internalization). The PKB sits in the middle of the SECI spiral. It cannot substitute for the processes on either side of it.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Internalization and Report 11's Transfer Problem**
> The SECI model's Internalization quadrant — where explicit knowledge is converted back into tacit form through practice — directly illuminates Report 11's Transfer Problem. Report 11 established that stored explicit knowledge frequently fails to transfer to novel contexts. SECI explains *why*: transfer requires internalization, not merely storage. Knowledge that has been stored in a PKB but not practiced, applied, and refined through situated engagement remains in the Combination quadrant — explicit and combinable, but not yet tacit and deployable. The Transfer Problem is, in SECI terms, the problem of explicit knowledge that has not yet completed the spiral into internalized tacit skill. The PKB cannot accelerate this internalization; only practice can.

### Embodied Cognition and the Sensorimotor Foundation

Merleau-Ponty's contribution is to ground tacit knowledge not merely in the psychological but in the *bodily*. For Merleau-Ponty, the skilled body is not an instrument of a disembodied mind but the primary medium of intelligent engagement with the world. The carpenter who knows wood does not have a mental representation of wood grain that they then apply through manual action; their hands *are* the knowing. The musician who plays with feeling does not mentally represent the emotion and then produce it through finger movements; the feeling and the movement are one integrated event.

This has a profound implication: there is a class of tacit knowledge — **sensorimotor expertise** — that is not merely difficult to put into words but is categorically incommensurable with propositional capture. The knowledge *is* the sensorimotor pattern. Propositions about the pattern are not approximations that, if sufficiently precise, could substitute for the pattern; they are representations of something that operates in a completely different register.

> [!tension-identified] **The Partial Capture Tension: Enough Externalization vs. Too Much?**
> A genuine design tension emerges from the evidence. Nonaka demonstrates that partial externalization is valuable: approximating tacit knowledge in explicit form enables organizational combination, communication, and scaffolding for learners. But Dreyfus warns that over-relying on explicit rules past the appropriate stage impedes expertise development. The tension: how much should a PKB user invest in externalizing tacit knowledge? The resolution requires contextual judgment: externalization is most valuable when the knowledge is (a) domain-general rather than performance-specific, (b) at an intermediate tacitness level where metaphor and example can approximate it usefully, and (c) intended to scaffold learners or future selves who are at earlier stages, not to replace the continued practice that develops expert intuition. Externalize strategically; practice relentlessly.

### Return-and-Deepen: Report 08's Experiential Learning with Tacit Knowledge in View

Report 08 introduced [[Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle]] as a framework for processing experience through Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualization → Active Experimentation. At the time, this cycle was presented as a loop for transforming experience into explicit knowledge. With the tacit knowledge framework now in view, the cycle can be seen more precisely.

The Kolb cycle's most PKB-relevant stage — Abstract Conceptualization, where experience becomes generalizable insight — is precisely the SECI Externalization move. But Kolb's cycle also contains what SECI's framework makes explicit: Active Experimentation is Internalization — the return of explicit insight to embodied practice. The full cycle is not complete until Internalization has occurred; a PKB that captures Abstract Conceptualizations without prompting Active Experimentation is systematically missing the second half of the cycle.

Furthermore, [[Schön's Reflective Practitioner]] framework, also introduced in Report 08, distinguishes [[Reflection-in-Action]] (the real-time, tacit adjustment of skilled practice) from [[Reflection-on-Action]] (the post-hoc, explicit articulation of what happened). A PKB captures Reflection-on-Action; Reflection-in-Action is uncapturable by definition — it is happening in the midst of performance, in a timeframe too compressed for documentation. The most sophisticated aspect of expert performance — the live adjustment, the felt sense of the situation changing — lives in Reflection-in-Action and is invisible to the PKB.

> [!analytical-insight] **The PKB as a Reflection-on-Action Archive**
> The PKB is precisely and only a Reflection-on-Action system. It captures what you know after the fact — what you can articulate in retrospect about experiences, concepts, and insights. It cannot capture Reflection-in-Action, which is where expert performance lives. This is not a flaw in the PKB; it is a structural feature. Knowing this boundary allows the PKB user to invest appropriately: rich, textured Reflection-on-Action capture is genuinely valuable (for building conceptual scaffolding, for developing novice and intermediate understanding, for the externalization that makes knowledge shareable and combinable). But it must be paired with deliberate practice that develops Reflection-in-Action through repeated situated engagement.

## Phase V: Implications for PKM/PKB Design & Limitations

### A Taxonomy of Knowledge Types by PKB-Suitability

The synthesis above supports a practical taxonomy of knowledge types organized by their relationship to the tacit-explicit spectrum and their suitability for PKB capture.

**Tier 1 — Fully PKB-suitable**: Definitions, factual propositions, formal arguments, structured procedures, reference frameworks, conceptual models, and analogical scaffolding. This is the natural currency of knowledge management systems and where a PKB excels.

**Tier 2 — Partially PKB-suitable (Externalization zone)**: Evaluative criteria and rubrics (partial approximations of aesthetic or quality judgments), annotated examples and worked cases (showing tacit judgment in action), reflective narratives (capturing the felt texture of an experience in words), decision records (documenting what factors influenced a judgment after the fact), and conceptual metaphors developed through dialogue. These are the products of SECI Externalization: they approximate tacit knowledge in explicit form, losing something in translation but retaining enough to be useful.

**Tier 3 — Not PKB-suitable (Tacit practice zone)**: Expert pattern recognition in complex, dynamic situations; sensorimotor skill execution; holistic aesthetic judgment; fast intuitive response; Reflection-in-Action; and the felt sense of situational rightness that characterizes genuine mastery. These cannot be captured in notes — not because language is insufficient but because the knowledge functions in a register that propositional systems cannot reach.

> [!best-practice] **Design for the Tier, Not the Aspiration**
> A common PKB design failure is treating all three tiers as if they were Tier 1 — attempting comprehensive capture as if everything can eventually be put into notes. The evidence strongly suggests this is both futile and potentially counterproductive. Instead, design your PKB to excel at Tier 1 (systematic and comprehensive), invest in Externalization workflows for Tier 2 (knowing you are producing approximations), and for Tier 3, design complementary practices outside the PKB — deliberate practice protocols, deliberate reflection partnerships, apprenticeship relationships, and portfolio documentation that *triangulates toward* rather than *captures* tacit expertise.

### Design Principle 1: The Externalization Scaffold

For Tier 2 knowledge, the PKB's role is to support and record the Externalization process. This means designing specific note types for partially tacit knowledge:

**The Annotated Example Note**: Rather than a rule or principle, capture an example with rich annotations explaining what you were attending to, why it worked, and what tacit judgment was operating. A collection of fifty annotated examples of good writing teaches more than a note about "good writing principles," because the examples allow the reader (including your future self) to triangulate toward the tacit standard.

**The Judgment Record Note**: After making a significant judgment or decision, write a brief record: what the situation was, what you noticed, what you decided, what you would do differently, and — critically — what aspects of the judgment you *cannot* fully articulate. Explicitly flagging the non-articulable residue keeps you honest about what the note is and is not capturing.

**The Pattern Hypothesis Note**: When you begin to notice a recurring tacit pattern (a feeling of recognition that keeps recurring), write a note attempting to articulate the pattern — knowing it will be wrong or incomplete, but that the exercise of attempted articulation is itself valuable. Revise the note as understanding develops. This is Externalization in practice.

> [!best-practice] **Obsidian Implementation: The Tacit Interface Layer**
> Create a dedicated section of your PKB vault (folder: `Tacit-Interface/` or tag: `#tacit-interface`) for Tier 2 notes — notes that are explicitly labeled as approximations of tacit knowledge rather than explicit knowledge claims. Use a consistent frontmatter field: `knowledge-type: tacit-approximation` and `confidence: partial`. This prevents future-you from treating an approximation as a reliable rule. These notes should link to a `[[Deliberate Practice Log]]` that records actual performance experience — creating the essential pairing of articulated approximation and embodied practice.

### Design Principle 2: Complementary Practice Architecture

For Tier 3 knowledge — the irreducibly tacit — the PKB's direct contribution approaches zero. What matters is the *practice system* that surrounds the PKB. Four complementary practices are supported by the evidence:

**Deliberate Practice Protocols**: Ericsson's deliberate practice research (1993) established that domain-relevant, feedback-guided, effortful practice is the mechanism for developing expert intuition. This is SECI Internalization in action. The PKB can support this by: storing the conditions and targets of practice sessions (not the tacit knowledge itself), recording outcomes and feedback, and creating structured challenges to trigger genuine performance rather than comfortable repetition.

**Apprenticeship and Legitimate Peripheral Participation**: Lave and Wenger's research suggests that tacit knowledge in craft and professional domains is transmitted primarily through situated co-participation with more experienced practitioners. The PKB can capture what is said in mentorship relationships, but the transmission of tacit knowledge happens in the doing, not the documenting.

**Portfolio Documentation**: Rather than trying to articulate tacit expertise directly, document the *evidence* of tacit expertise — artifacts, outcomes, projects, feedback received, performance records. A portfolio triangulates toward tacit competence by showing what it produces rather than what it is. A musician who cannot articulate what makes their performance musical can document the performances themselves; over time, the portfolio constitutes a form of tacit knowledge record.

**Deliberate Reflection Partnerships**: Structured dialogue with peers or coaches, focused on probing the non-articulable, is among the most powerful Externalization mechanisms Nonaka documents. The PKB can store the outputs of these conversations; the conversations themselves should be designed to challenge you to verbalize what you normally don't — using Socratic questioning, comparison cases, and hypothetical variations.

> [!warning] **The Comprehensive Capture Trap**
> The most dangerous misconception in PKM culture is the idea that a comprehensive, well-organized PKB is equivalent to comprehensive understanding. The evidence reviewed here makes clear that this is false: the most important dimension of advanced expertise — the tacit, embodied, situationally responsive knowing that actually drives expert performance — is precisely what a PKB cannot capture. A PKB user who equates their knowledge base with their knowledge risks the cognitive equivalent of the drunk looking for his keys under the streetlight because the light is better there. They may have excellent notes; they may still be ignorant where it matters most.

### Design Principle 3: The Observatory Orientation

The most fundamental reorientation suggested by this synthesis is a shift in how you relate to your PKB in tacit-knowledge-rich domains. Instead of trying to capture expertise in your PKB, use the PKB to **observe** expertise — to systematically surround the tacit with explicit context, to triangulate toward the tacit standard through accumulated evidence, and to create the conditions under which the tacit can develop through deliberate practice.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Knowledge State — After**
> Return to your earlier position: What did you believe a PKB could do for tacit knowledge development? How has the analysis shifted your position? Was the shift incremental (adding to what you knew) or structural (reorganizing how you think about the relationship between notes and knowing)?

### Limitations: Where This Synthesis Reaches Its Bounds

Three limitations deserve explicit acknowledgment. First, the tacit-explicit distinction has been contested. Some knowledge management researchers (e.g., Tsoukas, 2003) argue that *all* knowledge is tacit to some degree, collapsing the distinction rather than maintaining it as a sharp boundary. This is a defensible position, but it does not invalidate the practical claim that different kinds of knowledge sit at very different points on the convertibility spectrum. Second, the Dreyfus model has been criticized for romanticizing intuition and undervaluing explicit reasoning in expert performance (Ericsson, 2006). The resolution here is contextual — both explicit deliberation and intuitive recognition play roles in expert performance, with context determining which predominates. Third, the design principles above are necessarily schematic; their implementation in any specific domain will require substantial judgment about which knowledge types sit in which tier.

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

### The Report's Central Synthesis: The PKB as Tacit Knowledge Observatory

> [!original-synthesis] **The PKB as Tacit Knowledge Observatory**
> The accumulated cross-domain analysis — Polanyi's focal/subsidiary structure, Dreyfus's expertise progression, Nonaka's SECI spiral, and the embodied and situated cognition tradition — converges on a reconception of the PKB's proper role in tacit-knowledge-rich domains. The reconception is this: **the PKB is not a repository of knowledge but an observatory of knowing**.
>
> An astronomical observatory cannot touch or contain the stars it observes. Its instruments cannot reach the objects of study. Yet the observatory is extraordinarily powerful precisely because systematic, cumulative observation allows triangulation toward understanding that no single observation could provide. The astronomer who cannot hold a star can nonetheless build extraordinarily precise models of stellar behavior through systematic observation over time.
>
> Similarly, the PKB user who cannot capture tacit expertise can nonetheless *observe* it systematically: documenting the conditions under which excellent performance occurs, recording the outcomes that serve as evidence of tacit competence, capturing the approximations produced through Externalization exercises, logging the trajectory of deliberate practice, and building a portfolio of cases that triangulate toward the tacit standard. This is not consolation for what cannot be stored — it is a genuinely powerful alternative relationship between a knowledge system and the tacit knowing it cannot contain.
>
> The design implication: a mature PKB in any domain of serious expertise should include an Observatory layer — a set of note types, workflows, and linking structures specifically designed for observing rather than capturing tacit expertise. This layer is not the PKB's primary content; it is its most intellectually honest contribution to expertise development.

### The Synthesis Question Revisited

Can a text-based PKB capture expert knowledge? The answer, now with full cross-domain grounding, is: *the kind of explicit, propositional knowledge that characterizes novice and intermediate understanding, yes; the kind of embodied, intuitive, situationally responsive knowing that characterizes genuine expertise, only approximately and partially — and some dimensions of expert knowing, not at all.*

This answer demands that PKB design respond to expertise development differently at different stages. At novice and advanced beginner stages, the PKB should maximize explicit knowledge accumulation, structural scaffolding, and rule documentation — these map onto Dreyfus's early stages where explicit guidance genuinely drives performance. At competent and proficient stages, the PKB's role shifts toward Externalization support — helping articulate tacit patterns as they emerge, building annotated example banks, and creating reflection structures that support the transition to holistic perception. At the expert stage, the PKB's role shifts again, toward Observatory functioning — documenting the conditions and evidence of expertise, supporting the deliberate practice that develops intuition, and preserving the outputs of rare successful Externalization moments when expert insight can be partially captured in words.

### Return-and-Deepen: Report 10's Scaffolding and Fading

Report 10 established the [[Expertise Reversal Effect]] — the finding that instructional supports that help novices actually harm experts, because explicit scaffolding interferes with the automated, intuitive processing that expertise relies on. With the tacit knowledge framework in view, the expertise reversal effect can now be understood mechanistically: scaffolding holds knowledge in explicit, focal awareness; expertise depends on knowledge operating subsidiarily. When scaffolding is present, the subsidiary operation is blocked and performance degrades. The PKB should therefore *fade its own scaffolding* in proportion to the user's developing expertise — not by removing notes, but by changing how notes are used. A highly detailed procedure note that was essential at the novice stage should eventually be replaced by a sparse Observatory entry that documents when the procedure is appropriate and what outcomes serve as evidence of its successful application — trusting that the execution itself is now tacit.

### Return-and-Deepen: Report 17's Note-Making as Knowledge Construction

Report 17 established that the highest-value note-making involves active construction — elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, generation — rather than passive capture. With the tacit knowledge framework in view, this finding can be extended: the generation effect's power, in tacit-knowledge-rich domains, comes not only from deeper encoding of explicit content but from the Externalization process itself. When you generate a note by trying to articulate what you know about a difficult evaluative judgment, the generative struggle is not merely encoding the resulting proposition — it is the partial conversion of tacit knowing into explicit form. The struggle, the approximation, the felt inadequacy of the words — these are signs that real Externalization is occurring, not just proposition-copying.

### Open Questions

This synthesis raises questions that warrant further inquiry. Does the Observatory orientation require systematic documentation protocols — and if so, what does a mature Observatory layer actually look like in Obsidian? How much deliberate practice is required before tacit internalization of explicit content reliably occurs — and does this vary by domain? What is the relationship between tacit knowledge development and the kind of retrieval practice that Report 20 recommends — can retrieval practice develop tacit pattern recognition, or only explicit recall?

## Phase VIII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Tacit Knowledge (Polanyi, 1958/1966 — Philosophy of Mind / Epistemology)**
> Knowledge that is functionally operative in skilled performance but cannot be fully articulated in explicit propositional form. Constitutively pre-reflective; not merely not-yet-articulated. Distinguished from explicit knowledge by its resistance to propositional capture and its dependence on subsidiarity for proper functioning. Boundary condition: admits of degrees — some tacit knowledge is more convertible than other tacit knowledge.

> [!definition] **Focal Awareness (Polanyi, 1966 — Philosophy of Mind)**
> The explicit, attentional foreground of skilled performance — what the performer is consciously attending to as the object of their activity. Distinguished from subsidiary awareness. Making subsidiary elements focal disrupts skilled performance. PKB relevance: notes capture content that is or was in focal awareness; they do not and cannot capture what operates subsidiarily.

> [!definition] **Subsidiary Awareness (Polanyi, 1966 — Philosophy of Mind)**
> The background, pre-reflective dimension of skilled performance — the tools, the body, the accumulated experience, the cultural norms that inform the focal performance without being its explicit object. Subsidiary awareness is the primary vehicle of tacit knowledge. Its conversion to focal awareness disrupts performance; its proper functioning depends on remaining in the background.

> [!definition] **SECI Model (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995 — Knowledge Management)**
> A four-quadrant spiral model of knowledge creation through conversion between tacit and explicit forms: Socialization (tacit to tacit via shared experience), Externalization (tacit to explicit via metaphor and dialogue), Combination (explicit to explicit via systematization), and Internalization (explicit to tacit via practice). A PKB operates primarily in the Combination quadrant and supports Externalization.

> [!definition] **Externalization (Nonaka, 1995 — Knowledge Management)**
> The conversion of tacit knowledge to explicit form through metaphor, analogy, dialogue, hypothesis, and modeling. Always partial and approximate — the explicit product approximates but never fully captures the tacit original. The most PKB-relevant quadrant of the SECI model outside of Combination.

> [!definition] **Internalization (Nonaka, 1995 / Dreyfus, 1986 — Knowledge Management / Educational Philosophy)**
> The conversion of explicit knowledge back into tacit form through embodied practice and experiential application. This is the mechanism by which explicit rules become tacit intuitions — the essential process of expertise development. Cannot be accomplished by reading notes; requires situated, effortful practice. The PKB can prompt and document internalization but cannot accomplish it.

> [!definition] **Dreyfus Skill Acquisition Model (Dreyfus & Dreyfus, 1980/1986 — Educational Philosophy / Phenomenology)**
> A five-stage developmental model from rule-following Novice to embodied, intuitive Expert. Each stage represents a different relationship between explicit knowledge and performance: at Novice, explicit rules *are* the performance; at Expert, explicit rules have been superseded by tacit intuition. PKB relevance: the PKB's contribution to core performance decreases as expertise increases, while its Observatory function becomes more important.

> [!definition] **Embodied Cognition (Merleau-Ponty, 1945; Varela et al., 1991 — Cognitive Science / Philosophy of Mind)**
> The thesis that cognition is constituted by patterns of sensorimotor engagement with the environment, not stored in a disembodied representational system. Expert knowledge in physical and perceptual domains is substantially embodied — it consists of refined sensorimotor contingencies that have no propositional equivalent. Boundary condition: abstract and formal domains may be less fully embodied than practical and perceptual ones.

> [!definition] **Situated Cognition (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Brown et al., 1989 — Cognitive Science / Educational Philosophy)**
> The thesis that knowledge is constituted by and inseparable from the activity, context, and community in which it is developed and applied. Decontextualized, abstract knowledge representations lose properties essential to practical function. The PKB note is constitutively decontextualized, which partially explains the Transfer Problem and the limits of PKB-based learning.

> [!definition] **Procedural Memory (Squire & Zola, 1996 — Cognitive Psychology / Neuroscience)**
> The memory system underlying skills, habits, and automated procedures. Operates through different neural architecture than declarative (explicitly accessible) memory. Procedural memory is the neurological substrate of tacit knowledge at the cognitive level: it encodes the *how* of performance rather than the *that* or *why*. Cannot be written to or read from via verbal instruction; develops only through practice.

> [!definition] **Legitimate Peripheral Participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991 — Educational Philosophy / Sociology)**
> The process through which novices become members of communities of practice by participating in authentic tasks at the periphery of the community, gradually taking on greater responsibility as competence develops. The primary mechanism of tacit knowledge transmission in craft and professional apprenticeship. Cannot be replicated by PKB study alone; requires situated co-participation with more experienced practitioners.

> [!definition] **Tacit Knowledge Observatory (This Report — Original Synthesis)**
> A reconception of the PKB's proper role in tacit-knowledge-rich domains: not a repository that captures tacit knowledge, but an observatory that systematically surrounds the tacit with explicit context, triangulates toward tacit standards through accumulated evidence, and creates conditions for tacit development through deliberate practice. Analogous to an astronomical observatory that cannot contain the stars but builds precise understanding through systematic, cumulative observation.

> [!definition] **Reflection-in-Action (Schön, 1983 — Educational Philosophy / Professional Practice)**
> The real-time, in-the-midst-of-performance adjustment and knowing characteristic of expert practitioners. Distinguished from Reflection-on-Action (post-hoc explicit articulation). Reflection-in-Action is tacit and situationally responsive; it operates in a timeframe incompatible with explicit documentation. PKBs capture Reflection-on-Action; Reflection-in-Action is structurally invisible to any documentation system.

### C. Methodology and Sources Note

> [!methodology-and-sources] **Research Grounding for This Report**
> This report draws on three primary knowledge traditions. Exploring this parallel illuminates both the powerful complementarity between AI assistance and human embodied expertise and the dangers of assuming that AI can substitute for the tacit knowledge development that only practice and experience can provide.
