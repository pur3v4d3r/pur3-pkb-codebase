# Cognitive Evaluation Theory: How External Events Shape Intrinsic Motivation

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Analytical Focus

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Prediction Point**
> Before reading further, commit to a prediction: Suppose a child who genuinely loves drawing is given a gold-star certificate every time they complete a drawing. After several weeks, the certificates stop. Does the child draw *more*, *less*, or *about the same* as before the reward programme began? Write down your prediction and the intuition behind it. The answer — and the explanation — is the core of what this report analyses.

There is a paradox at the heart of how we typically think about motivation, and it concerns something most people assume is straightforwardly good: rewards. Common sense, and a century of behaviourist psychology, tells us that rewarding behaviour strengthens it. Give someone a sticker for reading, praise them for completing a task, pay them for performing well, and they will be more likely to engage in that behaviour again. The logic seems airtight.

Yet in the early 1970s, a converging set of experiments began to tell a more unsettling story. Under specific conditions, rewarding a behaviour that a person intrinsically enjoys can lead, paradoxically, to *less* of that behaviour once the reward is removed. The pleasure of the activity itself appears to be partially consumed by the act of being rewarded for it. The child who loved drawing for its own sake now draws to get certificates; when the certificates stop, the drawing stops too.

This is not a minor anomaly or a curiosity. It is a phenomenon with profound implications for education, management, parenting, and any context in which one person tries to motivate another. It suggests that the relationship between external reinforcement and motivation is far more complex than operant conditioning models acknowledged, and that something important about human psychology is missed when we treat motivation as merely a function of reward history.

**Cognitive Evaluation Theory** ([[Cognitive-Evaluation-Theory|CET]]), developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan as a sub-theory of [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]] ([[Self-Determination-Theory|SDT]]), provides the most theoretically sophisticated and empirically supported account of this phenomenon. It explains *how* external events affect [[Intrinsic-Motivation|intrinsic motivation]] — not by issuing a blanket condemnation of rewards, but by identifying the precise psychological mechanism through which different types of external events, and even the same event administered differently, produce radically different motivational effects.

**This report's analytical focus is specific**: it examines the central explanatory mechanism in CET — the concept of [[Functional-Significance|functional significance]] — and asks how and why an event's functional meaning determines its motivational impact. The central question is: *What cognitive-evaluative process determines whether an external event enhances or undermines intrinsic motivation, and what structural features of events influence which pathway is activated?*

For comprehensive background on SDT's broader architecture — including [[Organismic-Integration-Theory|Organismic Integration Theory]], [[Basic-Psychological-Needs|Basic Psychological Needs Theory]], and the full continuum of [[self-determination]] — see the Foundational Report on [[Self-Determination-Theory|Self-Determination Theory]]. Here, we focus specifically on the sub-theory that addresses how external conditions modify intrinsic motivation states, drilling into mechanisms that the foundational treatment necessarily glosses.

Phase III examines the evidence base critically, including the major meta-analytic dispute that makes the field more contentious than it sometimes appears. Phase IV analyses the underlying psychological mechanisms — particularly the two separable pathways through which functional significance acts. ## Phase II: Analytical Framework

Before we can follow CET's argument, we need four conceptual tools: a precise definition of intrinsic motivation; the theory's account of perceived locus of causality; the construct of functional significance; and the distinction between reward contingency types. These are not merely definitional formalities — each one does serious theoretical work that the subsequent analysis depends on.

> [!definition] **Intrinsic Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> Motivation that arises from the inherent interest, enjoyment, or satisfaction of an activity itself, independent of separable outcomes. An intrinsically motivated person engages in an activity because it is experienced as freely chosen, interesting, and inherently rewarding. The prototypical markers are: voluntary initiation, persistence in the absence of external prompts, positive affect during engagement, and free-choice resumption after an interruption. Intrinsic motivation is NOT the same as liking an activity — one can like (be positively valenced toward) an activity one is extrinsically motivated to perform. The critical property is the perceived source of regulation: intrinsic motivation is internally regulated; extrinsic motivation is regulated by anticipated consequences outside the activity itself.

> [!definition] **Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC) (de Charms, 1968; Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> The subjective perception of the source of one's own behaviour — experienced as either originating within oneself (internal PLOC, "I chose to do this") or determined by external forces (external PLOC, "I am doing this because of outside pressure"). PLOC is not a binary state but a continuum, and it is phenomenological rather than objective: the same objectively constrained situation may register as internally caused for one person and externally caused for another. CET treats perceived locus of causality as the primary mediator linking external events to intrinsic motivation: events that shift PLOC toward the external pole undermine intrinsic motivation; events that preserve or strengthen an internal PLOC protect it. This is distinct from actual causality — the point is what the person *perceives*.

> [!definition] **Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> The psychological meaning an event carries for the person who experiences it, specifically with respect to two possible aspects: the **controlling aspect** (the degree to which the event is experienced as pressuring the person to behave in a particular way, implying that the event is managing or directing behaviour) and the **informational aspect** (the degree to which the event is experienced as providing competence-relevant feedback, conveying information about how well one is doing relative to a standard). Every external event — reward, praise, deadline, feedback, surveillance — carries *both* aspects to some degree, but one tends to be more salient. It is this relative salience, not the objective nature of the event, that determines its motivational effect. Events whose controlling aspect is most salient shift PLOC external and undermine intrinsic motivation. Events whose informational aspect is most salient enhance intrinsic motivation by supporting perceived competence, provided PLOC remains internal.

> [!definition] **Reward Contingency Types**
> The structural relationship between performance and reward. Three types matter most for CET: (1) **Task-contingent rewards** — given simply for participating in or completing the task, without reference to quality (e.g., "here is a sticker for drawing"). (2) **Completion-contingent rewards** — given for completing the task to a basic standard. (3) **Performance-contingent rewards** — given in proportion to quality of performance (e.g., "top marks get a prize"). CET predicts that task-contingent and completion-contingent rewards carry primarily a controlling functional significance and reliably undermine intrinsic motivation; performance-contingent rewards are more ambiguous because they also carry informational content about competence, though their controlling aspect typically dominates unless the framing is explicitly informational.

With these four tools in place, CET's core claim can be stated precisely: **external events affect intrinsic motivation through their functional significance — the relative salience of controlling versus informational aspects — which operates on intrinsic motivation through two separable mediating pathways: [[Perceived-Locus-of-Causality|perceived locus of causality]] (the autonomy pathway) and [[Perceived-Competence|perceived competence]] (the competence pathway)**.

> [!key-claim] **CET's Central Proposition**
> No external event has an intrinsically fixed effect on intrinsic motivation. Its effect is determined by the cognitive-evaluative interpretation that establishes its functional significance. Controlling events (those perceived primarily as pressure) undermine intrinsic motivation by externalising PLOC. Informational events (those perceived primarily as competence feedback) can enhance intrinsic motivation when they convey positive competence information and when PLOC remains internal. Amotivating events (those that convey neither agency nor competence — such as meaningless failure or arbitrary incompetence feedback) undermine both autonomy and competence perceptions, producing the deepest motivational damage.

What factors would determine which meaning is more salient? If you're reaching for the definitions above rather than understanding them, consider re-reading before proceeding.

### The Foundational Studies

The empirical story of CET begins with three experiments published within a few years of each other that converged on the same counterintuitive finding from different methodological angles.

In 1971, Deci published what became one of the most cited experiments in motivational psychology. Participants were asked to solve Soma puzzles (intrinsically interesting three-dimensional puzzles) across three sessions. The experimental group received monetary payment in the second session; the control group was never paid. In the third session, both groups continued with no payment. The critical measure was the amount of time participants spent with the puzzles during a "free choice" period — when the experimenter had stepped out of the room and no one was watching. Deci's finding was stark: participants who had been paid in Session 2 spent significantly less free-choice time with the puzzles in Session 3 compared to those who had never been paid. Payment had, it appeared, partially consumed their intrinsic interest.

Two years later, Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973) published a study with preschool children and drawing materials — the paradigmatic "expected reward" study. Children who showed initial high interest in drawing were divided into three conditions: one group was told they would receive a "Good Player" award for drawing (expected reward), one group received a surprise award after drawing (unexpected reward), and a control group neither expected nor received an award. The critical observation came one to two weeks later, when the drawing materials were made available in a naturalistic classroom free-play setting. Expected-reward children showed dramatically lower interest in drawing compared to both the unexpected-reward group and the control group. Crucially, the unexpected-reward group showed no decline — consistent with CET's prediction that it is the anticipation of reward (and thus the controlling significance of working *for* the reward) that causes the damage, not the reward stimulus itself.

> [!evidence] **The Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973) Core Finding**
> Children in the expected-reward condition showed significantly lower free-choice engagement with drawing materials 1–2 weeks after the reward phase, compared to unexpected-reward and no-reward controls. Effect sizes were substantial (Cohen's d estimates from later meta-analyses place the effect around 0.4–0.5 for expected tangible rewards). This study established the "overjustification effect" as a concept — the idea that externally justifying behaviour you already enjoy paradoxically weakens your motivation to engage in it.

By the mid-1980s, a substantial body of experimental literature had accumulated. When Deci and Ryan published their systematic theoretical account in 1985, they could draw on dozens of studies showing similar patterns across varied populations, activities, and reward types.

### The Meta-Analytic Battleground

The field became genuinely contentious in 1994 when Judy Cameron and W. David Pierce published a meta-analysis of 96 studies on extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation and reached a conclusion that contradicted CET's central claims. Cameron and Pierce argued that the undermining effect was largely a methodological artefact — detectable primarily in studies using free-choice behaviour as the dependent measure (rather than self-reported interest), and specifically for tangible expected rewards with no performance contingency. More provocatively, they argued that verbal rewards (praise) and performance-contingent rewards did not undermine intrinsic motivation and could, in some circumstances, enhance it.

The response was swift and pointed. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999) published a new meta-analysis of 128 studies — the most comprehensive to date — that reached the opposite conclusion. Their analysis found consistent evidence that:

> [!evidence] **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) — Key Findings from 128 Studies**
> (1) Expected tangible rewards reliably undermined intrinsic motivation as measured by free-choice behaviour (d ≈ −0.36) and self-reported interest (d ≈ −0.15). (2) Unexpected rewards did not undermine intrinsic motivation. (3) Verbal rewards (praise) enhanced intrinsic motivation when conveying positive competence information. (4) Engagement-contingent and completion-contingent tangible rewards showed the strongest undermining. (5) Performance-contingent tangible rewards showed an intermediate pattern, reflecting their dual controlling/informational character.

> [!tension-identified] **The Meta-Analysis Interpretation Battle**
> Cameron/Pierce (1994) and Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999) analysed largely overlapping sets of studies and reached opposite conclusions. How? The disagreement is not primarily about the data — it is about *how to code the variables*. The two teams classified studies into contingency categories differently, defined "undermining" using different measurement criteria, and made different modelling choices about moderator variables. This reveals a methodological problem that is partly irresolvable: the functional significance construct is difficult to operationalise objectively. When an experiment labels a condition "expected reward," it is not obvious whether participants experienced this as primarily controlling or informational — and the researchers' coding decisions implicitly embed theoretical commitments. The dispute is not just empirical; it is partly about what counts as a fair test of the theory.

Cameron and Pierce's subsequent work (1994, 2002) made a valuable contribution despite this controversy: they forced the field to disaggregate reward effects more finely. Their finding that performance-contingent rewards don't uniformly undermine motivation is consistent with CET's framework if we accept that performance-contingent rewards carry informational significance alongside controlling significance. The real disagreement is about which effect dominates, and under what conditions.

### What the Evidence Genuinely Supports

Stepping back from the meta-analytic dispute, several conclusions emerge from the full evidence base with reasonable confidence.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **What the Pattern of Findings Actually Shows**
> The undermining effect is real but not universal. Expected tangible rewards for task engagement (where reward receipt does not depend on quality) reliably undermine free-choice behaviour in laboratory paradigms. This effect is medium-sized and has been replicated across decades. However, the effect is highly conditional: it does not apply to unexpected rewards, it is weakened or reversed when rewards carry genuine positive competence information, it is sensitive to whether the controlling aspect is made salient by the administration context (how the reward is presented, by whom, and in what relational context), and it is moderated by individual differences in need for autonomy. The effect is NOT a universal property of rewards-as-objects; it is a property of the psychological meaning that particular reward interactions create in specific people in specific contexts.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **The Praise Finding Is Particularly Informative**
> Verbal rewards (praise) consistently emerge from the meta-analytic literature as enhancing rather than undermining intrinsic motivation — which is exactly what CET predicts. Praise is, structurally, a controlling event: someone in authority is evaluating you and finding you sufficient. But when praise is framed as conveying genuine competence information ("You did that correctly; you have real skill here"), it activates the informational pathway more than the controlling pathway, and the result is enhanced intrinsic motivation. This finding is important because it shows that CET's mechanism works in both directions: it is not that external events are inherently undermining; it is that the functional significance framework correctly predicts *which* events undermine and *which* enhance.

## Phase IV: Mechanisms, Dynamics & Deep Analysis

### The Two-Pathway Architecture

CET posits that functional significance acts on intrinsic motivation through two separable mechanisms. Understanding them as genuinely separate — rather than as two faces of a single process — matters because they can operate in opposite directions simultaneously, they respond to different features of the environment, and they have different developmental trajectories.

**Pathway 1: The Autonomy Pathway (via PLOC)**

When an external event's controlling aspect is most salient, the person experiences their behaviour as being caused by the external pressure — a shift from internal PLOC toward external PLOC. This shift is not simply a cognitive re-labelling of why one is doing something. It reflects a genuine motivational transformation: the activity is no longer experienced as freely chosen, as expressive of one's interests and values, as an extension of the self. Instead, it becomes instrumental — a means to obtain the reward or avoid the consequence.

The mechanism here operates at the level of psychological freedom. [[Self-determination]] is not just a philosophical value; within SDT, it is a basic psychological need whose satisfaction is necessary for well-being and sustained motivation. When PLOC shifts external, that need is frustrated. The activity may continue — extrinsic motivation can maintain behaviour for extended periods — but the phenomenological quality of engagement changes. Intrinsic motivation, which requires that the activity be experienced as volitional, diminishes.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Invisible Transformation of Meaning**
> What the autonomy pathway mechanism reveals is that rewards do not merely add a new reason to perform an activity alongside the pre-existing intrinsic reason. They can transform the *experienced meaning* of the activity itself. Before the reward, drawing was an act of self-expression. After repeated reward administration, drawing is an act of earning. The activity's identity — what it *is* for the person — has changed. This is why the effect persists after rewards are removed: you cannot simply subtract the reward and expect to return to the previous motivational state, because the activity's meaning has been reorganised. This is a far more radical claim than merely saying "people become accustomed to rewards" — it is a claim about how reward structures reshape the self-world relationship.

**Pathway 2: The Competence Pathway (via Perceived Competence)**

Simultaneously and independently, external events carry information — or the absence of information — about competence. CET holds that perceived competence is a second basic psychological need: people need to feel effective in their interactions with the environment. Intrinsic motivation is, in part, motivated by the inherent satisfaction of experiencing oneself as competent.

Events that convey positive competence information — genuine praise, success feedback that is performance-contingent and framed as informational, mastery-referenced evaluation — satisfy the competence need and can enhance intrinsic motivation. Events that convey negative competence information, or no meaningful competence information at all, frustrate this need.

Critically, the competence pathway has a directional asymmetry with the autonomy pathway: negative competence events (failure feedback, criticism, arbitrary negative evaluation) reliably undermine intrinsic motivation through the competence pathway, but they *also* tend to shift PLOC external (because the person experiences themselves as failing to be in control of outcomes). This means negative competence experiences operate through both pathways simultaneously, producing particularly severe motivational damage.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Asymmetry Between Enhancement and Damage**
> The two-pathway architecture produces an interesting asymmetry: enhancing intrinsic motivation requires satisfying *both* the autonomy and competence needs simultaneously — an event must convey positive competence information *and* do so in a way that preserves internal PLOC. This is a conjunction requirement. Undermining intrinsic motivation, by contrast, requires only one pathway — either externalising PLOC *or* frustrating perceived competence will damage intrinsic motivation. This means that intrinsic motivation is harder to build than to erode. It is robust against threats from a single pathway, but only when the other pathway is actively supported. In instructional contexts, this implies that well-intentioned informational praise can be partially neutralized if delivered in a surveillance-heavy, controlling evaluative atmosphere — the competence benefit is offset by autonomy cost.

### The Temporal Dynamics of Functional Significance

The functional significance of an event is not static. It can shift over time and with repeated administration, and this dynamism has important implications for understanding how reward systems erode motivation gradually rather than immediately.

Initial task-contingent rewards may carry a mixed functional significance — the person genuinely does not know what to make of them, and the controlling aspect may not immediately be the most salient. With repetition, however, the contingency structure becomes transparent: the person learns that they are being given something to do something. The instrumentality of the relationship becomes salient, and the controlling aspect strengthens over repeated administrations.

This is consistent with the finding that the undermining effect is often stronger in studies with longer reward administration periods, and that the effect on self-reported interest (a more internal, dispositional measure) typically lags behind the effect on free-choice behaviour. The motivational transformation takes time; it is a gradual reorganisation of the person's relationship to the activity.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **CET and Communicative Pragmatics: The Same Sentence, Different Speech Acts**
> Cognitive Evaluation Theory's central insight — that the same event carries different meaning depending on its functional significance — has a precise structural parallel in linguistic pragmatics, specifically [[speech act theory]] (Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969). In speech act theory, an utterance's meaning is not exhausted by its propositional content (what it literally says); its force depends on the social act it performs (a promise, a threat, a directive, an assessment). "You're good at this" can be a genuine expression of admiration (informational), a manipulative tool to elicit compliance ("since you're so good at this, you should keep doing it" — controlling), or a performance of social role. The *same surface form* carries radically different illocutionary force depending on context, relationship, power dynamics, and history. CET's functional significance is, in essence, the motivational analogue of illocutionary force: it is not what the event literally delivers but what social-psychological act it performs in the relationship that matters. This parallel suggests that CET would benefit from closer engagement with sociolinguistic and relational accounts of how competence and control signals are communicated — and why the same reward, administered by different people in different relational contexts, has such different effects.

### What Determines Functional Significance?

If functional significance is the key mediating variable, the practical question becomes: what features of an event, its administration, and the surrounding context determine which aspect — controlling or informational — is more salient? CET identifies several factors:

**Contingency structure**: How the reward is connected to the behaviour. Task-contingent rewards emphasise "you will get X if you do Y," which makes the instrumentality salient. Performance-contingent rewards connect reward to quality, introducing a competence dimension. Unexpected rewards, administered after the fact, cannot logically have been the reason for the behaviour — they carry primarily informational significance.

**Administrative language**: The way rewards or feedback are presented strongly influences functional significance. Deci and Ryan (1985) describe the difference between "informational language" ("You did really well on that puzzle — you found an efficient strategy") and "controlling language" ("You did exactly what you were supposed to do; you met the standard"). The first presents performance as reflecting the person's competence; the second presents performance as conformance to an external standard.

**Power and surveillance context**: Being observed, evaluated, or otherwise subject to scrutiny heightens the controlling aspect of any event, because it makes the evaluative relationship — and the power asymmetry within it — salient. This is why research on surveillance consistently shows motivational costs even when no specific rewards or punishments are involved: the awareness of being watched carries a controlling functional significance on its own.

**Relational history**: Whether the person has experienced the rewarder/evaluator as autonomy-supportive or controlling in the past. A teacher who has consistently respected students' perspectives and acknowledged their experiences creates a relational context in which even evaluative events carry primarily informational significance; a teacher who has used grades and praise as management tools creates a context in which any evaluation is likely to register as primarily controlling.

> [!tension-identified] **The Operationalisation Problem: When Functional Significance Becomes Unfalsifiable**
> CET's most significant theoretical vulnerability is related to its greatest strength. The concept of functional significance is powerful precisely because it does not tie motivational effects to the structural properties of events — it allows the same event to have different effects depending on how it is experienced. But this flexibility comes at a cost: functional significance is extraordinarily difficult to operationalise independently of the motivational outcomes it is supposed to predict. In most studies, the researcher manipulates the event (reward type, feedback framing) and then measures intrinsic motivation. The functional significance is inferred from the outcome, not measured directly. This creates the risk of circular explanation: "The reward undermined intrinsic motivation because it was controlling; how do we know it was controlling? Because it undermined intrinsic motivation." The studies that get closest to independent measurement of functional significance (using manipulation checks, thought-listing procedures, or phenomenological reports) provide more compelling evidence — but they remain the minority. This is not a fatal flaw but a persistent limitation that needs to be held in mind when interpreting the evidence base.

## Phase V: Implications, Applications & Limitations

### Direct Implications

CET's most direct implication is a challenge to any educational or organisational practice that uses tangible reward contingencies as a primary motivational tool. This does not mean that rewards are universally counterproductive — the evidence does not support that conclusion. It means that reward systems need to be designed with functional significance in mind: will this reward be experienced as controlling or informational by the person receiving it?

The implications branch across several domains:

**Feedback design**: CET provides the most useful theoretical framework for instructional feedback design currently available. Feedback that is competence-referenced (telling the person what they did well and why it reflects their skill), specific rather than global, and delivered in a relational context characterised by autonomy support will tend to activate the informational pathway and enhance intrinsic motivation. Feedback that is evaluative-comparative (telling the person where they rank relative to others), tied to consequences, delivered in a surveillance context, or framed in normative rather than mastery-referenced terms will tend to activate the controlling pathway and undermine intrinsic motivation.

> [!best-practice] **Designing Informational Rather Than Controlling Feedback**
> Effective feedback under CET principles: (1) References the person's own prior performance rather than comparative norms — progress feedback is more informational, rank feedback is more controlling. (2) Uses language that attributes performance to internal competencies ("you found an efficient approach") rather than external compliance ("you followed the instructions correctly"). (3) Is delivered in a context where the relationship is characterised by autonomy support — acknowledgment of the person's perspective, explanations rather than directives, non-controlling language. (4) Avoids surveillance-like monitoring that makes the evaluative relationship salient even in the absence of explicit evaluative events.

**Praise and recognition**: CET provides a principled explanation for the research finding that praise is not uniformly positive. Person-directed praise ("you're smart") tends to create ego involvement and carries controlling significance; performance-directed, mastery-referenced praise ("your approach to that problem was effective") carries informational significance. The growth mindset literature (Dweck) and CET converge on this point from different theoretical starting positions — and their convergence provides stronger grounds for the recommendation than either alone.

**Game-based learning and gamification**: [[Gamification]] in educational contexts — adding point systems, badges, leaderboards, and achievement structures to learning activities — is, from a CET perspective, a practice that systematically layers controlling functional significance onto activities. This does not mean gamification always undermines intrinsic motivation, but it means the burden of proof should be on demonstrating that the specific game elements preserve rather than damage the informational significance of engagement. Leaderboards, in particular, are highly problematic: they make social comparison salient, activate external reference frames for competence evaluation, and convert intrinsically satisfying performance into ranked instrumentality.

> [!warning] **The Gamification Paradox**
> The activities most commonly targeted for gamification are those that educators and designers perceive as insufficiently engaging — and the addition of game mechanics is intended to supply the missing motivation. But CET predicts that if the activity was already somewhat intrinsically interesting (or had the potential to become so with appropriate autonomy-supportive framing), the addition of controlling reward structures will damage that potential. Gamification often imports the motivational dynamics of extrinsic contingency precisely where intrinsic motivation was most valuable to preserve. The question to ask before gamifying is not "will this increase engagement?" (it often will, in the short term) but "what kind of engagement, through what motivational mechanism, and at what cost to intrinsic interest?"

### Limitations and Honest Boundaries

CET has been enormously generative and remains the most theoretically rigorous account of how external events affect intrinsic motivation. But the theory has genuine limitations that practitioners and researchers need to hold alongside its insights.

**The operationalisation problem revisited**: As noted in Phase IV, functional significance is theoretically post-hoc in many studies. The theory can explain most findings retrospectively by invoking which functional significance aspect was dominant, but it is harder to predict in advance, in a specific context, which aspect will dominate for a specific person. Until we have reliable, independent measures of functional significance (separate from the motivational outcomes they predict), the causal story remains somewhat circular.

**Population specificity**: The foundational research was conducted almost entirely in North American, Western European, and East Asian academic populations, with laboratory tasks of limited ecological validity. The degree to which CET's predictions hold in contexts with different cultural relationships to authority, evaluation, and reward is not well established. There is some cross-cultural evidence for the basic need structure of SDT, but the specific functional significance mechanism may operate differently in collectivist contexts where evaluation is understood within a relational rather than autonomous frame.

**Individual differences are underspecified**: CET predicts that controlling events undermine intrinsic motivation on average, but individuals differ substantially in how sensitive they are to this effect. People with high [[Autonomy-Orientation|autonomy orientation]] (from SDT's [[Causality-Orientations-Theory|Causality Orientations Theory]]) show smaller undermining effects; people with high [[Control-Orientation|control orientation]] may actually show facilitated performance under controlling conditions, at least in the short term. CET's current theoretical architecture does not fully integrate these individual difference moderators — it acknowledges them, but they are not part of the core explanatory model.

**The intensity question**: CET predicts the direction of effects (controlling events undermine, informational events enhance) but is less precise about magnitude. Under what conditions are the effects large enough to be practically significant? The meta-analytic literature shows medium effects on average, but individual studies show enormous variation — and the moderator analysis is not yet at a point where practitioners can reliably predict the size of motivational costs in their specific context.

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

### The Central Question Revisited

We began with the question: *What cognitive-evaluative process determines whether an external event enhances or undermines intrinsic motivation?*

After sustained analysis, we can answer with precision. The cognitive-evaluative process is the interpretation of an event's functional significance — specifically, the assignment of relative salience to its controlling and informational aspects. This interpretation is not a simple perception of objective properties; it is a *construction* assembled from the event's structural features, the administrative context, the relational history, and the individual's dispositional orientations. Once the functional significance interpretation is established, it acts on intrinsic motivation through two separable pathways: the autonomy pathway (via PLOC) and the competence pathway (via perceived competence). The net motivational effect is the integration of these two pathway outputs — which is why the same structural event can produce enhancement, no effect, or undermining depending on the interpretation that is constructed.

This answer has important second-order implications. It means that the question "do rewards undermine motivation?" is, in the strict sense, unanswerable as posed. The correct question is "do *these rewards*, administered *in this way*, *by this person*, *in this relational context*, *for this person with this motivational history*?" tend to register as primarily controlling or primarily informational? And it is this reframing that is CET's most important practical contribution — not a prohibition on rewards, but a radical shift in how we think about what external events actually do to motivation.

### The Interpretive Filter Model: An Original Synthesis

> [!original-synthesis] **The Interpretive Filter Model of Functional Significance**
>
> Synthesising CET's core architecture with the evidence on moderating factors and the temporal dynamics section, I propose a model that makes the functional significance concept more tractable for both research and practice. Rather than treating functional significance as a property of events (or even of events-in-context), the model treats it as the output of an *interpretive filter* — a relatively stable, individual-level attentional and evaluative orientation that determines which aspects of external events are most salient for a given person at a given time.
>
> The interpretive filter has three components:
>
> **(1) The relational frame**: The established meaning-structure of the evaluative relationship between the person and the source of the external event (teacher, employer, parent, system). This frame is built up through history and cannot be altered by a single event; it acts as a background context within which new events are interpreted. A controlling relational frame means that even genuinely informational events will be partly assimilated to a controlling interpretation; an autonomy-supportive relational frame allows informational events to be received as such.
>
> **(2) The motivational history with the specific activity**: Whether the activity has previously been predominantly internally or externally regulated. Activities with a long history of intrinsic engagement have a "motivational baseline" that is more resistant to controlling events; activities with a history of extrinsic regulation are more vulnerable to further externalisation of PLOC because the internal referent for the activity's value has already been weakened.
>
> **(3) The dispositional orientation**: The individual's relatively stable tendency to orient toward internal versus external causality (captured in SDT by Causality Orientations Theory). Autonomy-oriented individuals maintain internal PLOC more readily under controlling conditions; control-oriented individuals are more vulnerable to the undermining effect.
>
> What this model adds to standard CET is a mechanism for understanding why the *same* event produces such different effects across people and contexts: the interpretive filter is doing real work, not as a passive receiver but as an active constructor of meaning. It also provides a leverage point for intervention: rather than focusing only on event properties (making rewards informational), practitioners should attend to the relational frame (building an autonomy-supportive relationship context over time) and the motivational history (avoiding early externalisation of regulation for activities where intrinsic interest is the goal). The event-level and relationship-level interventions are not substitutes; they operate at different timescales and through different mechanisms.

### The Asymmetry Theorem Refined

An important synthesis point emerges from integrating the two-pathway model with the temporal dynamics analysis. Phase IV noted that intrinsic motivation is harder to build than to erode (the conjunction vs. disjunction requirement). We can now add temporal dimension to this asymmetry: **the enhancement pathway is fragile and requires sustained conditions, while the undermining pathway is robust to variation in conditions**.

Positive competence feedback in an autonomy-supportive context can enhance intrinsic motivation — but this enhancement requires the autonomy-supportive context to be maintained. A single controlling event in an otherwise informational context can partially reverse the enhancement. By contrast, once controlling events have shifted PLOC external and established an extrinsic motivational regulation for the activity, reverting to no-reward conditions does not automatically restore intrinsic motivation. The activity's meaning has been reorganised, and re-establishing internal PLOC requires active intervention.

> [!original-synthesis] **Motivational Asymmetry and Instructional Design Sequencing**
> The temporal asymmetry between undermining (fast-acting, hard to reverse) and enhancement (slow-building, fragile) has direct implications for the *sequencing* of external events in instructional contexts. Specifically: the damage done by early controlling events in a learning sequence is disproportionately costly relative to late controlling events, because early events shape the interpretive filter through which subsequent events are processed. A teacher who begins the year with high-stakes, controlling evaluation practices, then shifts to autonomy-supportive practices mid-year, will likely achieve lower intrinsic motivation outcomes than a teacher who maintains autonomy-supportive practices throughout — even if the total "dose" of controlling events is identical. The order matters. This is not explicitly stated in standard CET, but it follows directly from the interaction between temporal dynamics and the interpretive filter model. Instructional designers should treat the first exposures to a domain or activity as particularly high-stakes for motivational architecture, not merely for knowledge structure.

>
> - **[[999-report-orginizing/_permanent-notes/_permanent-notes/Self-Regulated-Learning|Self-Regulated Learning]]** — The SRL report examines how learners regulate their cognitive, metacognitive, and motivational processes. CET connects to SRL through the motivational regulation dimension: the degree to which a learner is intrinsically versus extrinsically regulated determines their regulatory strategy selection, persistence, and deep processing engagement. Learners with internally regulated motivation engage in more effective self-regulatory processes. Conversely, external reward structures that undermine intrinsic motivation (per CET) would be expected to simultaneously degrade self-regulatory quality — particularly the autonomous goal-setting and self-evaluation processes that SRL depends on. The CET → SRL pathway is undertheorised in the literature and represents a promising integration target.
>
> - **[[Cognitive-Load-Theory|Cognitive Load Theory]]** — CLT and CET address different aspects of the learning situation (cognitive architecture vs. motivational architecture), but they intersect in important ways. High extrinsic motivation under controlling conditions may produce surface processing (focused on completing the task to obtain the reward) that aligns with lower cognitive investment — potentially increasing extraneous cognitive load while reducing germane load. Conversely, high intrinsic motivation, by producing deeper engagement with the material's structure, may facilitate the kind of elaborative processing that builds schemas (the goal of CLT-informed instruction). This is a connection neither theory makes explicit, but exploring it would deepen understanding of why motivationally-optimal conditions tend also to be cognitively-optimal conditions.
>
> - **[[Attribution-Theory|Attribution Theory]]** — Weiner's attribution theory addresses how people explain their successes and failures. CET's PLOC construct is conceptually related to Weiner's locus of control dimension, but they are not identical: PLOC in CET is specifically about the perceived cause of *behaviour* (internal vs. external), while locus of control in attribution theory is about the perceived cause of *outcomes* (success/failure). The intersection is particularly important for understanding how competence feedback interacts with attributional patterns: negative feedback attributed to controllable, internal causes (effort) has different motivational effects than the same feedback attributed to stable, internal causes (lack of ability) — and CET's competence pathway prediction depends on this attributional dimension without fully theorising it.
>

>
> - **[[Self-Determination Theory — Foundational Report]]** — This focused analysis extends the foundational SDT treatment by providing a detailed mechanistic account of how external conditions affect the intrinsic/extrinsic motivation dimension. It adds the functional significance concept, the two-pathway model, and the temporal asymmetry analysis as layers that the Foundational Report does not cover in depth. ## Phase VIII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Amotivation**
> A state in which the person lacks any sense of agency with respect to an activity — neither intrinsic motivation (engagement for its own sake) nor extrinsic motivation (engagement for separable outcomes). Amotivation is the motivational consequence of events that simultaneously frustrate perceived competence and externalise PLOC to the point of helplessness. It is the deepest form of motivational damage in the SDT framework and is associated with disengagement, helplessness, and eventually dropout. Amotivation is distinct from low intrinsic motivation — one can be extrinsically motivated (working for rewards) without being amotivated.

> [!definition] **Autonomy Orientation (from Causality Orientations Theory)**
> An individual-level difference variable describing the general tendency to orient toward the autonomous aspects of environments — to notice and be regulated by features that support interest, value, and personal meaning. Autonomy-oriented individuals show attenuated undermining effects in response to controlling events. They are not *immune* to functional significance effects, but their interpretive filter has a stronger default bias toward internal PLOC. Contrasts with *control orientation* (tendency to be regulated by controlling features) and *impersonal orientation* (tendency to attribute regulation to uncontrollable forces, associated with amotivation).

> [!definition] **Causality Orientations Theory (COT)**
> A mini-theory within SDT that describes individual differences in people's tendencies to orient toward particular types of motivational regulation. Not to be confused with [[Attribution-Theory|attribution theory]]. COT predicts that autonomy-oriented individuals will respond differently to controlling events than control-oriented individuals, and this moderates the functional significance mechanism in CET. An important implication: identical instructional interventions will produce different motivational outcomes for individuals with different causality orientations.

> [!definition] **Ego Involvement**
> A motivational state in which self-worth is contingent on performance outcomes — performance becomes a test of the person's value or adequacy rather than an expression of their interests. Ego involvement is theoretically related to the controlling pathway in CET: events that create ego involvement (typically comparative evaluation, normative feedback, and high-stakes assessment) introduce a self-esteem regulation layer that makes the activity extrinsically salient and shifts PLOC external. Harackiewicz and Manderlink (1984) showed that ego-involving praise ("you're much better than average") undermine intrinsic motivation while task-involving praise ("you've really mastered the strategy") does not.

> [!definition] **Engagement-Contingent Reward**
> A reward contingent simply on engaging in or starting an activity, without reference to completion or quality. This is the most purely controlling reward type in CET's taxonomy: the contingency "if you do it, you get the reward" makes the external reason for the behaviour maximally salient. Empirically, engagement-contingent and task-contingent rewards show the strongest undermining effects in the Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999) meta-analysis.

> [!definition] **Free-Choice Behaviour**
> The dependent variable most commonly used in CET research to measure intrinsic motivation: the amount of time a person voluntarily spends with an activity when they are not required to do so, typically measured during a period when the experimenter is absent and no external contingencies are operating. Free-choice behaviour is considered a more direct behavioural measure of intrinsic motivation than self-reported interest, because it does not depend on verbal self-report. However, it also has limitations: it may reflect habit or accessibility rather than intrinsic motivation, and it is difficult to measure in ecological (non-laboratory) contexts.

> [!definition] **Informational Feedback**
> Feedback whose primary functional significance is informational — it conveys genuine competence-relevant information to the recipient in a context where PLOC remains internal. Informational feedback is characterised by specificity (pointing to particular aspects of performance), mastery-referencing (comparing performance to personal standards or task standards rather than to other people), process orientation (attributing performance to strategies and effort rather than global ability), and non-controlling language (describing rather than evaluating). CET predicts that informational feedback enhances intrinsic motivation by satisfying the competence need without threatening the autonomy need.

> [!definition] **Internalisation**
> The process by which externally regulated behaviours are gradually taken in and re-regulated by the self — moving along the self-determination continuum from external regulation toward identified regulation and ultimately integrated regulation. While CET specifically addresses intrinsic motivation (the far end of the intrinsic pole), Organismic Integration Theory (another SDT sub-theory) addresses internalisation of extrinsic motivation. The two processes are related: creating conditions that support autonomy and competence facilitates both intrinsic motivation (when activities are inherently interesting) and internalisation (when activities are not inherently interesting but have personal or social value).

> [!definition] **Overjustification Effect**
> The specific psychological phenomenon in which adding an external justification (reward) to a behaviour that is already intrinsically motivated reduces the person's subsequent intrinsic interest in that behaviour. The term "overjustification" refers to the idea that the external reason provides more justification than is needed — and in the process, changes the person's interpretation of why they were performing the activity in the first place. Originally named by Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973); theoretically explained by CET's PLOC mechanism.

> [!definition] **Perceived Competence**
> The subjective sense of effectiveness in one's interactions with the environment — feeling capable, skilled, and able to produce intended outcomes. Distinct from objective competence: a person can be highly competent but have low perceived competence (as in the Impostor Phenomenon), or low competence but high perceived competence (overconfidence). In CET, perceived competence is a basic psychological need whose satisfaction by external events (through positive informational feedback) can enhance intrinsic motivation, and whose frustration (through negative feedback or task failure without informational framing) undermines intrinsic motivation.

> [!definition] **Surveillance**
> The awareness of being observed or monitored, even in the absence of explicit evaluative events. CET research (Lepper & Greene, 1975) shows that surveillance carries a controlling functional significance on its own: being watched introduces an external audience, makes the evaluative relationship salient, and tends to shift PLOC external. This finding has significant implications for digital learning environments in which learner behaviour is continuously logged and monitored, even if that monitoring is not connected to explicit rewards or consequences.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Expectancy-Value Theory]]
> > EVT (Eccles, Wigfield) and CET approach motivation from different directions: EVT focuses on the cognitive appraisal of tasks (do I expect to succeed? does this task have value for me?), while CET focuses on the source of regulation (am I doing this for internal or external reasons?). These frameworks are frequently treated as competitors, but they are better understood as complementary: EVT explains the structure of motivation (what determines whether someone will engage), while CET explains the quality and sustainability of motivation (what determines whether engagement is internally vs. externally regulated). A focused comparative analysis would map the intersection points, identify where each is insufficient alone, and propose an integration.

> > Dweck's growth vs. fixed mindset framework shares important territory with CET — particularly around praise, competence feedback, and the effects of evaluative contexts on motivation and persistence. But the two frameworks make somewhat different predictions and are grounded in different theoretical mechanisms (implicit theories of ability vs. functional significance of feedback). A focused analysis would compare their predictions for specific instructional scenarios, examine where they converge (process praise) and diverge (the role of challenge and difficulty), and ask what an integrated account would look like.

> > [!topic-idea] [[Gamification in Educational Contexts]]
> > Gamification is the application of game-design elements (points, badges, leaderboards, progress bars, challenges) to non-game contexts, typically with the goal of increasing engagement. CET predicts that many common gamification elements introduce controlling functional significance and will undermine intrinsic motivation in the medium-to-long term, even if they increase short-term engagement. But the picture is more complicated: some game elements (challenge structures, mastery feedback, narrative framing) may activate the informational pathway.
