# Demand-Architecture Isomorphism

> [!definition] **Demand-Architecture Isomorphism** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> The principle that the level of cognitive architecture engaged in processing a text or task is isomorphic to the level required to satisfy the task's demand structure. Under this principle, a task requiring textbase-level response produces textbase-level encoding regardless of effort; a task requiring situation model construction forces that level of encoding.
> 
> **Boundary:** This principle operates at the level of task design, not individual learner effort. Additional effort on a textbase-level task does not produce situation model encoding; only restructuring the demand does.
> 
> **See also:** [[Levels-of-Processing-Theory]], [[Situation-Model]], [[Deep-Processing]]

*Source: this report*

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 1 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is elaborative interrogation, and what is the specific question format it uses?

**A:** Elaborative interrogation is a study strategy in which the learner generates answers to "why" questions about factual material being studied — specifically, "Why is it true that [fact]?" The defining feature is the causal-explanatory demand, which distinguishes EI from other question-asking strategies that use "what" or "when" formats.

**Source:** Phase I / Lexicon A

**Difficulty:** Basic

**Type:** Definition

**Tags:** #elaborative-interrogation, #reading-strategies, #learning-science

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 2 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between a textbase and a situation model? Give a concrete reading example.

**A:** A textbase is the propositional representation of what the text asserts, stripped of exact wording — it supports verbatim recall and close-paraphrase recognition. A situation model is the integrated representation of the subject matter itself, constructed by fusing textbase propositions with prior knowledge schemas — it supports inference, transfer, and novel application. Example: a reader who can recall that "photosynthesis converts light into glucose" has a textbase; one who can explain why plants use glucose as an energy carrier rather than storing ATP directly has a situation model.

**Source:** Phase II

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Distinction

**Tags:** #situation-model, #textbase, #text-comprehension, #van-dijk-kintsch

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 3 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Comprehension Gateway Mechanism, and what are its three structural components?

**A:** The Comprehension Gateway Mechanism is the process by which EI produces comprehension benefits: a Register 2 "why" question makes textbase-level retrieval insufficient, forcing situation model construction. Its three components are: (1) Demand-Exceeds-Textbase Structure — the question cannot be answered from the text; (2) Integration Anchor Requirement — construction requires connecting text propositions to prior knowledge schemas; (3) Construction Premium — generating the connection produces richer encoding than receiving it.

**Source:** Phase VI, Synthesis

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Process

**Tags:** #comprehension-gateway, #elaborative-interrogation, #situation-model, #original-construct

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 4 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** Why does prior knowledge moderate elaborative interrogation effectiveness, and is this moderation peripheral or structural?

**A:** Prior knowledge moderation is structural, not peripheral. Situation model construction — the process EI invokes — requires prior knowledge schemas as integration anchors: points of connection to which new text propositions can be attached within a causal-relational framework. A learner without relevant prior knowledge cannot execute the gateway mechanism because they lack the integration anchors that construction requires. This is why EI effects collapse (not merely diminish) when prior knowledge is absent, and why providing background reading before EI instruction partially restores EI benefits.

**Source:** Phases III-IV

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Connection

**Tags:** #prior-knowledge, #elaborative-interrogation, #integration-anchor, #situation-model

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 5 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Dual Register Diagnostic, and how is a Register 1 question distinguished from a Register 2 question?

**A:** The Dual Register Diagnostic classifies EI questions by their answer level: Register 1 questions can be answered by someone who knows only the text (textbase-satisfiable); Register 2 questions require world knowledge not in the text (situation model-requiring). The diagnostic test is: "Could a reader with only this text, and no other knowledge, answer this question?" If yes: Register 1. If no: Register 2. Register 1 questions do not function as comprehension gateways and produce weak EI effects.

**Source:** Phase IV, Section 4.2

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Distinction

**Tags:** #dual-register, #elaborative-interrogation, #question-design, #original-construct

> **Q:** Seed 6 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the generation advantage in EI, and why does it specifically arise in elaborative interrogation rather than from the generic generation effect alone?

**A:** The generation advantage is the finding that self-produced elaborations outperform experimenter-provided elaborations in EI. While the generic generation effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) contributes, EI's generation advantage also includes a specific construction premium: generating a causal connection requires actively traversing the conceptual path from text proposition to prior knowledge schema, reinforcing both the path and the endpoint. Provided elaborations allow recognition of a connection; generated elaborations require its construction — and the construction is the learning.

**Source:** Phases III and IV

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Process

**Tags:** #generation-effect, #elaborative-interrogation, #construction-premium

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 7 — Application
> **A:** **Q:** How should elaborative interrogation be integrated into PKB note-building practice, and what is the key design criterion for EI-style note prompts?

**A:** In PKB practice, EI should be applied by: (1) formulating Register 2 "why" questions as you read — ones whose answers require constructing causal connections, not locating sentences; (2) generating answers before consulting other sources; (3) writing notes at the situation model level ("X causes Y because [mechanism]") rather than the textbase level ("X causes Y"); (4) tagging unanswerable questions as prior knowledge gaps requiring background reading. The key design criterion for note prompts is the Dual Register Diagnostic: a prompt should be answerable only by constructing a causal-functional account, not by paraphrasing the source.

**Source:** Phase V, Best Practice callout

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Application

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 8 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Demand-Architecture Isomorphism, and what does it predict about the relationship between task design and learning depth?

**A:** The Demand-Architecture Isomorphism is the principle that the level of cognitive architecture engaged in processing is isomorphic to the level required to satisfy the task's demand structure. It predicts that: the level of understanding produced by a learning activity is determined by the minimum representational level needed to answer the task, not by the learner's subjective effort. A textbase-satisfiable question produces textbase encoding regardless of effort; a situation-model-requiring question forces situation model encoding. This implies that the most important instructional design decision is not "how difficult" but "what level of architecture does answering this demand?"

**Source:** Phase VI

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 9 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does elaborative interrogation connect to the desirable difficulties framework, and what specifically is "desirable" about EI's difficulty?

**A:** EI is a desirable difficulty (Bjork, 1994) because the cognitive demand it imposes is precisely the demand of situation model construction — the load that produces durable, transferable understanding. But EI's difficulty is not generically desirable; it is specifically targeted: the question format structurally blocks the textbase shortcut and requires exactly the kind of cognitive work (integration of text propositions with prior schemas) that produces rich encoding. The difficulty is desirable because it *is* the mechanism of the benefit — not an unfortunate cost of a useful technique but the productive struggle of construction itself.

**Source:** Phase IV, Section 4.4

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Connection

**Tags:** #desirable-difficulties, #elaborative-interrogation, #productive-struggle

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[elaborative-interrogation-reading-focused-analysis-2026-03-30]])*
> **Q:** Seed 10 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the expertise reversal effect for EI, and why does the Comprehension Gateway Mechanism predict it?

**A:** The expertise reversal effect for EI is the finding that EI benefit diminishes as learners become expert in a domain — eventually becoming redundant or counterproductive. The Comprehension Gateway Mechanism predicts this: experts already construct situation models automatically during reading because their prior knowledge schemas are rich and activated rapidly. The gateway mechanism is no longer needed — the door is already open. Requiring experts to generate elaborations for material they already have situation models for produces redundancy effects (wasted processing resources on unnecessary elaboration), not learning benefits.

**Source:** Phase V, Section 5.2

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Process

## References

- **Pressley, M., McDaniel, M. A., Turnure, J. E., Wood, E., & Ahmad, M. (1987). Generation and precision of elaboration: Effects on intentional and incidental learning. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 13(2), 291-300.**: This foundational study established the basic EI paradigm and demonstrated robust effects of why-question elaboration on both intentional and incidental memory. Critically introduces the manipulation of elaboration precision and the comparison of generated versus provided elaborations.

**Recommended Sections:** Experiments 1-3 for the core EI demonstration; Experiment 4 for the generation advantage.
- **Woloshyn, V. E., Pressley, M., & Schneider, W. (1992). Elaborative-interrogation and prior-knowledge effects on learning of facts. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 84(1), 115-124.**: The pivotal prior knowledge moderator study. Systematically manipulated learner background knowledge and demonstrated that EI's advantage over re-reading collapses when prior knowledge is absent. Essential reading for understanding the mechanism argument.

**Recommended Sections:** Experiments 1-3 for the prior knowledge manipulation; General Discussion for the theoretical interpretation.
- **Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 671-684.**: The theoretical foundation from which EI research emerged. Introduces the depth-of-processing framework and the distinction between maintenance and elaborative rehearsal. Essential for understanding why EI is usually described as a "deep processing" strategy — and why that description requires upgrading.

**Historical Significance:** One of the most cited papers in cognitive psychology; initiated decades of research on encoding processes and replaced the structural models of memory with a processing-oriented framework.
- **van Dijk, T. A., & Kintsch, W. (1983). *Strategies of discourse comprehension.* New York: Academic Press.**: The source of the three-level text representation model (surface form, textbase, situation model) that serves as the primary analytical framework in this report. The situation model / textbase distinction is the architectural foundation of the Comprehension Gateway Mechanism.

**Recommended Sections:** Chapters 3-5 for the theoretical architecture; Chapter 6 for the empirical grounding of the situation model construct.
- **Kintsch, W. (1998). *Comprehension: A paradigm for cognition.* Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**: The definitive statement of Kintsch's construction-integration model. Refines the three-level model extensively and provides the computational/process-level account of how situation models are constructed during reading.
- **Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4-58.**: The most comprehensive meta-analytic review of learning strategies. Rates EI as moderate utility, identifies the prior knowledge moderator as a key limitation, and situates EI within the broader strategy landscape. Essential for understanding EI's position relative to retrieval practice, distributed practice, and other high-utility strategies.

**Recommended Sections:** Section 3.2 on elaborative interrogation; the General Discussion for practical implications.
- **Slamecka, N. J., & Graf, P. (1978). The generation effect: Delineation of a phenomenon. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory*, 4(6), 592-604.**: The foundational generation effect paper. Demonstrates that self-generated items are remembered better than read items across a variety of paradigms. Provides the theoretical backdrop for the EI generation advantage discussed in Phase III.
- **Bjork, R. A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings. In J. Metcalfe & A. Shimamura (Eds.), *Metacognition: Knowing about knowing* (pp. 185-205). Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.**: The canonical statement of the desirable difficulties framework. Establishes that conditions that impair performance during acquisition often enhance long-term retention — the theoretical context in which EI's cognitive demand is understood as beneficial rather than merely taxing.
- **Wood, E., Pressley, M., & Winne, P. H. (1990). Elaborative interrogation effects on children's learning of factual content. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 82(4), 741-748.**: Extension of the EI research program to older children. Documents the generation advantage and provides evidence that EI's mechanism requires the active construction of causal connections, not merely the provision of additional information about the topic.
- **Martin, V. L., & Pressley, M. (1991). Elaborative-interrogation effects depend on the nature of the question. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 83(1), 113-119.**: Directly demonstrates that EI effect magnitude varies with question type — a finding that presages the Dual Register Diagnostic. Questions requiring genuine causal reasoning produce larger effects than questions that can be answered by paraphrase or text retrieval.
