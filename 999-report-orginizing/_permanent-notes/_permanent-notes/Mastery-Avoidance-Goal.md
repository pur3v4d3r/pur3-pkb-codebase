# Mastery-Avoidance Goal

> [!definition] **Mastery-Avoidance Goal** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> Oriented toward the negative possibility of intrapersonal incompetence: failing to learn, declining in ability, not living up to one's prior standard. The person asks "Am I avoiding regression?" This goal type is theoretically interesting but practically less common and somewhat harder to study. It may be common among experts, high-performing athletes, or older adults who are concerned with avoiding cognitive decline. It is associated with worry and exhaustion even when performance remains high.

A cornerstone reference for applications in instructional and classroom design. Relevant to Phases III and V.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*

Two studies using confirmatory factor analysis demonstrate the empirical distinctiveness of the four goal types and their differential relationships to cognitive and affective outcomes. The most influential recent theoretical paper in the field. Relevant to Phase III throughout.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*

An essential corrective to naive readings of the empirical literature and a key text for understanding the limitations discussed in Phase V.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> An influential classroom study documenting systematic differences in reported learning strategies between students in mastery-oriented versus performance-oriented classes. One of the clearest empirical demonstrations of the link between motivational climate and cognitive strategy use. Relevant to Phase IV and Phase V.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> Introduces the 3×2 model, which distinguishes task-based, self-based, and other-based standards of competence crossed with approach-avoidance valence to yield six goal types. Represents the current frontier of AGT theoretical development. Relevant as an expansion topic and as context for Phase III's discussion of theoretical evolution.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> A meta-analysis examining the relationship between competitive contexts, goal adoption, and performance. Relevant to the contested question of when performance-approach goals may be adaptive. Discussed in the limitations section of Phase V.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> The standard of competence a person is using to evaluate their performance determines the psychological meaning of effort, difficulty, and failure — and therefore determines the motivational consequences of those experiences. Achievement Goal Theory proposes that much of the variability in adaptive and maladaptive motivation in achievement contexts can be traced to which standard of competence is operative at a given moment.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The most robust finding for MAP goals is not their relationship to academic performance — which is positive but smaller than expected — but their relationship to deep processing strategies, curiosity, and elaborative encoding. Learners with strong MAP orientations are more likely to ask "why" questions, seek connections between concepts, engage in [[Retrieval-Practice]], and allocate effort to understanding rather than surface reproduction. This has led researchers to describe MAP goals as *process-oriented* rather than performance-oriented — a distinction with direct implications for long-term retention and transfer.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> What the evidence suggests, taken as a whole, is that PAV goals constitute a *threat-regulatory mode* that systematically hijacks cognitive resources away from learning and toward self-protective monitoring. This is not merely a correlation — the mechanisms are well-documented. Learners in PAV states show increased self-referential processing (monitoring whether they appear incompetent to others), reduced working memory capacity for task-relevant processing, heightened physiological arousal, and characteristic attribution patterns (attributing failure to stable, uncontrollable causes — ability — rather than unstable, controllable ones — effort or strategy). The uniformity of these effects is striking precisely because it persists even when the learner has sufficient ability to succeed. PAV goals are not simply what low-ability learners adopt; they are a motivational mode that suppresses performance below what ability would otherwise predict.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The core of the controversy can be stated precisely. Harackiewicz et al. (1997, 2002) found that PAP goals predicted course grades better than MAP goals in introductory psychology, suggesting that normative competition is motivationally potent under conditions of evaluative pressure. Midgley et al. (2001) challenged these findings on methodological and theoretical grounds, arguing that (1) MAP goals predict more important long-term outcomes than PAP goals even when the reverse is true for near-term grades, and (2) the finding reflects assessment artifacts (grades reward relative standing, so performance goals activate relevant strategies) rather than genuine motivational superiority. A subsequent meta-analysis (Hulleman et al., 2010) partially resolved the controversy by demonstrating that the PAP-performance association is stronger under conditions of evaluative threat, high-stakes testing, and ability-focused classroom environments — precisely the conditions that activate normative comparison.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The contested status of PAP goals — beneficial for some outcomes in some conditions, harmful in others — reveals something important: PAP goals sit at the intersection of approach-mode motivation (which is generally adaptive) and normative competence evaluation (which is context-dependent in its consequences). The approach component of PAP goals confers genuine benefits: increased effort, sustained engagement, strategic behavior. But the performance component introduces context sensitivity: whether those benefits translate to learning, long-term retention, and intrinsic motivation depends on whether the learning environment allows approach regulation to operate without triggering the threat responses characteristic of PAV goals. High-stakes, zero-sum evaluation environments tend to convert the approach orientation of PAP goals into avoidance dynamics over time — a process Elliot and colleagues have called *goal adoption instability*.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> Ryan and Pintrich (1997) demonstrated that PAV goals were negatively associated with adaptive help-seeking (asking for the understanding needed to master the material) while being positively associated with avoidant help-seeking (avoiding situations where help might be needed, or asking for answers rather than explanation). MAP goals showed the reverse pattern: positive association with adaptive help-seeking. This mechanism has direct instructional implications: classrooms structured to activate PAV goals — through public comparative evaluation, normative grading curves, or ability tracking — may systematically suppress the very behaviour (asking questions, seeking clarification, exposing confusion) that enables learning.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> What the evidence suggests about the stability-malleability question is a *malleable infrastructure* model: individuals have dispositional tendencies toward particular goal orientations that are shaped by prior achievement history, implicit theories of intelligence, and parenting and teaching style — but these dispositions are continuously updated by environmental signals. This explains both the individual difference evidence (people differ predictably in their baseline orientations) and the motivational climate evidence (environments powerfully shift goal distributions). The practical implication: both individual-level intervention (e.g., growth mindset interventions that shift implicit theories of intelligence) and environment-level intervention (motivational climate design) are valid levers, and they operate through partially independent pathways.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The foundational theoretical paper establishing the distinction between task and ego involvement and the differentiated ability concept. Essential for understanding the historical preconditions of the 2×2 framework. Recommended in full — Nicholls' theoretical exposition is unusually clear. Primary source for Phase II.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> There is something philosophically peculiar about the differentiated ability concept that Nicholls' framework only partially surfaces. Once a learner has differentiated effort from ability, they have acquired a cognitive tool that makes the motivational landscape more dangerous. The very cognitive sophistication that enables strategic learning — understanding that effort produces learning — also enables the self-handicapping inference: effortful success implies lower ability. This is not irrationality. It is the correct application of a valid inference rule to a socially charged domain. What makes ego involvement psychologically toxic is not cognitive deficiency but the over-application of a legitimate inference pattern to a domain (the demonstration of ability to others) that becomes the primary metric of self-worth. The trap, in other words, is epistemological before it is motivational.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> There is a deep irony embedded in PAV goal dynamics that the literature has not fully theorized. PAV goals are adopted precisely because the learner cares intensely about demonstrating competence — they wish to avoid the inference of incompetence by others. But the motivational and cognitive consequences of PAV adoption (surface processing, defensive help-seeking avoidance, reduced engagement with challenging material) systematically reduce the development of genuine competence over time. The goal adopted to protect the appearance of competence undermines the reality of it. This self-undermining dynamic is structurally similar to what [[Carol-Dweck|Dweck]] identified in the helpless response pattern but operates through a goal-mediation mechanism rather than an implicit theory mechanism. The convergence of these independent theoretical routes on the same self-undermining structure is analytically significant.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The existence and profile of MAV goals provides a natural experiment on what happens when avoidance regulation is directed at a self-referenced standard rather than a normative one. The finding that MAV goals reduce the severity of maladaptive outcomes compared to PAV goals — while still producing more anxiety and lower intrinsic interest than MAP goals — suggests that the *standard* dimension modulates the intensity of avoidance-mode pathology. Comparing MAV to an anxious self-critic perfecting their craft to protect a self-image of excellence. This is less socially threatening than PAV, but cognitively and emotionally expensive in ways that MAP is not. The MAV evidence thus supports the claim that avoidance regulation *per se* carries cognitive and affective costs, independent of whether the comparison standard is internal or external.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The narrowing of attentional scope under avoidance motivation is the most parsimonious explanation for the PAV-deep processing relationship. Deep processing requires sustained, flexible attention to complex material — following an argument, integrating new information with prior knowledge, generating inferences. This requires attentional resources that avoidance motivation systematically redirects toward threat monitoring. The consequence is not reduced effort but *misdirected effort* — effortful attention deployed on self-protective monitoring rather than epistemic exploration. This explains why PAV learners often report high effort while showing shallow processing: effort is real, but its target is wrong. It also explains why the PAV-performance relationship is worse than the effort investment would predict.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> A powerful way to understand the approach-avoidance asymmetry in failure attribution is as a *learning resource differential*. For MAP-oriented learners, failure is a resource — it contains information about the gap between current performance and mastery that can be used to improve strategy, allocate effort, and seek assistance. For PAV-oriented learners, failure is a threat to be managed — and threat management (defensive attribution, self-handicapping, withdrawal from challenging tasks) consumes cognitive and motivational resources that would otherwise be available for learning. The asymmetry is not about the amount of effort invested but about whether that effort is directed toward learning or toward self-protection. Over time, this resource differential compounds: MAP learners accumulate genuinely useful failure experience; PAV learners accumulate defensive responses that become increasingly reflexive.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> A theoretically important but empirically contested claim in achievement goal research is that PAP goals are unstable under competitive pressure — that they tend to convert toward PAV dynamics when normative comparison becomes threatening. Elliot and colleagues have provided experimental evidence for this conversion process. Critics (notably Harackiewicz and collaborators) have argued that the evidence for conversion is limited and that PAP goals can be held stably over time without generating PAV-like consequences. The current state of evidence suggests a conditional claim: goal conversion is more likely in populations with low perceived competence, in high-stakes competitive environments, and when PAP goal strength is matched by strong normative threat. The implication for educational practice is that the adaptive value of PAP goals is competence-contingent in a way that MAP goals are not — an asymmetry that is itself revealing about the fundamental difference between approach and avoidance as regulatory modes.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The contested status of PAP goals makes theoretical sense if we understand them as *approach motivation operating under a precarious evaluative standard*. The approach component — moving toward positive competence — confers genuine advantages. But the normative standard introduces fragility: it depends on the performance of others (uncontrollable), on evaluative judgments (external), and on zero-sum distributions of relative standing. MAP goals share the approach orientation but operate under a standard (self-referenced mastery) that is more robustly under the learner's control. The contrast illuminates why approach-avoidance is more fundamental than mastery-performance: it is the approach orientation, not the mastery standard, that drives the core adaptive benefits of MAP goals. PAP goals capture the approach orientation but apply it to a more fragile standard, producing context-dependent outcomes. The question is not whether to approach, but what to approach.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> It is tempting to treat achievement goals and self-regulatory strategies as independent — one motivational, one cognitive. The evidence resists this separation. Achievement goals do not merely provide the energy for self-regulation; they shape the cognitive processes that constitute it. A learner with MAP goals who monitors their comprehension and encounters a failure signal (I don't understand this) treats that signal as information to act on — the appropriate adaptive response. A learner with PAV goals who receives the same comprehension failure signal faces a motivational conflict: acting on it (seeking clarification, re-reading, asking for help) risks displaying incompetence. The goal type determines whether cognitive monitoring signals are processed as learning information or as social threat — and this determination happens upstream of the strategic response. Achievement goals, in this analysis, are the motivational infrastructure that determines whether the cognitive architecture of SRL can function adaptively.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10]])*
> Feedback that emphasises the learner's developing understanding ("You've clearly grasped the underlying principle here, and your next step might be to test this understanding by applying it to a novel problem") is more mastery-consistent than feedback that emphasises rank or comparison ("You scored in the top quartile"). This does not mean eliminating grading — it means attending carefully to what the *language* of evaluation signals about what matters.

> A common misreading of AGT — one worth flagging explicitly — is to conclude that mastery goals are simply "good" and performance goals are simply "bad," and that the practical implication is to lecture students or athletes into adopting mastery goals. The evidence does not support this prescriptive conclusion, for several reasons. > [!example] **Application** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> The practical implication of motivational climate research is not simply "praise effort rather than ability" — though that is supported. It is that the *structural features* of evaluation systems, not just their interpersonal texture, determine goal adoption. Public ranking (even friendly leaderboards), grade normalization, and comparative feedback language (you scored in the top 20%) activate performance goal orientations. Mastery-structured alternatives include: criterion-referenced grading (absolute standards rather than relative standing), private performance feedback, error-tolerant practice environments, and explicit metacognitive scaffolding that frames mistakes as information rather than judgment. Critically, these are not merely affective adjustments — they restructure the informational meaning of performance in ways that change which goal type is rationally adopted.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> High-stakes, single-shot, comparative assessments maximally activate PAP and PAV goal orientations — precisely the combination most likely to produce PAV-like processing strategies for lower-confidence learners and goal conversion risk for higher-confidence ones. Low-stakes, iterative, criterion-referenced assessments with granular feedback on specific skills activate MAP orientations. The practical implication for [[Formative-Assessment]] design: frequent low-stakes assessments with mastery-referenced feedback are not just pedagogically sound (they support retrieval practice and calibration) — they are motivationally protective, reducing the environmental conditions that generate PAV goal adoption.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> In organizational feedback systems — performance reviews, 360-degree assessments, developmental feedback programmes — the approach-avoidance asymmetry predicts that feedback systems structured around avoiding failure (don't miss targets, don't fall below benchmark) will activate PAV-like regulatory dynamics in recipients, suppressing genuine learning from feedback and increasing defensive processing. Adaptive alternatives: structure feedback explicitly around learning and growth targets (approach); use criterion-referenced rather than comparative standards; separate developmental feedback from evaluative judgment; create low-stakes practice contexts where failure-signal processing is decoupled from reward and punishment consequences. The key adaptation from the educational domain: the *stakes decoupling* that enables mastery-approach orientation in educational contexts is achievable in organizational settings through structured developmental feedback distinct from formal performance evaluation.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> Perfectionist psychopathology — which reliably predicts anxiety, depression, and reduced performance quality — maps onto the MAV and PAV profiles. Maladaptive perfectionism is not high standards per se but avoidance motivation applied to self-referenced standards. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy and Compassionate Mind Training work through mechanisms that can be understood as converting avoidance-mode regulation toward approach-mode — defusing the self-threatening character of failure signals and reestablishing the conditions for exploratory engagement with challenging material. The transfer from achievement goal theory: the therapeutic target in perfectionism is not the standard (reduce expectations) but the regulatory mode (reduce avoidance activation in response to failure signals). This explains why purely cognitive challenging of perfectionist beliefs is often insufficient — it addresses the content of the goals without changing the regulatory mode in which they are held.

> [!flashcard] **Spaced Repetition** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> **Q:** Seed 8
> **A:** **Q:** Why does criterion-referenced grading support mastery-approach goal orientations while norm-referenced grading activates performance goal orientations?
> **A:** Criterion-referenced grading defines success against a fixed absolute standard — making competence evaluation self-referenced and controllable (the learner's performance determines their grade independently of others). Norm-referenced grading defines success relative to the performance of peers — making success normative, partially uncontrollable, and zero-sum. The normative structure activates the differentiated ability concept (my relative performance says something about my relative ability) and raises the motivational stakes of comparative failure, generating conditions conducive to PAP and PAV goal adoption.
> **Source:** Phase V, Assessment Design; Phase IV, Mechanisms
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Application









## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **John G. Nicholls (1940–1994)** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> **Core Contribution:** Developed the foundational distinction between task involvement and ego involvement in achievement motivation, and the concept of the differentiated ability concept as its developmental precondition. Established that the standard by which competence is evaluated — self-referenced vs. normative — determines the motivational dynamics of achievement behavior.
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Influenced by Harter's competence motivation theory and White's effectance motivation concept; parallel development with Dweck's work on achievement patterns; preceded and theoretically grounded Elliot's 2×2 extension.
> **Key Works:** *The competitive ethos and democratic education* (1989); "Achievement motivation: Conceptions of ability, subjective experience, task choice, and performance" (*Psychological Review*, 1984).
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** Nicholls' differentiated ability concept is the psychological foundation of the PAV dynamics analyzed in this report — it explains why normative comparison generates self-worth threat in ways that self-referenced standards do not.

> [!person] **Andrew Elliot (1964– )** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> **Core Contribution:** Extended the mastery-performance dichotomy by adding the approach-avoidance dimension, producing the 2×2 achievement goal framework (with McGregor, 2001). Developed the hierarchical model of approach-avoidance motivation connecting personality traits (BIS/BAS) to achievement goal adoption.
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Built directly on Nicholls; developed the 2×2 in dialogue with Harackiewicz (with whom he had a productive theoretical controversy) and Dweck (whose implicit theories model provides a dispositional pathway to goal adoption).
> **Key Works:** "A hierarchical model of approach and avoidance achievement motivation" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1997); "A 2×2 achievement goal framework" (with McGregor, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 2001).
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** Elliot is the primary architect of the analytical framework examined in this report. His hierarchical model grounds the approach-avoidance distinction in personality systems (BAS/BIS), providing mechanistic depth that purely descriptive frameworks lack.

> [!person] **Carol Dweck (1946– )** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> **Core Contribution:** Independently developed the mastery-performance distinction (originally framed as learning vs. performance goals) from a social-cognitive perspective focused on implicit theories of intelligence. Identified the helpless vs. mastery-oriented response patterns as the behavioral signatures of fixed vs. growth mindset.
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Developed parallel to but largely independently of Nicholls; converged with Elliot in recognizing the approach-avoidance distinction as critical to understanding achievement motivation.
> **Key Works:** *Self-theories: Their role in motivation, personality, and development* (1999); "Motivational processes affecting learning" (*American Psychologist*, 1986).
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** Dweck's framework provides the developmental and cognitive pathway through which implicit theories of intelligence generate characteristic goal orientations, grounding the approach-avoidance analysis in a broader theory of the self.




## Conceptual Tensions

> [!tension] **The Self-Protection Paradox in PAV Goals** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> There is a deep irony embedded in PAV goal dynamics that the literature has not fully theorized. PAV goals are adopted precisely because the learner cares intensely about demonstrating competence — they wish to avoid the inference of incompetence by others. But the motivational and cognitive consequences of PAV adoption (surface processing, defensive help-seeking avoidance, reduced engagement with challenging material) systematically reduce the development of genuine competence over time. The goal adopted to protect the appearance of competence undermines the reality of it. This self-undermining dynamic is structurally similar to what [[Carol-Dweck|Dweck]] identified in the helpless response pattern but operates through a goal-mediation mechanism rather than an implicit theory mechanism. The convergence of these independent theoretical routes on the same self-undermining structure is analytically significant.

> [!tension] **The Goal Conversion Hypothesis and Its Empirical Status** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> A theoretically important but empirically contested claim in achievement goal research is that PAP goals are unstable under competitive pressure — that they tend to convert toward PAV dynamics when normative comparison becomes threatening. Elliot and colleagues have provided experimental evidence for this conversion process. Critics (notably Harackiewicz and collaborators) have argued that the evidence for conversion is limited and that PAP goals can be held stably over time without generating PAV-like consequences. The current state of evidence suggests a conditional claim: goal conversion is more likely in populations with low perceived competence, in high-stakes competitive environments, and when PAP goal strength is matched by strong normative threat. The implication for educational practice is that the adaptive value of PAP goals is competence-contingent in a way that MAP goals are not — an asymmetry that is itself revealing about the fundamental difference between approach and avoidance as regulatory modes.

> [!tension] **The PAP Goals Controversy: Performance-Approach as Adaptive or Disruptive?** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> **Position A (Harackiewicz et al.):** PAP goals are adaptive in performance-oriented contexts and should not be discouraged. They predict academic achievement, sustained engagement, and motivational persistence in competitive academic environments. Discouraging normative competition removes motivationally potent cues that many students rely on for sustained effort.
> **Position B (Midgley, Elliot, and colleagues):** PAP goals predict near-term performance at the cost of long-term learning, deep processing, intrinsic motivation, and motivational stability. They are adaptive only in the narrow sense that they fit the current (maladaptive) structure of competitive academic environments. Endorsing PAP goals endorses the evaluative structure that generates them.
> **Current Evidence State:** Meta-analyses support a conditional claim: PAP goals predict performance on near-term evaluations (especially under normative grading) but do not predict deep learning, intrinsic interest, or long-term retention. The relationship is competence-contingent and context-moderated.
> **Why This Matters:** The resolution of this controversy has direct implications for assessment design and motivational climate engineering. If PAP goals are adaptive in some contexts, climate design should optimize PAP goal stability (reducing goal conversion risk). If they are inherently fragile, climate design should prioritize MAP goal activation even in competitive environments.
> **This Report's Stance:** PAP goals are approach-mode and thus inherently less maladaptive than PAV goals, but their adaptive value is bounded by the precariousness of the normative standard. The regulatory mode primacy thesis suggests that the approach component of PAP goals is the source of their benefits; the performance component is the source of their fragility. Disentangling these components is the most important unanswered question in contemporary AGT research.




## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **The MAV Goal Functional Profile** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> Mastery-avoidance goals remain the least studied cell in the 2×2. The open question is whether MAV goals constitute a genuinely distinct motivational category with characteristic adaptive functions (perfectionist motivation for sustained performance maintenance) or whether they are a variant of anxiety-driven motivation that happens to target a self-referenced standard. What longitudinal evidence exists suggests that MAV goals are associated with elevated anxiety and reduced intrinsic interest even when they sustain performance level — but the mechanisms are incompletely specified and the population in which they are most functional (expert performers concerned with skill maintenance) has not been adequately studied.


## Protocols & Methods

> [!protocol] **Protocol: Motivational Climate Audit for Learning Environments** *(from [[achievement-goal-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24]])*
> **Context:** Use when evaluating or designing any learning environment (course, programme, workshop, training) for its goal-activating structure.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Evaluate the evaluation structure.** Is grading criterion-referenced (absolute standard) or norm-referenced (relative standing)? Norm-referenced structures activate PAP and PAV goal orientations.
> 2. **Assess comparison salience.** Are student performances made public or easily visible to peers? Public ranking and leaderboard-style feedback elevate normative comparison salience, activating performance goal orientations.
> 3. **Examine failure consequences.** Are mistakes and errors treated as learning information (low stakes, informative feedback) or performance deficits (high stakes, evaluative feedback)? High-stakes error consequences activate avoidance regulation.
> 4. **Check for mastery cues.** Does the environment explicitly communicate that improvement over time is valued as much or more than absolute performance level? Are progress narratives (you have improved in X) part of the feedback vocabulary?
> 5. **Assess help-seeking signals.** Is asking for help, admitting confusion, or revealing knowledge gaps culturally safe in this environment? Environments where such behaviors are stigmatized will generate PAV-like processing even without explicit competitive evaluation.
> 
> **Success Criteria:** A mastery-structured climate scores: criterion-referenced evaluation, low comparison salience, low-stakes error treatment, explicit mastery cues, and psychologically safe help-seeking.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** Well-intentioned "effort praise" without structural changes often fails because the evaluative structure (norm-referenced grading, public ranking) continues to activate performance goal orientations regardless of the interpersonal feedback style.
