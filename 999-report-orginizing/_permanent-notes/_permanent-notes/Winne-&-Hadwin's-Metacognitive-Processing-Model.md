# Winne & Hadwin's Metacognitive Processing Model

> [!definition] **Winne & Hadwin's Metacognitive Processing Model**
> **Core claim**: Self-regulated learning is fundamentally a cognitive event — specifically, an event of metacognitive monitoring and control operating on information-processing tasks. Learners build internal cognitive conditions (schemas, beliefs, prior knowledge) and external cognitive conditions (notes, resources, environmental structures) that they then deploy in tasks through operations. Metacognitive monitoring generates traces (cognitive products) that serve as feedback for calibration and strategy adjustment. Regulation, on this view, is essentially the management of *cognitive conditions, operations, products, and evaluations* (COPES) — a recursive information-processing cycle.
> 
> **How it works**: The COPES model specifies that self-regulation involves managing four categories of conditions (cognitive and contextual prerequisites), four types of operations (searching, monitoring, assembling, rehearsing), products (the outcomes of cognitive work), and evaluations (comparisons of products against standards). Critically, Winne & Hadwin distinguish *four phases* of SRL: defining the task (comprehending what is required), setting goals and planning (developing a representation of desired outcomes), enacting tactics and strategies (executing the plan), and *metacognitively adapting* studying (updating internal standards based on monitored outcomes). Monitoring quality — the accuracy of learners' judgments about their own cognitive states — is theorised as the linchpin of adaptive regulation.
> 
> **Distinctive contribution**: Winne & Hadwin provide the most detailed cognitive account of *monitoring* in the SRL literature. Their concept of *calibration* — the accuracy of the relationship between monitoring outputs and actual performance — is the theoretical foundation for research on the [[Dunning-Kruger-Effect|Dunning-Kruger phenomenon]], [[Illusion-of-Knowing|illusions of knowing]], and [[Fluency-Illusion|fluency illusions]] in learning. By treating SRL as an information-processing event, they generate the most precise predictions about when regulation will succeed or fail based on the quality of metacognitive information.
> 
> **Evidence base**: Research on metacognitive calibration, trace methodology (using verbal protocols, eye-tracking, log files as evidence of cognitive processes), and computational modelling of SRL. The trace methodology has been influential in identifying the specific cognitive operations involved in regulation that self-report measures cannot capture.
> 
> **When it shines**: Winne & Hadwin's model is most useful when the question is about the *calibration quality* of a learner's metacognitive monitoring — whether they accurately know what they know and don't know, whether their strategy selection is appropriately calibrated to task demands.
> 
> **Known limitations**: The model is motivationally thin. It treats motivation essentially as a cognitive condition (beliefs about task value, self-efficacy) rather than as a distinct psychological system with its own needs-based architecture. This limits its explanatory power for motivation-based regulatory failures — cases where calibration is adequate but motivation quality undermines regulatory enactment.

*Source: Philip Winne & Allyson Hadwin (1998–2008)*
