# Mastery Goals

> [!definition] **Mastery Goals**
> Achievement goals focused on developing competence, deepening understanding, and improving skill relative to one's own prior performance. Success is defined by personal growth rather than normative comparison. Associated with deeper learning strategies, greater persistence, help-seeking (when needed), positive attitudes toward challenging tasks, and adaptive responses to failure.

*Source: Educational Psychology*

> Elliot and McGregor (2001) validated the 2×2 model across two studies using confirmatory factor analysis, demonstrating that the four goal types are empirically distinct (not merely conceptually separable) and have different patterns of relationships to cognitive strategies, exam anxiety, and academic performance. Their results showed: mastery-approach goals predicted deep processing and positive test affect; performance-approach goals predicted surface processing but also higher exam performance; performance-avoidance goals predicted surface processing, disorganisation, and test anxiety; mastery-avoidance goals predicted disorganisation and worry despite strong GPA association.

Achievement Goal Theory proposes that much of the variability in adaptive and maladaptive motivation in achievement contexts can be traced to which standard of competence is operative at a given moment.

> [!example] **Application** *(from achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10.md)*
> When designing learning tasks, include features that make growth visible and valued: open-ended components that reward elaborated thinking, opportunities for revision and improvement, problems that can be approached multiple ways, and explicit framing that positions difficulty as an expected and valuable part of learning rather than a signal of failure. Avoid designs that make ability rankings immediately obvious (e.g., public performance boards, visibly stratified task sheets) unless the competitive signal is intentional and the context supports it.

> [!example] **Application** *(from achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10.md)*
> Feedback that emphasises the learner's developing understanding ("You've clearly grasped the underlying principle here, and your next step might be to test this understanding by applying it to a novel problem") is more mastery-consistent than feedback that emphasises rank or comparison ("You scored in the top quartile"). This does not mean eliminating grading — it means attending carefully to what the *language* of evaluation signals about what matters.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from achievement-goal-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-10.md)*
> A common misreading of AGT — one worth flagging explicitly — is to conclude that mastery goals are simply "good" and performance goals are simply "bad," and that the practical implication is to lecture students or athletes into adopting mastery goals. The evidence does not support this prescriptive conclusion, for several reasons. First, the relationship between goal orientation and outcomes is moderated by multiple factors including competence beliefs, task type, and cultural context. Second, simply telling people to adopt mastery goals rarely works — goal orientation is shaped more strongly by environmental structures than by explicit exhortation. Third, there are genuine motivational functions served by performance goals in certain contexts. The appropriate practical response to AGT is to design environments that support mastery orientation, not to diagnose and correct individuals who are responding rationally to their environment.
