# Double-Loop Learning

> [!definition] **Double-Loop Learning**
> A learning process distinguished from [[Single-Loop-Learning|Single-Loop Learning]] by its scope of correction. Single-loop learning corrects errors *within* an existing framework of assumptions — it adjusts behavior to better achieve existing goals. Double-loop learning questions and revises the framework itself — it asks whether the goals are correct, whether the assumptions underlying the strategy are valid, and whether the system's governing values are appropriate. In PKB terms: single-loop feedback says "this note format is not working; adjust the format." Double-loop feedback says "my entire approach to capturing information may be producing the wrong kind of knowledge; reconsider the approach."

> Multiple validation studies have found that SDLRS scores correlate positively with academic achievement, professional performance, and willingness to engage in continuing education. However, the factor structure underlying the 58-item instrument has not been consistently replicated, leading some researchers to question whether it is measuring a unitary construct or a heterogeneous collection of loosely related attributes. > [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from self-directed-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Self-Directed Learning is not merely a process variant on conventional teaching — it reflects a constructivist epistemology that treats knowledge as actively built by the learner rather than transmitted from instructor to student. The learner's role in diagnosing needs, selecting resources, and evaluating outcomes is not administrative but constitutive: these activities shape the knowledge that is constructed. This is why SDL cannot be reduced to "doing school independently." It involves a fundamentally different relationship between learner, knowledge, and context.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from self-directed-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Process and person are logically independent. SDL-structured environments do not automatically produce self-directed learners, and self-directed learners can learn effectively in non-SDL environments. This means that designing an SDL environment is not the same as developing learner autonomy, and measuring learner self-directedness is not the same as measuring SDL process engagement. Conflating the two produces interventions that fail and research that produces apparently contradictory findings.

> [!example] **Application** *(from self-directed-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Drawing from SDL theory and empirical research on learning environment design, several instructional design principles have consistent support: (1) make learning goals visible and negotiable — learners who understand why they are learning something and have some say in how they pursue it show stronger engagement and deeper processing; (2) build in structured reflection checkpoints — asking learners to regularly review what they have understood, what questions remain, and what they plan to do next activates metacognitive monitoring in learners who would not engage in it spontaneously; (3) scaffold resource evaluation skills — self-directed learners need to be able to identify and evaluate resources, not merely access them; (4) create graduated autonomy — reducing instructional scaffolding progressively as learner competence and readiness develop, rather than either maintaining maximal structure or withdrawing support abruptly; (5) prioritise mastery feedback over evaluative feedback — feedback that tells learners what they understand and don't understand is more useful for SDL than feedback that ranks or grades their performance.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from self-directed-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> The vast majority of SDL research — including the foundational studies on which the major theoretical frameworks rest — has been conducted with Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) samples. SDLRS validation studies have been conducted primarily with adult learners in North American continuing education settings; learning projects research drew on Canadian urban adults; online SDL research has focused heavily on university students in Western contexts. The generalisability of the frameworks and findings to learners in other cultural contexts, with different educational histories, in lower-resource environments, or with different relationships to institutional authority is uncertain and under-researched. This is a genuine limitation, not a caveat to be bracketed.
