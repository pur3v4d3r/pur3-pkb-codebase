# Extended Mind Theory — Philosophy of Mind

> [!definition] **Extended Mind Theory — Philosophy of Mind**
> The philosophical thesis that cognition is not bounded by skull and skin but can extend into the environment when external objects or systems satisfy the *parity principle*: if a process, were it occurring inside the skull, would be recognized as cognitive, then it should count as cognitive when implemented externally. Clark and Chalmers' canonical example is Otto's notebook — a man with memory impairment whose notebook functions as an external memory system. Their argument: Otto's notebook is functionally equivalent to biological memory, constituting part of his cognitive system. PKBs, under this theory, are not merely repositories *of* cognitive products — they are components *of* a distributed cognitive system.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from extended-mind-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Hutchins's studies of ship navigation demonstrated that: (a) no single crew member possesses all the knowledge required to navigate the vessel; (b) cognitive work is achieved through the coordinated interaction of crew members, physical artefacts (charts, plotting tools, alidades), and encoded procedures; (c) errors at the system level often arise not from individual incompetence but from breakdowns in the distribution of cognitive labour across the system; (d) the physical arrangement of the work environment encodes knowledge in a form that shapes and scaffolds individual cognition. These findings support the view that cognitive analysis must take the entire sociotechnical system as its unit of analysis.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from extended-mind-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> If a piece of information (a) is reliably accessible, (b) is automatically endorsed when accessed, (c) plays the same functional role in guiding behaviour as a biological memory trace would, then there is no principled reason to deny that it constitutes a belief — regardless of whether it is stored in neurons or in a notebook. The location of storage is cognitively irrelevant; the functional role is what counts.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from extended-mind-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Embedded cognition claims the mind exploits environmental structure; Extended Mind Theory claims the mind *incorporates* environmental structure. On the embedded view, removing the environment impairs cognitive performance but does not alter the boundary of the cognitive system. On the extended view, removing the environmental component is like removing part of the brain — it genuinely diminishes the system, not merely its resources. This distinction has real consequences for how we think about cognitive dependency, disability, and the design of learning environments.

> [!example] **Application** *(from extended-mind-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Drawing on extended mind theory, effective learning environment design should consider: (a) providing well-organised, reliably accessible external representations that can be trusted and automatically endorsed rather than requiring constant verification; (b) designing the spatial and material structure of the learning environment to encode relationships and procedures in accessible form; (c) scaffolding the construction of individual external knowledge systems (notes, diagrams, concept maps) as genuine cognitive extensions, not merely study aids; (d) attending to the conditions under which scaffolding can be faded without impoverishing the learner's extended cognitive system.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from extended-mind-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11.md)*
> Traditional "closed-book" assessments create artificial conditions in which learners must perform without the extended cognitive systems they habitually rely on. Extended mind theory suggests this may systematically underestimate learner competence — especially for individuals (like those with memory impairments, learning differences, or cognitive disabilities) whose extended cognitive systems are particularly important to their functioning. This is not an argument against all forms of closed-book assessment, but it is an argument for clarity about what such assessments are actually measuring.
