# PRACTICE PROBLEM (Easy) — FRISCO on a screen-time op-ed

> [!info] How to Use This Practice Problem
> Use the [[_master-frisco-quick-analysis-v1.0.0|FRISCO quick-analysis template]]. Budget **~10 minutes** total. The goal is **rapid triage**, not exhaustive analysis. If you find yourself spending >3 minutes on any single FRISCO letter, you are overrunning — that's a signal to use [[Paul-Elder Framework|Paul-Elder]] instead.

---

## 🎯 The Object of Analysis

> "Children today spend an average of 7 hours per day on screens. This screen time is rewiring their brains and destroying their ability to focus. We know this is true because attention-deficit diagnoses have tripled since smartphones became common. Parents must drastically cut their children's screen time or risk raising a generation incapable of deep thought."

## 🧭 Hints

> [!tip]- Hint 1 — Reasons → Inference
> Reason 1 ("kids spend 7 hours") is descriptive. Reason 2 ("ADHD diagnoses tripled") is correlational. The conclusion ("screens are causing this and you must cut") is causal and prescriptive. The inference chain leaps from correlation to causation without ruling out alternative explanations (changes in diagnostic criteria, awareness, screening rates).

> [!tip]- Hint 2 — Clarity
> "Screen time" is undefined. Is a video call with grandparents the same as autoplay TikTok? "Deep thought" is undefined. "Rewiring their brains" is true of *every* experience — the brain rewires from reading too. Each undefined term hides a weakness.

## ✅ Solution Sketch

> [!example]- Solution Sketch — Key Analytical Moves
>
> **F:** Screen time is harming children's attention; parents must drastically cut it.
>
> **R:**
> 1. Kids average ~7 hr/day of screens.
> 2. ADHD diagnoses have tripled since smartphones.
> 3. (Implicit) Therefore screens cause the attention damage.
> 4. (Prescriptive) Therefore parents must cut screen time drastically.
>
> **I (inductive/causal):** Fails. The argument commits:
> - **[[Correlation vs Causation]]** — diagnosis-rate change has multiple plausible causes (DSM-5 criteria shifts, increased awareness, school referral rates, telehealth access).
> - **[[Hasty Generalization]]** — treats "screen time" as a monolithic variable.
> - **[[Slippery Slope]]** (implicit) — "incapable of deep thought" is an unsupported endpoint.
>
> **S:** Parenting-anxiety media ecology; the claim performs well rhetorically because it activates protective instinct. Source ecology often includes book-promoting authors and digital-detox-industry commercial interests.
>
> **C:** "Screen time" (passive video ≠ video calls ≠ active creation); "rewiring" (literally true of all experience); "deep thought" (undefined); "drastically cut" (by how much, to what level).
>
> **O — Verdict: `questionable`.** The underlying empirical question (does heavy passive screen time correlate with attention difficulties in some children?) has some research support, but the argument as stated overgeneralizes, conflates correlation with causation, lumps heterogeneous activities into one variable, and prescribes a strong intervention without supporting it. A better-formed version would distinguish screen *types*, specify dose-response, and acknowledge confounders.
>
> **Metacognitive check:** Easy to dismiss because the rhetoric is heavy-handed; the *risk* is dismissing the underlying empirical question along with the bad argument. Separate bad-argument-for-claim from claim-itself.
>
> **Expected time:** 8-10 minutes. If you spent 20, you over-analyzed — try the harder problem ([[PP-04_frisco_minimum-wage-economic-claim|PP-04]]).
