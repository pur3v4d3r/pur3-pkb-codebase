# PRACTICE PROBLEM (Hard) — FRISCO on a minimum-wage argument

> [!info] How to Use This Practice Problem
> This is **FRISCO at its difficulty ceiling**. The argument is multi-premise, contains some genuine economic reasoning, and is genuinely contested in the literature. The challenge is to do FRISCO without sliding into a position-defense. Budget **~15 minutes**. If you find yourself >20, the object is too complex for FRISCO — switch to [[Paul-Elder Framework]].

---

## 🎯 The Object of Analysis

> "Raising the minimum wage to $20/hour will hurt the very workers it claims to help. Basic economics tells us that when you raise the price of something, demand for it falls — and labor is no exception. Employers facing higher labor costs will respond in three ways: they will hire fewer workers, they will cut hours, or they will pass the costs on through higher prices that erode the real value of the wage increase. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in 2021 that a $15 federal minimum wage would eliminate 1.4 million jobs. A $20 minimum would be even worse. Compassion is not enough — sound policy requires understanding the economics, and the economics on this question are settled. Anyone supporting a $20 minimum wage is either ignorant of basic supply-and-demand or willing to harm low-wage workers in exchange for the appearance of helping them."

---

## 📝 Your Workspace

Apply F, R, I, S, C, O. Pay particular attention to:
- **R** — separate the *premises* from the *rhetorical add-ons* ("compassion is not enough," "ignorant of basic supply-and-demand").
- **I** — the inference rests on a textbook model. Is the model the right one? What are its assumptions?
- **C** — "settled," "basic economics," "the economics" — each does heavy rhetorical work.

---

## 🧭 Hints

> [!tip]- Hint 1 — The textbook model trap
> Standard supply-and-demand for a homogeneous good in a competitive market predicts that a price floor above equilibrium reduces quantity demanded. But the labor market is **not** a homogeneous-good competitive market. Empirical labor economics since [[Card and Krueger]] (1994) has found that the textbook prediction is empirically weaker than expected, especially for moderate increases — implying significant **monopsony** power and **search frictions**. The argument treats a contested empirical question as if textbook theory settled it.

> [!tip]- Hint 2 — The CBO citation
> The CBO 2021 estimate is real. It also included a *range* — 0 to 2.7 million job losses, with 1.4M as a midpoint. The same report estimated **27 million workers** would receive raises and **900,000 lifted out of poverty**. Citing only the job-loss midpoint and not the offsetting benefits is [[Cherry Picking]].

> [!tip]- Hint 3 — The ad hominem coda
> "Anyone supporting a $20 minimum wage is either ignorant or willing to harm…" is a classic [[False Dichotomy]] coupled to an [[Ad Hominem]] — it forecloses the possibility that informed people weigh trade-offs differently. The presence of this rhetorical move *near the end* of an argument is often a tell that the author knows the empirical case is weaker than the rhetoric.

## ✅ Solution Sketch

> [!example]- Solution Sketch — Key Analytical Moves
>
> **F:** A $20 minimum wage would harm low-wage workers more than it helps them; therefore it should not be enacted.
>
> **R:**
> 1. Textbook supply-and-demand predicts a price floor reduces quantity demanded.
> 2. Employers will respond via fewer hires, reduced hours, or higher prices.
> 3. CBO (2021) estimated 1.4M job losses from a $15 minimum.
> 4. A $20 minimum would have larger effects than $15.
> 5. (Rhetorical) Therefore anyone disagreeing is ignorant or malicious.
>
> **I (deductive-from-model + inductive-from-evidence):**
> Mixed. The deductive piece (premise 1) is a *conditional* result of a specific market model whose assumptions (homogeneous good, perfect competition, no monopsony, full information) the labor market does not satisfy. The inductive piece (premise 3) cites real evidence selectively — the same CBO report estimates substantial wage gains for ~27M workers. Premise 4 *extrapolates* beyond the data range; effect sizes at $20 are not linearly inferable from $15 estimates.
>
> Identifiable issues:
> - **[[Cherry Picking]]** of CBO findings (job loss cited; wage benefits and poverty reduction omitted).
> - **[[Hasty Generalization]]** from $15 estimate to $20.
> - **[[Appeal to Authority]]** ("basic economics tells us") — treats a contested model as settled science.
> - **[[Ad Hominem]] / [[False Dichotomy]]** in the final sentence.
> - **Omitted counter-evidence**: the [[Card and Krueger]] natural-experiment tradition and subsequent meta-analyses (e.g., [[Dube]], 2019) showing modest minimum-wage increases produce small disemployment effects relative to wage gains.
>
> **S:** Politically contested topic; the argument has commercial sponsors on both sides (low-margin service-industry trade associations vs. labor unions); the rhetorical structure (textbook claim + selectively cited CBO + ad hominem coda) is characteristic of think-tank policy memos rather than academic argument.
>
> **C:** "Basic economics" — which model? competitive equilibrium or monopsony? "Settled" — false; this is one of the most empirically contested questions in labor economics. "Will hurt workers it claims to help" — *net* harm requires summing job losses against wage gains; the argument never does the sum.
>
> **O — Verdict: `questionable`.** The argument contains real elements (a real CBO citation, a real textbook prediction) embedded in a structure that selectively cites evidence, treats a contested empirical question as settled, and closes with rhetoric that forecloses disagreement. A defensible version of the argument would say: "Minimum-wage increases involve genuine trade-offs between wage gains for some workers and disemployment effects on others; reasonable people disagree about how to weigh these, and the empirical literature shows real but contested effect sizes that depend on local labor-market structure." The actual argument suppresses the trade-off framing in favor of one-sided polemic.
>
> **Metacognitive check:**
> Whichever side of minimum-wage policy you favor, the *argument as written* fails on FRISCO grounds — and you have to be careful that your verdict is about the argument, not the policy. If you found the verdict "easy" because you disagree with the policy, redo the analysis on a structurally identical argument from the opposite political direction (e.g., a labor-union memo that selectively cites only the wage-gain numbers and dismisses opponents as "corporate shills") and check whether your verdict process is symmetric.
>
> **Expected time:** 15-20 minutes; expected difficulty: high. If you completed it in <10 minutes, you almost certainly skipped Situation or Clarity.
