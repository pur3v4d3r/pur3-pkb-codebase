# Cognitive Evaluation Theory — A Foundational Report

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Context Setting

One of the most consequential and counterintuitive findings in twentieth-century motivational psychology is that rewarding people for activities they already enjoy can — under specifiable and now well-understood conditions — systematically destroy their motivation to engage in those activities. This finding, which emerged from Edward Deci's early laboratory experiments in the 1970s and was subsequently given theoretical architecture through [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-cet]], challenged decades of behaviorist assumptions about the universally reinforcing function of rewards and redirected the study of motivation toward an examination of the qualitative experience of engagement itself.

[[cognitive-evaluation-theory-cet]] (CET) is a mini-theory within the macro-framework of [[self-determination-theory]] (SDT), developed by [[edward-deci]] and [[richard-ryan]] to explain how external events — including tangible rewards, verbal praise, feedback, surveillance, deadlines, and competitive structures — affect the quality and durability of [[Intrinsic Motivation]]. CET does not treat all external events as equivalent in their motivational consequences. Instead, it proposes that the motivational impact of any external event depends on the functional significance that the recipient ascribes to the event — specifically, whether the event is experienced as providing information about competence or as exerting control over behavior.

The theory's intellectual significance extends well beyond the laboratory manipulation of rewards. CET provides a conceptual vocabulary for analyzing the motivational ecology of schools, workplaces, families, clinical settings, and digital environments. It grounds a substantial research tradition linking autonomous motivation to superior learning outcomes, creative performance, persistence, and psychological well-being. It informs the design of feedback systems, incentive structures, parenting practices, and teacher behaviors. And it occupies a central node in the theoretical architecture of SDT, which has become the dominant framework for understanding human motivation across cultures, developmental stages, and life domains.

> [!ask-yourself-this]
> Consider an activity you currently pursue without external incentives — perhaps a hobby, a personal project, or a form of intellectual inquiry. How would you respond if someone began offering you significant monetary rewards for engaging in it? Would your motivation increase, remain stable, or would something qualitative shift in how you experienced the activity? CET offers a precise theoretical answer to this question, and that answer — while initially counterintuitive — has proven remarkably robust across fifty years of empirical investigation.

### Scope of This Report

This foundational report provides comprehensive treatment of Cognitive Evaluation Theory across its theoretical architecture, empirical foundations, mechanistic account, and practical implications. It covers CET's core constructs — [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], perceived competence, and the functional significance of external events — its seven formal propositions as articulated by Deci and Ryan, the reward contingency taxonomy through which different reward structures are predicted to have differential effects, the protracted empirical debate over the theory's validity, and its integration within the broader SDT framework and the landscape of motivational psychology.

What this report deliberately excludes: a comprehensive treatment of [[organismic-integration-theory]] (OIT), SDT's mini-theory covering the internalization of extrinsically motivated behavior — though CET and OIT represent complementary analyses and their relationship is addressed in Phase VI. Similarly, [[causality-orientations-theory]] and [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]], while foundational SDT components, are treated here only insofar as they illuminate CET's mechanisms.

### Navigational Map

The report unfolds across six analytical phases. **Phase II** establishes the conceptual foundations: intrinsic motivation, the perceived locus of causality construct, perceived competence, and the taxonomy of external events. **Phase III** maps the theoretical landscape, situating CET within competing motivational frameworks and surveying the empirical controversy the theory has generated. **Phase IV** examines mechanisms — how external events actually produce their effects through psychological mediation. **Phase V** analyzes applications and limitations. **Phase VI** synthesizes the whole and extracts the report's deepest analytical contribution. The Appendix provides the lexicon, key figures, conceptual tensions, references, spaced repetition seeds, expansion topics, and PKB connections.

## Phase II: Conceptual Foundations

### The Problem CET Was Designed to Solve

To understand [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-cet]], it is necessary to understand the theoretical problem it was constructed to address. By the time [[edward-deci]] began his experimental program in the early 1970s, motivational psychology was dominated by behaviorist assumptions. Reinforcement theory, drawing from Thorndike's law of effect and Skinner's operant conditioning program, held that behavior is a function of its consequences: behaviors followed by rewards increase in frequency, and the stronger or more consistent the reward, the more firmly the behavior is established. On this account, motivation is essentially synonymous with the external contingencies that regulate behavior — an assumption that leaves no conceptual room for the distinction between intrinsically and extrinsically motivated behavior, because all behavior is, in effect, extrinsically maintained.

The problem with this view was that it could not account for a category of behavior that was both empirically obvious and theoretically significant: behavior undertaken for its own sake, in the absence of any external contingency. People engage in exploration, play, artistic creation, and intellectual inquiry without any apparent external incentive, and often with high levels of sustained engagement, creativity, and performance quality. The concept of [[Intrinsic Motivation]] — the propensity to engage in activities because they are inherently interesting, enjoyable, or satisfying — had been proposed by Robert White (1959) under the rubric of "effectance motivation" and been taken up by cognitive theorists, but it lacked the theoretical precision needed to generate testable predictions.

> [!definition] **Intrinsic Motivation (White, 1959; Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> Intrinsic motivation refers to the propensity to engage in activities for their inherent interest, challenge, or enjoyment — that is, for the qualitative experience of the activity itself rather than for any separable outcome or external consequence. Intrinsically motivated behavior is engaged in freely, without external pressure or inducement, and is typically characterized by higher levels of curiosity, spontaneous exploration, creativity, and willingness to persist in the face of challenge. Intrinsic motivation is not a personality trait but a functional state that is both enabled and undermined by specific psychological and interpersonal conditions.
>
> **Boundary:** Intrinsic motivation must be distinguished from behavior that appears voluntary but is maintained by internalized external standards (introjected regulation), self-concept contingencies (ego involvement), or the anticipation of future extrinsic rewards. These involve different regulatory processes with different outcomes.
>
> **Research Status:** Intrinsic motivation has been operationalized primarily through free-choice behavior paradigms (time spent with an activity after experimental conditions are terminated) and self-report measures. Both operationalizations have been criticized, and their relationship is not always consistent. The construct's validity is nevertheless supported by extensive convergent evidence.
>
> **See also:** [[extrinsic-motivation]], [[Autonomous Motivation]], [[self-determination-theory]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[free-choice-behaviour]]

Deci's 1971 experiments provided the first controlled evidence that introducing tangible extrinsic rewards for engaging in an intrinsically interesting activity — specifically, monetary payment for solving SOMA cube puzzles — reduced subsequent intrinsic motivation as measured by free-choice behavior during an ostensibly unmonitored period. This finding, replicated in subsequent studies, generated what Deci called the "undermining effect" — and what Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973) independently labeled the [[overjustification-effect]], based on attribution-theoretic reasoning.

### The Perceived Locus of Causality

The first and arguably most foundational construct in CET is the [[perceived-locus-of-causality]] (PLOC), adapted from de Charms' (1968) distinction between "Origins" and "Pawns." An individual who perceives themselves as the origin of their behavior — who experiences their engagement as self-determined and internally caused — has an internal PLOC. An individual who perceives their behavior as caused by external forces, pressures, or contingencies — who experiences themselves as a pawn of extrinsic circumstances — has an external PLOC.

> [!definition] **Perceived Locus of Causality (de Charms, 1968; Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> The perceived locus of causality refers to the subjective sense of whether an action is self-determined (internally caused) or controlled by external factors (externally caused). An internal PLOC involves experiencing one's behavior as initiated and regulated by the self — as an expression of one's own values, interests, or choices. An external PLOC involves experiencing one's behavior as pressured, constrained, or induced by forces outside the self. The PLOC construct is explicitly perceptual and functional: it does not describe the actual causal chain but the agent's interpretive experience of that chain.
>
> **Boundary:** The PLOC is not equivalent to Rotter's [[locus-of-control]], which refers to beliefs about the causes of outcomes rather than the perceived cause of one's own behavior. The PLOC concerns agency over action; locus of control concerns the predictability of outcomes from action.
>
> **Etymology:** From Latin *locus* (place) + causal (pertaining to causation). The construct names the perceived "location" of the cause of one's behavior — inside (internal) or outside (external) the self.
>
> **Common Misconception:** It is frequently assumed that behavior is either intrinsically or extrinsically motivated in an all-or-nothing sense. The PLOC is better understood as a continuum — behavior can be experienced as more or less self-determined, and the same external event can shift the PLOC in either direction depending on the context and the individual.
>
> **See also:** [[autonomy-need]], [[Autonomous Motivation]], [[Controlled Motivation]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[External Regulation]]

CET proposes that when external events shift the perceived locus of causality from internal to external — when an individual begins to experience themselves as doing an activity because of the external event rather than because of their genuine interest — intrinsic motivation for that activity decreases. The mechanism is fundamentally interpretive: external events acquire their motivational valence through the meaning the agent assigns to them, not through their reinforcing or punishing properties per se. This was a direct challenge to the behaviorist framework, which had no theoretical construct equivalent to the PLOC.

### Perceived Competence

The second core construct in CET is perceived competence — the sense of being effective and capable in one's interactions with the environment. This construct draws from White's (1959) effectance motivation thesis, which proposed that organisms are intrinsically motivated to demonstrate competence and that this motivation energizes exploratory and mastery-seeking behavior. For CET, perceived competence operates primarily through its satisfaction of the fundamental psychological need for [[competence-need|competence]], which is one of the three basic psychological needs posited by [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]].

> [!definition] **Perceived Competence (White, 1959; Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> Perceived competence refers to the sense that one is capable of producing desired outcomes and avoiding undesired ones within a given domain — the experience of effectiveness and mastery in interaction with the environment. Within CET, perceived competence is a mediating variable: external events that convey positive information about one's capacities (competence-affirming events) tend to enhance intrinsic motivation, while events that convey negative information (competence-disconfirming events) or that undermine the sense of effectiveness tend to diminish it. Critically, the competence-enhancing effect of positive feedback is contingent upon the contextual frame being autonomy-supportive rather than controlling.
>
> **Boundary:** Perceived competence in the CET framework must be distinguished from general [[self-efficacy]] (Bandura), which concerns confidence in one's capacity to execute specific behaviors toward specific outcomes. Perceived competence is a more diffuse satisfaction of a basic psychological need; self-efficacy is domain-specific and outcome-referenced.
>
> **See also:** [[competence-need]], [[self-efficacy]], [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]], [[perceived-competence]], [[mastery-goal]]

### The Functional Significance of External Events

The most theoretically sophisticated and practically important construct in CET is the concept of functional significance. CET proposes that external events can serve three distinct functions with respect to intrinsic motivation, and that the motivational consequences of any event depend on which function the individual perceives it to be serving.

> [!definition] **Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> The functional significance of an external event refers to its perceived motivational role — the function the agent interprets the event as serving in relation to their behavior. CET identifies three functional aspects: (1) the *informational* aspect, in which the event conveys competence-relevant information that supports the autonomy of the individual; (2) the *controlling* aspect, in which the event applies pressure toward specific outcomes or behavioral compliance, thereby shifting the perceived locus of causality toward external; and (3) the *amotivating* aspect, in which the event conveys incompetence or signals the impossibility of effective action. Informational events tend to enhance intrinsic motivation; controlling events tend to undermine it; amotivating events tend to produce a collapse of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.
>
> **Boundary:** Functional significance is not an objective property of events but a product of the individual's interpretive process. The same event — say, a competitive ranking — can serve a primarily informational function for one person (confirming their mastery) and a primarily controlling function for another (exerting pressure to maintain status). Context, individual differences in [[causality-orientations-theory|causality orientations]], and the interpersonal climate of the event's delivery all influence the functional significance that is perceived.
>
> **See also:** [[reward-contingency-types]], [[ego-involvement]], [[autonomy-support]], [[controlling-environment]], [[autonomy-supportive-environment]]

The informational/controlling distinction is the conceptual engine at the heart of CET. A verbally delivered positive evaluation is primarily informational — it conveys that one performed well, enhancing perceived competence within a context that preserves the sense of self-determination. A promised tangible reward contingent on completing a task is primarily controlling — it transforms the locus of causality from internal to external, and the activity is now understood as something one does *in order to* obtain the reward rather than for its inherent interest. The reward functions as a superordinate cause that retroactively reframes the activity's motivational basis.

> [!key-claim]
> **The Central Claim of CET**: The motivational impact of an external event is not determined by its objective properties (tangible vs. verbal, large vs. small, expected vs. unexpected) but by the functional significance the individual assigns to it — specifically, whether the event is experienced as informational and autonomy-supportive or as controlling and autonomy-undermining. The same event can have opposite effects depending on the interpretive frame the individual applies.

### The Seven Formal Propositions

Deci and Ryan (1985) formalized CET into seven propositions that structure its empirical predictions:

**Proposition 1**: External events can be characterized in terms of their functional significance — the informational, controlling, or amotivating aspects they convey. Intrinsic motivation is enhanced by informational events and undermined by controlling and amotivating events.

**Proposition 2**: The functional significance of external events is influenced by the interpersonal context in which they occur. Autonomy-supportive contexts increase the likelihood that events are parsed as informational; controlling contexts increase the likelihood that even informationally rich events are parsed as controlling.

**Proposition 3**: Intrapersonal events — including ego involvement, self-imposed deadlines, and internally competitive standards — operate functionally like external controlling events and can undermine intrinsic motivation through the same mechanism of perceived locus of causality shift.

**Proposition 4**: In addition to its effects on autonomy via the PLOC, perceived competence directly affects intrinsic motivation. Positive feedback that enhances perceived competence will, under autonomy-supportive conditions, increase intrinsic motivation.

**Proposition 5**: Rewards and other external events with a high degree of controlling salience — particularly expected tangible rewards, surveillance, deadlines, and imposed goals — are likely to undermine intrinsic motivation because they shift the PLOC externally.

**Proposition 6**: Choice, acknowledgment of feelings, and other autonomy-promoting contextual features counteract the controlling functional significance of expected rewards and can preserve intrinsic motivation even under incentive contingencies.

**Proposition 7**: Competition is a context with a high degree of controlling salience when it focuses individuals on winning (a performance-contingent external outcome) rather than personal mastery.

> [!reflection] **Phase II Reflective Questions**
> 1. How does the construct of "functional significance" challenge the standard assumption that rewards simply increase motivation? What does it require us to believe about how individuals process their own motivational states?
> 2. The distinction between perceived locus of causality and locus of control is technical but consequential. What practical difference would it make if a designer confused the two — for example, in designing a feedback system?
> 3. CET posits that even intrapersonal events (ego involvement) can undermine intrinsic motivation. What does this imply about the relationship between perfectionism, self-imposed standards, and the quality of engagement in a domain?

## Phase III: Theoretical Landscape

### Situating CET Within the SDT Family

[[cognitive-evaluation-theory-cet]] occupies a specific analytical niche within the SDT framework. SDT, as a macro-theory, comprises six interrelated mini-theories, each addressing a different facet of motivation and personality. CET is specifically concerned with the conditions under which [[Intrinsic Motivation]] is maintained, enhanced, or undermined by external events. It does not address — and explicitly declines to address — the full internalization spectrum for extrinsically motivated behavior, which is the province of [[organismic-integration-theory]] (OIT). The relationship between CET and OIT is complementary: CET explains what happens to intrinsic motivation when the environment introduces external events; OIT explains how initially external motivation can become progressively internalized and autonomous through a developmental process.

The other SDT mini-theories — [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]], [[causality-orientations-theory]], [[goal-contents-theory]], and Relationship Motivation Theory — each provide different lenses on the motivational landscape. CET draws most heavily on [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]]'s claim that [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] and [[competence-need|competence]] (along with relatedness) are universal psychological needs whose satisfaction is necessary for well-being and effective functioning. The needs for autonomy and competence are the specific psychological substrates through which CET's mechanisms operate: external events undermine intrinsic motivation primarily by frustrating autonomy (via the PLOC shift) and, in some cases, by undermining perceived competence.

> [!insight]
> CET can be understood as the micro-level analysis of the macro-level SDT claim about basic psychological needs. Where SDT-BPNT (Basic Psychological Needs Theory) asks "what are the needs and why do they matter?", CET asks "what happens to these needs — specifically autonomy and competence — when specific types of external events enter the environment?" CET thus provides the mechanism that BPNT presupposes but does not itself specify.

### The Historical Origins: Deci's Early Experiments

The empirical program that gave rise to CET originated with Deci's 1971 dissertation experiments at Carnegie Mellon University. In a paradigm that would become canonical in motivational research, participants in the experimental condition received monetary payment for solving SOMA cube puzzles during a second experimental session, while control participants received no payment. During a third session, when payment was discontinued for the experimental group, their free-choice time spent with the puzzles — measured during an ostensibly unmonitored period — was significantly lower than that of control participants who had never been paid. Deci interpreted this pattern as evidence that external monetary reward had shifted the perceived locus of causality from internal to external, reducing intrinsic motivation even after the reward was removed.

Simultaneously and independently, Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973) conducted the now-classic "expected reward" study with preschool children who enjoyed drawing. Children in the expected award condition who drew pictures to receive a "Good Player" certificate subsequently showed significantly less spontaneous drawing behavior in a naturalistic classroom setting. Their study introduced the term [[overjustification-effect]], derived from attribution theory: the child who receives an expected external reward for an enjoyable activity has an excess of justification for their engagement — both the intrinsic enjoyment and the extrinsic reward — and attribution principles predict that the more salient external cause (the reward) will be assigned causal priority, effectively "crowding out" the intrinsic cause.

> [!key-insight]
> **The Overjustification Effect as an Attribution Phenomenon**: The Lepper et al. (1973) framing draws on Kelley's attribution theory — specifically the discounting principle, which holds that the perceived role of one cause is diminished when other plausible causes are present. The salience of the external reward leads individuals to "underpercieve" their intrinsic interest as the cause, attributing their engagement primarily to the external incentive. Once the incentive is removed, the internal cause has been effectively extinguished from the person's self-understanding of their own motivation.

### The Reward Contingency Taxonomy

A critical theoretical refinement that emerged from both the Deci program and subsequent research was the recognition that not all rewards are equivalent in their predicted effects. The crucial variable is not the reward's magnitude or modality but its contingency structure — the relationship between the reward and the activity.

CET, as refined by Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999) in their landmark meta-analysis, distinguishes among:

**Task-noncontingent rewards**: Rewards delivered independent of task engagement (e.g., a flat fee for appearing in a study). These are predicted to have negligible effects on intrinsic motivation because they do not implicate the functional significance of the activity itself.

**Engagement-contingent rewards**: Rewards contingent on engaging with the task but not on how well it is performed (e.g., "You can earn a reward for working on these puzzles"). These are predicted to undermine intrinsic motivation because they transform engagement from a spontaneous act into an instrumental means toward an end. The [[engagement-contingent-reward]] is the paradigm case of the undermining effect.

**Completion-contingent rewards**: Rewards contingent on completing the task. These introduced an additional controlling dimension — the implicit pressure to complete rather than to explore — and showed similar undermining effects.

**Performance-contingent rewards**: Rewards contingent on performing well relative to a standard or other performers. These showed the most equivocal pattern: high positive-performance-contingent rewards carried an informational component (they confirmed competence), but were simultaneously embedded in a controlling contingency frame. The net effect depended on which functional aspect dominated the individual's interpretation.

**Unexpected rewards**: Rewards delivered after the activity, without advance knowledge. These consistently showed no undermining effect — a critical finding for the theory. The informational/controlling interpretation posits that unexpected rewards cannot shift the PLOC because they were not anticipated during engagement and therefore could not have been perceived as a reason for engaging.

> [!ask-yourself-this]
> The distinction between expected and unexpected rewards is one of CET's most precise and testable predictions — and one of its most counterintuitive. If rewards generally increase behavior, why would an unexpected reward fail to increase motivation but an expected reward decrease it? What does this asymmetry reveal about the psychological mechanism the theory is proposing?

### The Controversy: Cameron, Pierce, and Eisenberger's Challenge

CET generated substantial empirical controversy throughout the 1990s and 2000s. The two most significant challenges came from Cameron and Pierce (1994) and Eisenberger and colleagues.

Cameron and Pierce (1994) conducted a meta-analysis that they claimed showed the undermining effect to be limited to verbal rewards and to be generally absent for tangible rewards when reward contingency was properly controlled. Their analysis suggested that the body of literature showing undermining effects was methodologically confounded and that, properly interpreted, the data provided no systematic support for CET's predictions. This meta-analysis triggered a sustained methodological dispute, with Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999) conducting a comprehensive reanalysis of 128 studies that both rebutted Cameron and Pierce's conclusions and offered what remains the most systematic meta-analytic treatment of the literature.

Eisenberger and colleagues, drawing from interpersonal competence theory and learning theory, argued that performance-contingent rewards — far from undermining intrinsic motivation — could enhance it by communicating high expectations and confirming the individual's competence. Eisenberger's research suggested that the undermining effect was circumscribed to situations where rewards were not contingent on quality, and that a history of reward for high performance could shape a "learned industriousness" that generalized to intrinsic motivation.

> [!counter-argument]
> **The Cameron-Pierce Challenge to CET** rests primarily on an alternative categorization scheme for reward contingencies and a critique of the free-choice behavioral measure as the operational index of intrinsic motivation. Cameron and Pierce argued that the measure confounds intrinsic motivation with boredom satiation — that reduced engagement with a task after reward is simply the result of having performed the task multiple times, not from a reward-induced PLOC shift. The Deci et al. (1999) rebuttal showed that control groups with equivalent task exposure but no reward did not show the same reduction, which effectively refutes the satiation argument.

The Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis of 128 studies found that: (a) all expected tangible rewards reliably undermined intrinsic motivation as measured by free-choice behavior; (b) verbal rewards (positive feedback) significantly enhanced intrinsic motivation as measured by both free-choice behavior and self-report interest; (c) unexpected tangible rewards did not significantly affect intrinsic motivation; and (d) the reward effects were moderated by the controlling vs. informational functional significance of the context. This meta-analysis remains the most authoritative data synthesis in the literature and provides strong cumulative support for the core architecture of CET.

### CET in Relation to Other Motivational Frameworks

**In relation to [[achievement-goal-theory]]**: Achievement goal theory, associated with Elliot and Harackiewicz building on Dweck and Ames, distinguishes between mastery goals (focused on learning and personal improvement) and performance goals (focused on demonstrating ability relative to others). CET's prediction that controlling, performance-focused contexts undermine intrinsic motivation maps onto achievement goal theory's finding that ego-involving, performance-approach and performance-avoidance goal contexts are associated with reduced intrinsic motivation and diminished deep processing. The frameworks are complementary and mutually reinforcing, with CET providing the psychological mechanism (PLOC shift and competence need frustration) through which goal structures produce their motivational effects.

**In relation to [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]]**: The overlap between CET and attribution theory, particularly the application of the discounting principle to intrinsic motivation, was recognized by both Deci and Lepper et al. from the outset. However, CET departs from pure attribution theory by grounding the mechanism in basic psychological needs (autonomy and competence) rather than in attributional inference per se. This distinction is theoretically significant: need-satisfaction dynamics are not reducible to cognitive attributional operations and carry additional predictions about well-being and affect that attribution theory alone cannot generate.

**In relation to [[growth-mindset]] theory**: Dweck's entity/incremental theory of intelligence framework generates predictions that partially overlap with CET. The claim that entity theory environments (which signal that intelligence is fixed and being evaluated) produce defensive motivational orientations is congruent with CET's claim that ego-involving, controlling environments undermine intrinsic motivation. However, CET provides the motivational process account — through needs for autonomy and competence — that Dweck's framework does not specify.

(1999) and Cameron and Pierce (1994) illustrates the theory-laden nature of even quantitative literature syntheses — the categorization scheme used to organize studies determines the conclusions drawn. What does this suggest about how you should interpret meta-analytic findings in motivational psychology?

## Phase IV: Mechanisms & Processes

### The Psychological Mediation Chain

CET's mechanistic account is relatively explicit compared to many motivational theories. The theory specifies a three-step causal chain: (1) an external event enters the environment; (2) the individual interprets the functional significance of the event through their perception of its informational versus controlling properties; (3) this interpretation modulates the two psychological mediators — [[perceived-locus-of-causality|perceived locus of causality]] and perceived competence — which in turn determine whether intrinsic motivation is enhanced, maintained, or undermined.

This chain is not automatic. The same objective event can traverse it in different directions depending on contextual moderators (the interpersonal climate in which the event occurs), dispositional moderators (the individual's [[causality-orientations-theory|causality orientations]], which dispose them to interpret events as informational or controlling), and the domain-specific history of autonomous engagement (whether the activity has a baseline of intrinsic motivation that can be undermined).

> [!key-claim]
> **Psychological Mediation is the Core Testable Prediction of CET**: If the theory is correct, the effects of external events on behavioral outcomes (free-choice engagement, performance quality, creativity) should be mediated by changes in perceived autonomy and perceived competence — not by direct reinforcement of behavior. This prediction distinguishes CET sharply from behaviorist accounts and generates a specific methodological requirement: tests of CET should include mediation analyses rather than only documenting input-output relationships.

### How Controlling Events Undermine Intrinsic Motivation

The mechanisms through which controlling events undermine intrinsic motivation operate at multiple levels. The primary mechanism is the PLOC shift: when an individual engages with an activity under the condition of a salient controlling contingency, their cognitive-motivational processing of the activity is altered. The activity is no longer simply inherently interesting; it becomes instrumentally linked to an external outcome. The phenomenological quality of engagement shifts from autonomous, exploratory, and spontaneous to instrumental and pressured. The individual attends not to the intrinsic features of the activity — the challenge, the aesthetic qualities, the intellectual fascination — but to the contingency: whether they are meeting the criteria for reward.

This processing shift has downstream consequences for the quality of engagement. Research on the relationship between controlled motivation and cognitive performance consistently shows that controlled motivation leads to narrower attentional focus, reduced creativity, decreased conceptual depth, and elevated performance on algorithmic tasks while diminishing performance on tasks requiring heuristic exploration, flexible thinking, or genuine comprehension. These effects are congruent with CET's mechanistic account: when the perceived locus of causality is external, the individual optimizes for the contingency rather than for the activity, which systematically deflects attention from the features of the activity that would produce deep, transferable learning.

> [!evidence]
> **Amabile et al. (1976)** showed that artists who were asked to produce artwork for extrinsic evaluation received lower creativity ratings from professional judges than those who created artwork without evaluation, and this effect was mediated by the artists' self-reports of reduced intrinsic enjoyment during the controlled condition. This early study established the creativity-undermining effect of controlling external events that has been extensively replicated.
>
> **Grolnick and Ryan (1987)** demonstrated that children given autonomy-supportive instructions for reading a passage showed superior conceptual understanding and more durable retention compared to children given controlling instructions, despite equivalent surface-level performance — suggesting that controlled motivation produces a qualitative shift toward rote processing at the expense of meaningful encoding.

### How Informational Events Enhance Intrinsic Motivation

The mechanism through which informational events enhance intrinsic motivation is distinct and requires separate analysis. When an external event is parsed primarily as informational — when it conveys accurate, sincere feedback about the individual's competence without imposing pressure or contingent obligation — it supports both mediators in CET's model. It enhances perceived competence by confirming effective engagement, and it preserves the internal [[perceived-locus-of-causality|perceived locus of causality]] because the feedback is experienced as consistent with and supportive of the individual's own evaluative framework rather than as redirecting their behavior toward an externally imposed standard.

The classic laboratory demonstration of this positive effect involved verbal positive feedback. When participants were told they had performed "well" on an interesting task, subsequent free-choice engagement was higher than controls, suggesting that the competence-affirming information had enhanced intrinsic motivation. This effect is dependent, however, on the autonomy-supportive framing: if the same positive feedback is embedded in a controlling context ("You performed well — better than most people, which is what I expected of you"), its controlling aspect begins to compete with its informational aspect, and the net motivational effect can be neutral or even negative.

> [!insight]
> **The Functional Significance Interpretive Triangle** (original synthesis): CET's mechanism can be conceptualized as a three-point interpretive triangle in which every external event simultaneously activates three functional dimensions — informational, controlling, and amotivating — to varying degrees. The relative salience of each dimension is determined by contextual factors (the autonomy-supportiveness of the interpersonal climate), dispositional factors (the individual's causality orientation), and structural factors (the contingency architecture of the event). Effective motivational design consists of calibrating the event's architecture so that the informational dimension is dominant and the controlling dimension is minimized, which requires attention to how the event is framed, delivered, and contextualized — not only to its nominal content.

### The Role of the Interpersonal Climate

One of CET's most treatment-relevant claims is that the interpersonal climate in which external events are delivered significantly moderates their functional significance. Deci, Connell, and Ryan (1989) and subsequent research by Grolnick, Ryan, and colleagues established that even objectively controlling events — such as performance-contingent rewards, comparative feedback, or evaluative deadlines — can have their controlling salience reduced when they are delivered within an overall interpersonal context that is autonomy-supportive: one that acknowledges the individual's perspective, minimizes pressure, provides rationale for requirements, and conveys genuine respect for the individual's agency.

[[autonomy-support]] in this technical sense does not mean the absence of structure, standards, or requirements. An autonomy-supportive teacher can set high expectations, provide explicit standards, and offer corrective feedback — without these becoming controlling in their functional significance — provided that they are delivered with acknowledgment of the student's perspective and without pressure toward specific behaviors. Conversely, a teacher who offers rewards and praise but in a pressuring, surveillant, and conditionally approving manner will undermine intrinsic motivation despite the ostensibly positive valence of the events.

> [!key-claim]
> **The Interpersonal Climate as Functional Significance Amplifier**: The autonomy-supportiveness vs. controllingness of the interpersonal climate does not determine the motivational consequences of external events independently; it modulates the functional significance that those events are assigned. An event's informational vs. controlling parsing is not an objective reading of its properties but an interpretive act situated within a relational context. This is why identical reward structures can have opposite motivational effects in different interpersonal relationships.

### Ego Involvement as Intrapersonal Controlling Pressure

CET's extension to intrapersonal controlling events — specifically ego involvement — is among its most theoretically ambitious and practically significant claims. Ego involvement, in the SDT framework, refers to a psychological state in which one's engagement is driven by the contingent relationship between performance and self-worth: one performs not for the intrinsic interest of the activity but to demonstrate competence and avoid demonstrating incompetence, because the quality of performance is interpreted as a direct indicator of personal worth.

> [!definition] **Ego Involvement (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Nicholls, 1984)**
> Ego involvement refers to a motivational state in which engagement is driven by the desire to demonstrate or prove ability rather than to develop it, where self-worth is contingently dependent on performance outcomes. Ego-involved individuals experience their engagement as controlled by internal standards that function like external pressures: the compulsion is internally generated but operates like an external contingency in shifting the perceived locus of causality from the activity's intrinsic features toward self-protective performance management. CET treats ego involvement as an intrapersonal analogue to external controlling contexts.
>
> **Boundary:** Ego involvement must be distinguished from task involvement (focusing on mastery of the challenge itself, intrinsic to the activity) and from adaptive aspirations for excellence (which can be autonomously motivated and need-satisfying). The defining feature of ego involvement is the contingency between performance and self-worth — the stake one's fundamental sense of competence has in the outcome.
>
> **See also:** [[ego-involvement]], [[ego-involving-feedback]], [[contingent-self-esteem]], [[contingent-self-worth]], [[achievement-goal-theory]], [[mastery-goal-orientation]]

Ego-involved individuals exhibit many of the same motivational signatures as externally controlled individuals: reduced creativity, narrower exploratory behavior, heightened performance anxiety, and diminished intrinsic motivation over time. This is because the intrapersonal controlling process functions via the same mechanism as external control: it shifts the PLOC from the activity's intrinsic features toward the external-facing self-evaluative agenda, transforming engagement into an instrumental means of self-defense rather than an end in itself.

> [!reflection] **Phase IV Reflective Questions**
> 1. CET proposes a mediation chain in which external events → functional significance interpretation → PLOC and competence changes → intrinsic motivation. What are the points in this chain where individual differences could produce consistent deviations from the theory's average predictions?
> 2. The claim that ego involvement functions like an external controlling condition is theoretically bold — it asserts that internally generated pressures can undermine intrinsic motivation through the same mechanism as external reward. What evidence would fail to support this claim, and has that evidence been found?
> 3. If the interpersonal climate modulates how external events are parsed, what does this imply for the design of incentive systems in organizations? Could a reward-for-performance system be designed in a way that preserves intrinsic motivation?

## Phase V: Applications, Implications & Limitations

### Educational Applications

The educational implications of CET are among the most extensively studied and practically consequential in motivational psychology. The theory generates concrete prescriptions for how teachers, curriculum designers, and educational institutions should structure learning environments.

**Feedback design**: CET strongly predicts that feedback should be informational in its functional significance — conveying genuine information about competence and progress without exerting pressure toward specific performance outcomes. [[process-praise]] (praising the effort, strategy, or approach rather than the outcome or person) aligns with CET's requirements: it is informational (it identifies what contributed to effective performance), autonomy-preserving (it acknowledges the learner's agency), and competence-supporting without being contingency-creating. In contrast, person-level praise ("You are so talented") or outcome-contingent praise ("You'd better keep performing this well if you want to maintain your standing") activates the controlling functional aspect. The [[feedback-design]] literature has increasingly converged on CET-consistent principles for this reason.

**Reward structures in classrooms**: CET provides the theoretical basis for cautious use of extrinsic reward systems in educational settings, particularly for activities with an established intrinsic motivation baseline. Star charts, token economies, and grade-focused evaluation systems — all of which involve expected tangible or symbolic rewards contingent on engagement or performance — carry CET-predicted risk of undermining intrinsic motivation for learning. This does not mean that rewards must be eliminated from educational settings; it means that their contingency architecture, framing, and interpersonal delivery context require careful design to minimize controlling functional significance.

**Autonomy-supportive teaching**: The extensive research program on [[autonomy-supportive-teaching-and-learning-environments]] by Grolnick, Ryan, Reeve, and colleagues has operationalized and tested the CET prediction that teaching styles that support student autonomy — by offering choices, providing rationale for activities, acknowledging negative affect, and minimizing pressure — produce significantly higher intrinsic motivation, deeper conceptual understanding, and greater long-term interest compared to controlling teaching styles. This literature represents one of the most robust applied extensions of CET.

> [!best-practice]
> **CET-Consistent Feedback Design Principles**:
> (1) Prioritize informational content — be specific about what the learner did and why it worked, rather than simply evaluating whether the outcome met a standard.
> (2) Preserve agency in the feedback frame — use "you found an effective approach" rather than "you did what I asked," emphasizing the learner's authorship of their performance.
> (3) Minimize contingency — avoid framing feedback as the basis for future reward, punishment, or comparative ranking unless absolutely necessary, and when necessary, acknowledge the learner's perspective on the contingency.
> (4) Support rather than pressure — distinguish between setting standards (informational) and pressuring toward standards (controlling).

### Workplace Applications

CET's implications for organizational motivation have been extensively explored in work psychology. Deci, Connell, and Ryan (1989) moved early to test CET predictions in organizational settings, finding that managers trained in autonomy-supportive management styles produced employees with significantly higher intrinsic motivation, greater trust in the organization, and higher well-being — while employees of controlling managers showed the predicted patterns of diminished intrinsic motivation and lower engagement quality.

The implications for incentive system design are complex and consequential. Performance-contingent pay — a ubiquitous feature of contemporary organizational design — is predicted by CET to have fundamentally different motivational consequences for different types of work: for algorithmic, procedural tasks where creativity and intrinsic interest are not major performance determinants, the controlling effects of performance-contingent pay may have minimal negative impact (and potentially positive effects through the competence-confirming channel). For creative, complex, and deeply engaging knowledge work, where intrinsic motivation is the primary driver of performance quality, performance-contingent reward structures carry genuine risk of undermining the very motivation they are designed to harness.

### Open Problems and Limitations

**The measurement problem**: The free-choice behavioral measure, as the primary operationalization of intrinsic motivation in CET research, has attracted sustained criticism. Participants who spend less time with an activity during an ostensibly unmonitored period may be expressing satiation, contextual disinterest, or strategic time allocation rather than a genuine reduction in intrinsic motivation. Self-report measures of interest and enjoyment, while less subject to this limitation, carry their own validity concerns including demand characteristics and social desirability biases. The field has not settled on a single, unambiguous operationalization of intrinsic motivation.

**The baseline problem**: CET's undermining effect presupposes that the activity being targeted has an established intrinsic motivation baseline. For activities that are initially tedious, undifferentiated, or require instrumental motivation to begin in order to later develop intrinsic interest, controlling incentives may serve a legitimate scaffolding function. The theory's prescriptions risk being inappropriately extended beyond their domain of validity if this boundary condition is ignored.

**Cultural and individual variation**: While subsequent research (Chirkov, Ryan, Kim, & Kaplan, 2003) has demonstrated that the basic functional significance dynamics of CET are cross-culturally robust, the specific manifestations of autonomy and the social norms around reward and feedback differ significantly across cultural contexts. East Asian educational and workplace contexts, in which external standards and collective performance are more prominent features of motivational ecology, may show attenuated versions of the undermining effect — not because the psychological mechanism is absent, but because the interpersonal framing of external events more routinely integrates rationale, collective value, and respect for individual agency even within hierarchical structures.

> [!warning]
> **The Misapplication Risk in Educational Policy**: CET is sometimes misinterpreted as a blanket indictment of all external rewards and evaluations in educational settings. This reading is both theoretically inaccurate and practically damaging. CET's predictions are contingency-specific and context-sensitive. Verbal positive feedback under autonomy-supportive conditions *enhances* intrinsic motivation. Performance standards with rationale and acknowledgment of student perspective are consistent with need satisfaction. The theory's prescriptive force targets controlling functional significance, not external events per se. Policy applications that abolish reward systems without attending to the functional significance dimensions they are designed to manage may produce environments that are structurally different but motivationally similar to what they replaced.

## Phase VI: Synthesis & Integration

### The Deep Pattern: Motivation as Interpretive Architecture

Having moved through CET's conceptual foundations, theoretical landscape, empirical mechanisms, and practical implications, the synthetic insight that emerges is this: motivation is fundamentally an interpretive achievement rather than a hydraulic or energetic process. The same objective environment can be motivationally nourishing or motivationally toxic depending on the meaning the individual assigns to its events — and that meaning, while influenced by individual dispositions, is most powerfully shaped by the interpersonal context in which events are embedded.

This is not a trivial observation. It means that motivational design — whether of classrooms, organizations, or any other human system — cannot be reduced to the engineering of stimulus-response contingencies. It requires sustained attention to the relational and communicative dimensions of how events are framed, delivered, and received. A reward system that is technically identical in its contingency structure will produce different motivational outcomes in a controlling climate versus an autonomy-supportive one, because the functional significance of the reward is not a property of the reward but a product of the interpretive context.

> [!key-insight]
> **Motivation as Interpretive Architecture**: CET reveals that the fundamental unit of motivational analysis is not the behavior-consequence contingency but the subjective interpretation of what a given event means for one's sense of self-determination and effectiveness. This shifts the design challenge for all motivational systems from the engineering of contingencies to the cultivation of interpretive contexts — a far more demanding but also far more powerful form of motivational technology.

### CET as a Motivational Thermodynamics Model

CET can be conceptualized — and this represents an original theoretical synthesis advanced in this report — as a *motivational thermodynamics model*: a framework that describes the conditions under which motivational energy is conserved, enhanced, or dissipated. In thermodynamic systems, energy transformations depend on the properties of the medium through which they occur. Similarly, in CET, the motivational properties of external events (their reinforcing or punishing valence) are not directly transferred to behavior; they are transformed through the medium of functional significance interpretation, which acts as a motivational translating layer.

Just as thermodynamic efficiency depends on maintaining the appropriate temperature gradient and minimizing energy dissipation, motivational efficiency in CET depends on maintaining the informational gradient (competence-affirming events) while minimizing the controlling gradient (autonomy-undermining pressure). The result is a system in which motivational energy is not expended in the management of external pressure but available for internal engagement — which correlates empirically with all of the performance outcomes most valued in learning and knowledge work.

**The "so what"**: Understanding motivation through the lens of CET makes visible the hidden motivational tax that controlling environments impose. Organizations and educational institutions that rely heavily on contingent rewards, surveillance, imposed goals, and competitive rankings may achieve behavioral compliance — the outputs are produced — while simultaneously depleting the intrinsic motivational capital on which creative, self-sustaining performance depends. CET provides the theoretical framework for estimating this tax and designing systems that minimize it.

### The Integration with SDT's Broader Ecosystem

The deepest value of CET as a theoretical contribution lies not in its specific predictions about reward effects but in its role as the mechanistic bridge within SDT's theoretical ecosystem. CET operationalizes what basic need satisfaction actually means at the level of specific environmental inputs. [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]] tells us that [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] and [[competence-need|competence]] are universal needs; [[organismic-integration-theory]] tells us how extrinsically motivated behavior can become progressively autonomous; [[causality-orientations-theory]] tells us how dispositional tendencies to interpret events as autonomy-supportive or controlling moderate all of these effects. CET provides the micro-level specification of how individual environmental events either nourish or frustrate the needs that all of these frameworks reference.

> [!reflection] **Phase VI Integration Questions**
> 1. If CET is correct that motivation is fundamentally an interpretive achievement, what does this imply for contexts where the individual has limited interpretive agency — for example, under extreme scarcity, institutional coercion, or neurological constraint?
> 2. The "motivational thermodynamics" framing suggests that controlling environments impose a hidden motivational tax. How would you design a research study that could directly measure this tax — that is, quantify the motivational capital depleted by controlling contingencies relative to autonomy-supportive ones?
> 3. CET focuses on how environments affect individual motivation. What is the corresponding account of how individuals can develop the dispositional capacities to preserve intrinsic motivation even in controlling environments? (This connects to [[causality-orientations-theory]] and the [[self-regulated-learning]] literature.)

## Phase VII: Appendix

### Appendix Section 1: Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Cognitive Evaluation Theory — CET (Deci & Ryan, 1975, 1985)**
> Cognitive Evaluation Theory is the mini-theory within [[self-determination-theory]] that specifies the conditions under which external events — rewards, feedback, surveillance, deadlines, competitive structures — affect intrinsic motivation. CET proposes that external events have three aspects of functional significance: informational (competence-relevant, autonomy-preserving), controlling (autonomy-undermining, PLOC-shifting), and amotivating (competence-disconfirming). Informational events enhance intrinsic motivation; controlling events undermine it; amotivating events collapse it. The theory's central claims are: (1) expected tangible rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting activities, (2) positive verbal feedback reliably enhances intrinsic motivation, and (3) both effects are mediated by changes in perceived locus of causality and perceived competence.
>
> **Boundary:** CET applies specifically to activities with an established intrinsic motivation baseline. It does not predict effects for activities that were never intrinsically motivated. CET also does not cover the internalization of extrinsically motivated behavior — that is OIT's domain.
>
> **See also:** [[self-determination-theory]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[overjustification-effect]], [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]]

> [!definition] **Overjustification Effect (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973)**
> The overjustification effect refers to the reduction in intrinsic motivation that occurs when an individual receives an expected extrinsic reward for engaging in an activity they were already intrinsically motivated to perform. The term derives from Kelley's attribution-theoretic discounting principle: when both an internal cause (intrinsic interest) and an external cause (extrinsic reward) are present, the individual "overjustifies" their behavior by attributing it primarily to the more salient external cause, thereby discounting the internal cause and reducing subsequent intrinsic motivation. The effect is reliably observed for expected, tangible, engagement-contingent rewards but not for unexpected rewards or verbal praise under autonomy-supportive conditions.
>
> **Historical Note:** The term originated in Lepper et al.'s 1973 study using preschool children and reward certificates for drawing, and was originally grounded in attribution theory. Deci and Ryan later retheorized the mechanism through need satisfaction language (PLOC shift and competence modulation) rather than purely attributional inference.
>
> **See also:** [[overjustification-effect]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], [[engagement-contingent-reward]], [[the-undermining-effect]]

> [!definition] **Perceived Locus of Causality — PLOC (de Charms, 1968; Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> See full definition in Phase II. The PLOC is the subjective sense that one's behavior is self-determined (internal PLOC) or externally caused (external PLOC). In CET, controlling events shift the PLOC toward external; informational events preserve or enhance the internal PLOC. The PLOC mediates the relationship between external event type and intrinsic motivation.
>
> **See also:** [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], [[autonomy-need]], [[Autonomous Motivation]], [[Controlled Motivation]], [[External Regulation]]

> [!definition] **Functional Significance — Informational, Controlling, Amotivating (Deci & Ryan, 1985)**
> See full definition in Phase II. Functional significance is the perceived motivational role of an external event — the function the agent interprets the event as serving. Three aspects are identified: informational (competence-affirming, autonomy-preserving), controlling (pressure toward specific outcomes), and amotivating (competence-disconfirming). The dominant aspect in an individual's interpretation determines the motivational consequence of the event.
>
> **See also:** [[autonomy-support]], [[controlling-environment]], [[autonomy-supportive-environment]], [[reward-contingency-types]]

> [!definition] **Autonomy-Supportive Climate (Deci, Connell & Ryan, 1989)**
> An autonomy-supportive climate is an interpersonal context characterized by acknowledgment of the other's perspective, minimization of pressure toward specific outcomes, provision of rationale for requirements, and genuine respect for the other's agency and self-determination. Within CET, autonomy-supportive climates moderate the functional significance of external events by reducing the likelihood that any given event will be parsed as controlling. Teachers, managers, and caregivers can be trained in autonomy-supportive behavior; systematic differences in autonomy-support reliably predict differences in the intrinsic motivation, well-being, and performance of those they oversee.
>
> **Boundary:** Autonomy-supportive does not mean permissive or unstructured. High expectations, explicit standards, and performance feedback are all compatible with an autonomy-supportive interpersonal style when they are delivered with acknowledgment and rationale. The binary of "autonomy-supportive vs. controlling" applies to the relational style, not to the presence or absence of structure.
>
> **See also:** [[autonomy-support]], [[autonomy-supportive-teaching-and-learning-environments]], [[autonomy-supportive-environment]], [[controlling-environment]]

> [!definition] **Engagement-Contingent Reward (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999)**
> An engagement-contingent reward is an external incentive whose receipt is conditional on engaging with the target activity — the individual receives the reward simply for working on the task, regardless of how well they perform. Within the reward contingency taxonomy of CET, engagement-contingent rewards constitute the paradigm case of the undermining effect: they introduce a controlling external contingency that transforms the activity from a spontaneous expression of interest into an instrument for obtaining an extrinsic outcome.
>
> **See also:** [[engagement-contingent-reward]], [[reward-contingency-types]], [[overjustification-effect]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]]

> [!definition] **Ego Involvement (Nicholls, 1984; Ryan & Deci, 2000)**
> See definition in Phase IV. Ego involvement refers to a motivational state in which performance is driven by the contingency between outcome and self-worth, functioning as an intrapersonal analogue to external controlling pressure. CET predicts that ego involvement undermines intrinsic motivation through the same mechanism as external control — by shifting the perceived locus of causality from the activity's intrinsic features to self-evaluative agenda management.
>
> **See also:** [[ego-involvement]], [[ego-involving-feedback]], [[contingent-self-esteem]], [[contingent-self-worth]], [[mastery-goal-orientation]]

> [!definition] **Free-Choice Behavior Paradigm (Deci, 1971)**
> The free-choice behavioral paradigm is the primary experimental operationalization of intrinsic motivation in CET research. Following an experimental manipulation (typically involving reward or feedback conditions), participants are left in a room with the target activity available along with competing options, and an experimenter covertly measures the amount of time participants spontaneously spend with the activity during an ostensibly unobserved period. The theoretical assumption is that time spent freely engaging with the activity — without any ongoing external contingency — indexes the individual's intrinsic motivation for it.
>
> **Common Misconception:** The free-choice measure is not equivalent to self-reported interest or enjoyment, and the two operationalizations do not always converge. Meta-analyses find somewhat different patterns for free-choice behavior vs. self-report outcomes, which has generated theoretical and methodological discussion about what each measure actually captures.
>
> **Research Status:** The free-choice paradigm has been criticized for conflating intrinsic motivation with satiation and for potential experimenter demand effects. Multiple studies use both measures simultaneously; the Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis addressed these concerns systematically.
>
> **See also:** [[free-choice-behaviour]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[Intrinsic-Motivation-Research-Deci-1971]]

> [!definition] **Reward Contingency (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999)**
> Reward contingency refers to the relationship structure between a reward and the behavior or outcome for which it is delivered. CET distinguishes: task-noncontingent (reward independent of engagement), [[engagement-contingent-reward|engagement-contingent]] (reward for engaging), completion-contingent (reward for finishing), and performance-contingent (reward for performing above a standard). The contingency structure determines, to a significant degree, the functional significance of the reward: engagement- and completion-contingent rewards are most reliably controlling; unexpected, task-noncontingent rewards are least likely to undermine intrinsic motivation; performance-contingent rewards have the most mixed effects, reflecting competition between informational and controlling functional aspects.
>
> **See also:** [[reward-contingency-types]], [[engagement-contingent-reward]], [[overjustification-effect]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]]

> [!definition] **Amotivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000)**
> Amotivation refers to the absence of intentionality and volition with respect to an activity — the state in which an individual has neither intrinsic motivation nor any form of extrinsic motivation, typically resulting from repeated experiences of incompetence, learned helplessness, or the perception that no engagement with the activity can produce desired outcomes. CET identifies amotivating events — those that convey the impossibility of effective action — as producing amotivation. Amotivation is the most autonomous-need-depriving motivational state and is associated with the most negative outcomes across performance, well-being, and persistence dimensions.
>
> **See also:** [[amotivation]], [[learned-helplessness]], [[competence-need]], [[self-determination-theory]]

### Appendix Section 3: Conceptual Tensions & Open Questions

> [!tension] **Informational vs. Controlling Effects of Performance-Contingent Rewards**
> CET predicts that performance-contingent rewards have mixed effects because they carry both an informational dimension (confirming competence) and a controlling dimension (creating outcome pressure). Deci et al. (1999) found that high performance-contingent rewards produced the most variable results in the meta-analysis, with some studies showing enhancement and others showing undermining.
>
> **Position A (CET):** The controlling functional aspect dominates in most naturally occurring performance-contingent reward contexts, producing net undermining — unless the context is strongly autonomy-supportive.
>
> **Position B (Eisenberger):** Performance-contingent rewards that genuinely confirm high competence can enhance intrinsic motivation by satisfying the competence need without necessarily imposing controlling pressure.
>
> **Current Evidence:** The weight of meta-analytic evidence supports CET's prediction for engagement- and completion-contingent rewards but is genuinely mixed for high-level performance-contingent rewards when autonomy-supportive framing is present.
>
> **Why It Matters:** This tension has direct practical significance for performance management systems and educational assessment design.
>
> **See also:** [[reward-contingency-types]], [[engagement-contingent-reward]], [[autonomy-support]], [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]]

> [!tension] **Free-Choice Behavior vs. Self-Report as Intrinsic Motivation Operationalization**
> CET research has relied primarily on the free-choice behavioral paradigm (time spent freely with an activity) and self-reported interest as the two main operational indices of intrinsic motivation. These measures do not always converge.
>
> **Position A:** Free-choice behavior is the more valid measure because it is behavioral rather than self-reported and is less susceptible to demand characteristics and social desirability.
>
> **Position B:** Self-reported interest is more comprehensive, capturing the phenomenological dimension of intrinsic motivation rather than a single behavioral proxy that conflates multiple motivational and non-motivational determinants (satiation, external scheduling, etc.).
>
> **Current Evidence:** Meta-analyses show that reward effects on free-choice behavior and self-reported interest converge in direction but often differ in magnitude, with self-report measures showing larger positive feedback effects and smaller undermining effects than behavioral measures.
>
> **See also:** [[free-choice-behaviour]], [[Intrinsic Motivation]], [[mslq]]

> [!open-question] **The Generalizability of CET to Uninteresting Tasks**
> CET's undermining effect predictions explicitly presuppose an established intrinsic motivation baseline. For activities that are initially tedious or aversive — tasks for which there is no baseline intrinsic motivation to undermine — does the functional significance analysis still apply, or does CET become irrelevant?
>
> **Current Answer:** The theory explicitly acknowledges this boundary condition. For initially uninteresting tasks, the internalization account of [[organismic-integration-theory]] (rather than CET) is the relevant theoretical framework. However, the practical challenge of determining which tasks have "sufficient" intrinsic motivation to be vulnerable to the undermining effect has no clear empirical resolution.
>
> **Why It Matters:** Many educational tasks and most workplace tasks are perceived by participants as initially low in intrinsic interest. If CET applies only to a restricted range of activities, its prescriptive scope may be more limited than advocates suggest.

> [!debate] **The Cross-Cultural Universality of the Undermining Effect**
> Several studies in East Asian contexts — particularly Japan and China — have found attenuated or absent undermining effects, raising questions about whether the PLOC mechanism operates differently across cultural contexts that organize the self-environment relationship differently.
>
> **SDT Position (Chirkov et al., 2003):** The basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence are universal; the cultural variation reflects differences in how autonomy is expressed and how relational context moderates functional significance, not differences in the underlying mechanism.
>
> **Alternative Position:** The relative weight given to autonomous vs. controlled motivation as a default regulatory style, and the baseline functional significance assigned to authority-mediated events, may differ substantially across cultures in ways that produce genuinely different effect sizes for CET predictions.
>
> **See also:** [[self-determination-theory]], [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]], [[autonomy-need]]

> **Q:** What is the difference between the perceived locus of causality (PLOC) and locus of control?
> **A:** The PLOC concerns the perceived cause of one's *behavior* — whether one's actions are experienced as self-determined (internal PLOC) or externally caused (external PLOC). Locus of control (Rotter) concerns beliefs about the causes of *outcomes* — whether one's actions reliably produce intended results. The PLOC is about agency over action; locus of control is about the predictability of outcomes from action.

The primary criticism is that it may conflate intrinsic motivation with satiation, scheduling constraints, or contextual availability of competing activities — rather than purely indexing motivational state.

> **Q:** What is the overjustification effect, and what attribution-theoretic principle generates it?
> **A:** The overjustification effect is the reduction in intrinsic motivation that follows the introduction of an expected extrinsic reward for an already-interesting activity. The attribution-theoretic principle is Kelley's discounting principle: when multiple plausible causes for an action are present, the perceived role of any single cause is reduced. The highly salient external reward "crowds out" the internal cause (intrinsic interest) in the individual's causal attribution.

CET treats it as an *intrapersonal* controlling condition: like external controlling events, ego involvement shifts the PLOC from the intrinsic features of the activity toward a self-evaluative agenda, converting exploratory engagement into self-protective performance management. The mechanism is the same as external control — a shift in functional significance from informational to controlling — but the pressure source is internal.

Conversely, a controlling climate increases the likelihood that even informationally rich events will be experienced as pressure.
