# Cosmic Dawn

> [!definition] **Cosmic Dawn**
> Cosmic Dawn marks a pivotal period in cosmological history, approximately 150 to 500 million years after the Big Bang (redshifts z ≈ 10–30), when the first stars and galaxies emerged from the cosmic dark ages. This epoch is crucial as it signifies the transition from an opaque universe filled with neutral hydrogen to one illuminated by the light of nascent celestial bodies, setting the stage for reionization. It falls under cosmological epochs, distinct from both the preceding dark ages and the subsequent era of reionization.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It is distinct from the subsequent epoch of reionization and does not include the later stages of galaxy formation or evolution. It should not be confused with the 'dark ages' preceding it.

## Core Explanation

Cosmic Dawn is a critical phase in the early universe's evolution, marking the emergence of the first luminous objects. This period saw the formation of Population III stars—massive, short-lived stars composed primarily of hydrogen and helium—which were instrumental in initiating reionization by emitting intense ultraviolet radiation that ionized surrounding neutral hydrogen. Theoretical models suggest these initial stars formed within dense molecular clouds, where gravity overcame thermal pressure to collapse matter into protostellar cores.

Observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have provided tantalizing glimpses of galaxies at redshifts greater than 12, corresponding to cosmic ages less than 400 million years. These detections are crucial for understanding the early stages of galaxy formation and evolution. However, initial photometric redshift estimates from JWST have been revised following spectroscopic follow-up, highlighting the complexity in accurately dating these distant objects.

Theoretical frameworks predict that the first galaxies formed within dark matter halos, which acted as gravitational wells attracting gas to form stars. These early galaxies were likely smaller and more compact than their modern counterparts, with intense star formation rates driven by high molecular hydrogen fractions. The interplay between radiation from nascent stars and surrounding neutral hydrogen is thought to have initiated the reionization process.

The EDGES experiment's claimed detection of a strong 21-cm absorption signal at redshifts around 17 has sparked significant debate within the astronomical community. This signal, if confirmed, would provide unprecedented insights into the thermal history of the intergalactic medium during Cosmic Dawn. However, subsequent observations by SARAS-3 have failed to replicate this result, leaving the existence and nature of such a signal in question.

The Cosmic Dawn period is marked by a dramatic shift in the universe's energy balance, as the first stars began to emit light and radiation that would eventually ionize the intergalactic medium. This transition from an opaque universe filled with neutral hydrogen to one illuminated by starlight was not instantaneous but rather occurred over millions of years, during which time the nascent galaxies grew brighter and more numerous. Theoretical models suggest that these early stars were much larger and hotter than those we observe today, emitting intense ultraviolet radiation that ionized surrounding hydrogen atoms, thus initiating the epoch of reionization.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Observational Astronomy**
> The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is poised to revolutionize our understanding of Cosmic Dawn by providing high-resolution images and spectroscopic data from the earliest galaxies. By observing these distant objects, astronomers can infer properties such as star formation rates, metallicities, and morphologies that were present in the universe's infancy. This information is crucial for validating theoretical models of galaxy formation and evolution.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Future Facilities**
> Upcoming facilities like the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and Lunar Surface Electromagnetic Environment Explorer-Night (LuSEE-Night) are designed to probe Cosmic Dawn in unprecedented detail. These instruments will enable astronomers to study the 21-cm hydrogen line at redshifts corresponding to this epoch, potentially resolving the EDGES controversy and providing a clearer picture of the thermal history of the intergalactic medium.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Cosmic Dawn vs. Reionization**
> While Cosmic Dawn marks the formation of the first stars, galaxies, and black holes, reionization is the process by which these objects ionized the intergalactic medium. The two epochs are closely linked but distinct: Cosmic Dawn sets the stage for reionization, while reionization completes the transition from a neutral to an ionized universe.

> [!key-distinction] **Dark Ages vs. Cosmic Dawn**
> The Dark Ages refer to the period immediately following the cosmic microwave background radiation, characterized by a lack of luminous sources and neutral hydrogen filling the intergalactic medium. In contrast, Cosmic Dawn marks the emergence of the first stars and galaxies, signaling the end of this dark era.

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning] **Misconception** — People think the first stars formed immediately after the Big Bang.
>
> The first stars, known as Population III stars, did not form until Cosmic Dawn, approximately 150 to 500 million years post-Big Bang. This delay was due to the time required for matter to cool and collapse into dense regions where gravity could overcome thermal pressure, initiating star formation.
