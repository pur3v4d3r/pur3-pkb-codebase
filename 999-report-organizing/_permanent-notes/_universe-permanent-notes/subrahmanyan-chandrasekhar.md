# Chandrasekhar Limit

> [!definition] **Chandrasekhar Limit**
> The Chandrasekhar limit (~1.4 M☉) is a theoretical upper mass boundary for white dwarfs supported by electron-degeneracy pressure, derived by Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar in 1930. This concept does not encompass neutron stars or black holes, which are governed by distinct physical principles and have their own mass limits, such as the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit for neutron stars. It falls under theoretical astrophysics.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes other types of stellar endpoints such as neutron stars and black holes, which are governed by different physical principles. It should not be confused with the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit for neutron stars.

## Core Explanation

Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar's groundbreaking work in 1930 established that white dwarfs cannot exceed a mass of approximately 1.4 solar masses due to the limitations imposed by electron-degeneracy pressure, which is the quantum mechanical force preventing electrons from occupying the same energy state within an atom. This discovery was pivotal as it implied that stars more massive than this limit would not end their lives as white dwarfs but would instead collapse into denser states, such as neutron stars or black holes.

The theoretical underpinnings of Chandrasekhar's work were rooted in the understanding of stellar evolution and the physics governing dense matter. His calculations revealed that beyond a certain mass threshold, electron-degeneracy pressure could no longer counteract gravitational collapse, leading to the formation of more compact objects. This insight was revolutionary as it suggested the existence of neutron stars and black holes long before observational evidence confirmed these predictions.

Chandrasekhar's identification of this upper limit for white dwarfs not only advanced our understanding of stellar endpoints but also laid the groundwork for future research into compact objects in astrophysics. Despite initial skepticism from prominent figures like Sir Arthur Eddington, who dismissed Chandrasekhar's findings at a 1935 Royal Astronomical Society meeting, the concept has since been widely accepted and forms a cornerstone of modern astrophysical theory.

The empirical validation of Chandrasekhar's limit came decades later with observations that confirmed the existence of neutron stars and black holes. This theoretical anticipation of compact-object physics before observational confirmation underscores the power of theoretical astrophysics in predicting phenomena that can only be observed through advanced technology.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Stellar Evolution**
> Understanding the Chandrasekhar limit is crucial for modeling stellar evolution, particularly for stars that end their lives as white dwarfs. It implies that if a star's core mass exceeds this threshold after shedding its outer layers through planetary nebulae formation, it will collapse into a denser state rather than remaining stable as a white dwarf. This insight guides predictions about the final stages of stellar life cycles and helps astronomers classify different types of compact objects.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Compact Object Formation**
> The Chandrasekhar limit provides critical information on how stars evolve into neutron stars or black holes, depending on their initial mass. Stars that exceed this limit during the white dwarf phase will collapse further due to gravitational forces overcoming electron-degeneracy pressure. This leads to the formation of neutron stars if the core is not too massive, or directly into a black hole if the star's mass surpasses even higher limits. Thus, it delineates the pathways for stellar remnants and informs our understanding of cosmic phenomena like supernovae.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **White Dwarfs vs Neutron Stars**
> The Chandrasekhar limit (~1.4 M☉) defines the maximum mass a white dwarf can have before it collapses into a neutron star or black hole, whereas neutron stars are formed from more massive stellar cores that exceed this limit but do not reach the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit for black holes. This distinction is crucial as it helps classify different types of compact objects based on their mass and physical properties.
