# Calibration Engine

> [!definition] **Calibration Engine** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Definition:** A framing of the Forethought-Reflection cycle as a recursive self-calibration system whose core function is to align the learner's self-model with their actual competence level. **Boundary Conditions:** The engine metaphor emphasizes that calibration is an emergent structural output of the cycle, not a goal the learner explicitly pursues. **Report-Specific Significance:** Reframes the purpose of SRL practice from "performing better on individual tasks" to "developing an increasingly accurate self-model of growing competence." **Operational Indicators:** A well-functioning Calibration Engine produces a Calibration Log that shows convergence between predicted comprehension levels and actual comprehension outcomes across sessions. **Cross-References:** [[metacognitive-calibration]], [[Zimmerman's-Cyclical-SRL-Model]], [[self-efficacy]]

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 2
> **A:** **Q:** Why is the comprehension criterion the structural counter to the fluency illusion?
**A:** Because the comprehension criterion requires *cognitive production* (recall, explanation, example generation) to satisfy — which cannot be faked by recognition fluency. The fluency illusion operates through phenomenological ease of processing, which can feel like understanding without being it. A production-based criterion forces the learner to generate rather than recognize, revealing genuine comprehension status.
**Source:** Phase IV, Goal Setting mechanism
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Process

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 3
> **A:** **Q:** What makes an attribution "adaptive" in the SRL cycle? Give the three necessary criteria.
**A:** An adaptive attribution is (1) SPECIFIC — names a particular mechanism or behavior rather than a global trait; (2) UNSTABLE — implies the outcome could be different with different strategy/preparation; (3) CONTROLLABLE — within the learner's influence to change. All three are necessary: a specific but uncontrollable attribution (e.g., "this is just a particularly hard domain") does not produce adaptive inference.
**Source:** Phase IV, Causal Attribution mechanism
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Distinction

> **Q:** Seed 4
> **A:** **Q:** What does the Calibration Engine synthesis identify as the *true output* of a well-designed Forethought-Reflection cycle over time?
**A:** Metacognitive calibration — an increasingly accurate self-model of the learner's actual competence level (what they understand, at what depth, with what confidence). Calibration is not a skill the learner explicitly targets but an emergent structural output of consistently well-executed cycles. The engine metaphor captures this: build the cycle well, and calibration emerges as a byproduct.
**Source:** Phase IV, Original Synthesis; Phase VI
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Connection

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 5
> **A:** **Q:** Why does the Self-Reflection Phase require the Forethought Phase to have produced a written comprehension criterion?
**A:** Because self-evaluation requires a comparison between actual performance and a pre-set standard. Without a written criterion, (a) the original goal is typically lost to cognitive load interference during the reading session, and (b) self-evaluation defaults to phenomenological assessment (did this feel productive?) rather than epistemic assessment (did I achieve what I set out to understand?). Written criteria are the structural mechanism that preserves evaluability.
**Source:** Phase IV and V
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Process

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 6
> **A:** **Q:** What is the three-part learning goal structure introduced in this report, and why does each component matter?
**A:** (1) Content Anchor — specifies WHAT concept/mechanism is targeted (activates attentional direction); (2) Comprehension Criterion — specifies WHAT LEVEL of understanding is targeted and how it will be verified (provides evaluable standard for Self-Reflection); (3) Transfer Target — specifies WHY this understanding is wanted (provides functional relevance that sustains intrinsic motivation). Remove any component and a specific mechanism is compromised.
**Source:** Phase IV, Goal Setting section
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Definition

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 7
> **A:** **Q:** What is the "expertise trap" in relation to goal orientation, and why does it specifically affect advanced learners?
**A:** The expertise trap is the pattern where intellectually sophisticated learners who have succeeded academically have internalized a performance orientation — their identity is linked to appearing competent. When encountering genuinely difficult material in private learning, this manifests as avoidance of the most challenging sections, surface processing, and resistance to explicit self-evaluation (which makes deficits visible and thus threatening). Advanced learners are *more* susceptible, not less, because they have more identity investment in appearing competent.
**Source:** Phase IV, Goal Orientation section
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Application

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 8
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Handoff Mechanism, and why is it structurally necessary rather than merely helpful?
**A:** The Handoff Mechanism is a physical link (a specific field in the Self-Reflection template that feeds directly into the next session's Forethought template) that transfers the adaptive inference from one cycle to the next. It is structurally necessary because adaptive inferences — the most valuable output of the Self-Reflection Phase — evaporate without explicit capture and forward connection. In a solo learning context without social accountability structures, the handoff mechanism is the only reliable mechanism for actually applying inferences across sessions rather than generating and discarding them.
**Source:** Phase V, Application 2
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Application

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 9
> **A:** **Q:** How does the Self-Reflection Phase *construct* self-efficacy for the next Forethought Phase?
**A:** Self-efficacy in a solo PKB learning system is built primarily through mastery experiences — direct experience of successfully achieving comprehension criteria. These experiences are *generated* in the Performance Phase but *interpreted* in the Self-Reflection Phase through the attribution mechanism. If Self-Reflection produces accurate mastery-referenced self-evaluation and controllable attributions, self-efficacy updates accurately. If it produces ability-based attributions, self-efficacy either deflates (failure → I'm not capable) or becomes inflated through fluency illusion (apparent success without genuine comprehension). Self-efficacy is therefore a *dynamic output* of Self-Reflection, not a stable input to Forethought.
**Source:** Phase IV, Return-and-Deepen: Self-Efficacy Revisited
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Connection

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[srl-forethought-self-reflection-pkb-focused-analysis-2026-03-24 (1)]])*
> **Q:** Seed 10
> **A:** **Q:** What does the SDT research add to the language design requirements for SRL tools that Achievement Goal Theory does not cover?
**A:** SDT's autonomy support research adds that *controlling language* — even self-directed controlling language without any external agent — undermines intrinsic motivation. "I must understand this" generates internal pressure that is phenomenologically equivalent to external control and activates the same amotivation mechanisms. Achievement Goal Theory covers the mastery/performance orientation distinction but does not address the autonomy dimension — the difference between "I want to understand X because it connects to [valued goal]" (autonomy-supportive) and "I must understand X" (controlling). Both are mastery-framed; only one is autonomy-supportive.
**Source:** Phase V, Language Architecture section
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Distinction
