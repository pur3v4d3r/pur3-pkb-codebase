# The Locus of Causality Dimension

> [!definition] **The Locus of Causality Dimension** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> The [[locus-of-causality]] dimension classifies causes as either internal to the person (ability, effort, mood, personality) or external to the person (task difficulty, luck, other people's actions, institutional factors). This dimension is the direct descendant of [[fritz-heider]]'s original internal/external distinction, though Weiner refined it by distinguishing locus of causality from locus of control — a distinction that matters because a cause can be internal (it resides within me) without being controllable (I cannot change it).
> 
> **Boundary:** Locus of causality should not be confused with [[locus-of-control]] (Rotter, 1966), which concerns generalised beliefs about whether outcomes are determined by one's own actions. Locus of causality is situation-specific and causal; locus of control is dispositional and expectancy-based.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The locus dimension is the primary determinant of pride and shame: internal attributions for success produce pride, internal attributions for failure produce shame.
> 
> **See also:** [[locus-of-causality]], [[locus-of-control]], [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], [[self-esteem]]

## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Fritz Heider (1896–1988)** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> Austrian-American psychologist, University of Kansas. Foundational figure who established the study of causal attribution with *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations* (1958). Introduced the naive psychologist metaphor and the fundamental internal/external causal distinction. His work seeded both correspondent inference theory (Jones & Davis) and the covariation model (Kelley). All subsequent attribution research descends from Heider's core insight that ordinary people are intuitive causal theorists.

> [!person] **Bernard Weiner (1935–present)** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> American psychologist, UCLA. The architect of the three-dimensional causal taxonomy (locus, stability, controllability) and the attribution-emotion mediation model. Weiner's contributions span from 1972 to the present, with his 1985 and 1986 publications representing the mature statement of his intrapersonal theory. His extension to interpersonal motivation (1995, 2006) — examining how attributions for others' outcomes generate emotions that drive helping behaviour and social judgments — extended the theory's reach into social psychology, moral judgment, and public policy.

Developed the covariation model (1967) and causal schemata theory (1972), providing the normative input-side account of how people combine consistency, distinctiveness, and consensus information to arrive at internal or external attributions. > [!section-summary] **Section 1 Summary** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> Attribution theory begins with the recognition that causal explanation is a selective, event-triggered cognitive process — activated most reliably by unexpected outcomes, negative results, and personally significant events. The process is purposive: it serves the forward-looking function of guiding future action, not merely the backward-looking function of explaining the past. > **Q:** Definition: Causal Attribution
> **A:** **Q:** What is a causal attribution in Weiner's framework?
**A:** The cognitive process of assigning a specific cause to an event or outcome, classifiable along three dimensions (locus, stability, controllability), whose dimensional coordinates determine its emotional and motivational consequences.
**Source:** Section 1, Lexicon A.1

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Distinction: Locus of Causality vs. Locus of Control
> **A:** **Q:** How does locus of causality (Weiner) differ from locus of control (Rotter)?
**A:** Locus of causality is a situation-specific classification of a particular cause as internal or external. Locus of control is a generalised dispositional belief about whether outcomes in general are determined by one's own actions or by external forces. The first is a property of a single attribution; the second is a personality trait.
**Source:** Section 2
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #attribution-theory, #distinction, #locus

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Process: The Stability-Expectancy Mechanism
> **A:** **Q:** How does the stability dimension determine expectancy change after an outcome?
**A:** Stable causes produce the expectation that the same outcome will recur in the future, maintaining the current expectancy level. Unstable causes break the causal continuity between past and future, allowing expectancy to shift — opening the possibility of different outcomes.
**Source:** Section 3
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #attribution-theory, #stability, #expectancy

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Distinction: Outcome-Dependent vs. Attribution-Dependent Emotions
> **A:** **Q:** What distinguishes outcome-dependent from attribution-dependent emotions?
**A:** Outcome-dependent emotions (happiness, sadness) are generated by the valence of the outcome itself and require no causal analysis. Attribution-dependent emotions (pride, shame, guilt, anger, pity, hope, hopelessness) are generated by the dimensional properties of the causal attribution and emerge only after causal processing is complete.
**Source:** Section 4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #attribution-theory, #emotion, #distinction

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Definition: Fundamental Attribution Error
> **A:** **Q:** What is the fundamental attribution error?
**A:** The systematic tendency for observers to overweight dispositional (internal) factors and underweight situational (external) factors when explaining another person's behaviour. It reflects both cognitive mechanisms (behaviour salience, insufficient adjustment) and is culturally variable, being stronger in individualist societies.
**Source:** Section 5, Lexicon A.1
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** #attribution-theory, #bias, #fundamental-attribution-error

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Connection: Implicit Theories and the Stability Dimension
> **A:** **Q:** How do Dweck's implicit theories of intelligence function as upstream constraints on attribution?
**A:** Entity theorists (fixed intelligence) classify ability as stable, so failure attributed to ability produces hopelessness and withdrawal. Incremental theorists (malleable intelligence) classify ability as unstable, so the same failure attribution produces effort and strategy change. The implicit theory constrains the stability coordinate of the attribution, not the attribution itself.
**Source:** Section 8
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #attribution-theory, #implicit-theories, #growth-mindset, #connection

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Application: Attribution Retraining's Two-Stage Architecture
> **A:** **Q:** What are the two stages of effective attribution retraining, and why are both necessary?
**A:** Stage 1 (Induction) provides normative information and models to introduce adaptive attribution patterns. Stage 2 (Consolidation) builds capacity for spontaneous self-correction through practice, self-monitoring, and vocabulary expansion. Stage 1 alone produces fragile change; Stage 2 produces internalised, self-sustaining attributional habits.

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Distinction: Shame vs. Guilt in Attribution Theory
> **A:** **Q:** How does attribution theory distinguish shame from guilt, and why does this distinction matter motivationally?
**A:** Shame arises from internal-uncontrollable failure attributions (low ability), targeting the whole self and producing withdrawal and helplessness. Guilt arises from internal-controllable failure attributions (insufficient effort), targeting a specific behaviour and producing reparative action and increased effort. The distinction matters because shame is motivationally paralysing while guilt is motivationally activating.
**Source:** Section 4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Process: Actor-Observer Asymmetry Mechanism
> **A:** **Q:** What produces the actor-observer asymmetry in attribution, and through what mechanisms?
**A:** Actors attribute their own behaviour more to situational factors; observers attribute the same behaviour more to dispositional factors. This arises from: (1) informational differences — actors have access to their own history and context, observers do not; (2) perceptual differences — actors' attention is directed outward toward the situation, observers' attention is directed at the actor; (3) motivational differences — actors may be motivated to explain away negative behaviour situationally.
**Source:** Section 5
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #attribution-theory, #actor-observer, #bias, #process

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]])*
> **Q:** Connection: Attribution as the Interpretive Layer of Motivation
> **A:** **Q:** In what sense does attribution function as the "interpretive layer" connecting other motivational theories?
**A:** Self-efficacy depends on attributional interpretation of mastery experiences (effort vs. ability attribution for success). Implicit theories constrain the stability dimension of attributions. SDT's perceived locus of causality is the attribution dimension itself. Attribution provides the cognitive mechanism through which raw experience is converted into motivationally significant causal narratives — the common plumbing through which different motivational constructs flow.

Clarifies the sequential model of dispositional inference and situational correction.

The essential reference for this report's theoretical core.
- **Weiner, B. (1995). *Judgments of responsibility: A foundation for a theory of social conduct*.
