# Calibration vs. Sensitivity in Metacognitive Judgment

> [!definition] **Calibration vs. Sensitivity in Metacognitive Judgment** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> *Sensitivity* is the discriminative power of metacognitive judgments — the degree to which higher confidence reliably accompanies better performance and lower confidence reliably accompanies worse performance, abstracted from the absolute confidence level. *Calibration* (or *bias*) is the absolute alignment between confidence level and performance level — the question of whether stated confidence corresponds to actual probability of being correct. A reader with high sensitivity but inflated calibration knows which passages they understood best but systematically overestimates how well they understood any of them. The two properties dissociate, and instructional interventions tend to affect one without the other.
> 
> **Boundary:** Calibration error is not a generalized cognitive deficit. It coexists with high intelligence, deep expertise, and substantial reading skill. The error is *structural* to how the monitoring system uses indirect cues, not *informational* about the reader's underlying ability.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The calibration problem is what prevents the monitoring–control loop from operating as theory predicts it should. Sensitive monitoring with poor calibration produces fewer regulatory responses than the actual rate of comprehension failure would warrant, leaving silent failures uncorrected.
> 
> **See also:** [[Calibration-Science-and-Metacognitive-Accuracy]], [[Calibration-Training-Methods-and-Evidence]], [[metacognitive-monitoring-accuracy]], [[metacognitive-sensitivity]]

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Definition: Comprehension Monitoring
> **A:** **Q:** What is comprehension monitoring, and how does it differ from comprehension itself?
**A:** Comprehension monitoring is the metacognitive process by which a reader evaluates the state of their developing comprehension during reading and detects breakdowns. It differs from comprehension itself in that comprehension is the object-level construction of meaning while monitoring is the meta-level evaluation of how that construction is going. A reader can comprehend without monitoring (passive reading) and can monitor without comprehending (detecting that comprehension has failed).
**Source:** Section 1, Section 3

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Definition: Standard of Coherence
> **A:** **Q:** What is the standard of coherence in reading, and what are the levels Baker identified?
**A:** The standard of coherence is the criterion against which a reader evaluates the coherence of their developing comprehension. Baker identified at least five levels: lexical (word meaning), syntactic (sentence parsing), propositional (assertions making sense individually), structural (local discourse coherence), and external (consistency with world knowledge and reading purpose).

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Distinction: Sensitivity vs. Calibration
> **A:** **Q:** What is the formal distinction between metacognitive sensitivity and calibration, and why does it matter?
**A:** Sensitivity is discriminative — the ability to assign higher confidence to better-comprehended material. Calibration is absolute — the alignment between confidence level and actual performance level. They dissociate: a reader can have good sensitivity but poor (inflated) calibration. The distinction matters because instructional interventions tend to affect one without the other, and the two failure modes have different regulatory consequences.
**Source:** Section 5

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Distinction: Declarative, Procedural, Conditional Knowledge
> **A:** **Q:** What is Brown's distinction among declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge, and which is most often missing?
**A:** Declarative knowledge is knowing *that* (knowing strategies exist); procedural is knowing *how* (knowing how to execute them); conditional is knowing *when and why* (knowing under what circumstances each strategy is appropriate). Conditional knowledge is the most consequential and most often missing, producing readers who can name and execute strategies but cannot select them appropriately.

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Process: The Monitoring–Control Loop
> **A:** **Q:** Describe the structure of the Nelson–Narens monitoring–control loop and how it applies to reading.
**A:** The framework distinguishes two functional levels — the *object level* performing the cognitive task and the *meta level* observing it — with two information flows between them: monitoring (object → meta) and control (meta → object). Applied to reading, comprehension processes operate at the object level while metacognitive processes operate at the meta level, with monitoring providing signals about comprehension state and control providing regulatory action when monitoring detects breakdown.

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Application: Why Self-Explanation Works
> **A:** **Q:** Why is self-explanation consistently more effective than rereading for comprehension and transfer?
**A:** Self-explanation forces the reader to make implicit inferences explicit, exposes gaps in the developing representation that monitoring alone would not flag, and requires articulation that surfaces the reader's actual rather than assumed understanding. It substitutes a *direct test* of representation for the *indirect inference* that ordinary monitoring relies upon, making it a regulatory action and a calibration check at the same time.

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Connection: Fluency Illusion and Calibration Error
> **A:** **Q:** How does the fluency illusion mechanism produce calibration error, and under what conditions is it strongest?
**A:** The monitoring system uses processing fluency as an indirect comprehension cue because no direct cue is available. But familiarity, visual clarity, topic recognition, and fluent prose style all produce fluency without comprehension. Under these conditions, fluency-based monitoring generates inflated confidence even when comprehension is uncertain. The illusion is strongest exactly under the conditions of ordinary reading (rereading, well-formatted text, familiar topics), making it a pervasive rather than occasional source of miscalibration.

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Connection: Reciprocal Teaching and the Architectural Layers
> **A:** **Q:** How do reciprocal teaching's four strategies (predicting, questioning, clarifying, summarizing) map to the four-layer architecture of metacognitive reading?
**A:** Predicting activates prior knowledge (Layer 3: knowledge of cognition) and sets a standard against which subsequent reading will be evaluated (Layer 2: standard of coherence). Questioning forces explicit articulation that exercises monitoring sensitivity (Layer 1: monitoring–control loop). Clarifying selects appropriate fix-up strategies for identified breakdowns (Layer 4: regulatory repertoire). Summarizing tests structural comprehension and exposes integration failures. Together the four strategies exercise the entire architecture, which is why the intervention is effective.

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> **Q:** Definition: Illusion of Explanatory Depth
> **A:** **Q:** What is the illusion of explanatory depth, and why does it matter for metacognitive reading?
**A:** The illusion of explanatory depth is the systematic finding that people believe they understand topics well enough to explain them and discover, when actually attempting the explanation, that their understanding was a felt sense of comprehension supported by recognizable fragments rather than an integrated representation. It matters for metacognitive reading because it is the calibration problem in acute form, demonstrating that internal monitoring routinely fails to flag gaps that explanation reveals.
