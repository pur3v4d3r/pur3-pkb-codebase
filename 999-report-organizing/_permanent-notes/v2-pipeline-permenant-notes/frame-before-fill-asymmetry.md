# Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry

> [!definition] **Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The directional asymmetry in situation model construction: a structural frame (causal, architectural, or schematic) can accept and organize incoming details, but an accumulation of unframed details cannot generate a structural frame. Situation models must be constructed top-down (frame first, details second) because the reverse direction does not produce coherent mental models.
> 
> **Boundary:** "Frame-Before-Fill" is not an instruction to learn abstractions before specifics in all cases. It is specifically a claim about the construction of mental models: the organizing structure must precede the detailed content it organizes.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry is the central original synthesis of this report, unifying all four mechanisms and all four interventions under a single structural principle.
> 
> **See also:** [[advance-organizer]], [[schema-theory]], [[mental-models]], [[situation-model]], [[the-schema-progressive-architecture-for-pkb-design]]

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Before reading any complex text, execute a 3–5 minute pre-reading sequence: (1) Read only the headings, subheadings, and the first and last paragraphs. (2) Write a one-sentence statement of what you predict the text argues or explains. (3) Write a specific purpose question: "I am reading this to understand \_\_\_\_\_." This frame initiates partial situation model construction before content reading begins, giving incoming propositions structural anchors.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> During reading, actively track the causal chain. In the margin or in your notes, maintain a running causal map: "X → Y → Z." Whenever a new causal claim appears, add it to the chain. Whenever the text moves to a new topic without providing a causal connector, pause and ask: *What is the causal connection between what I just read and what I am about to read?* If you cannot answer, this is a gap requiring active inference generation before proceeding.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> After every paragraph, briefly cover the text and restate the paragraph's *causal contribution* in your own words — not what it said, but what it *did* to the overall argument or explanation. "This paragraph established the mechanism by which X causes Y" rather than "This paragraph was about X and Y." The active reformulation forces situation model construction.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Within five minutes of completing a reading session, without looking at the text, write down everything you can remember — in any order, without evaluation. Then specifically attempt to reconstruct the causal spine: "What caused what, in what sequence?" This retrieval attempt both reveals gaps and strengthens the memory traces for what was successfully constructed. [[retrieval-practice|Retrieval practice]] research consistently shows this produces better long-term retention than re-reading.

For readers with near-zero prior knowledge in a domain, the situation model construction process will be severely limited regardless of strategy, because the bridging inferences simply cannot be generated from nothing. ## Spaced Repetition Seeds

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 1 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between a textbase and a situation model?

**A:** A textbase is a propositional representation of *what the text said* — the ideas expressed in the text, stripped of their exact wording. A situation model is a representation of *what the text is about* — the state of affairs, system, or argument described, integrated with prior knowledge. The textbase represents the text; the situation model represents the world the text describes.

**Source:** Phase II, Three-Level Architecture
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** #situation-model #textbase #reading-comprehension

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 2 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What are the five dimensions of the Event Indexing Model (CATSM), and which is most vulnerable to disruption in academic expository text?

**A:** Causality, Agency, Time, Space, Motivation. Causality is most vulnerable in academic text because causal relations are frequently implicit — the text presents X and Y without explicitly marking the causal connection, requiring the reader to generate bridging inferences using prior knowledge. When prior knowledge is insufficient, the causal dimension collapses, disrupting all other dimensions.

**Source:** Phase II, Event Indexing Model; Phase IV, Mechanism 3
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Process
**Tags:** #CATSM #event-indexing-model #causal-inference #situation-model

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 3 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Fluency Illusion, and why is it specifically dangerous for situation model construction?

**A:** The Fluency Illusion is the metacognitive error in which ease of processing (fluency) is mistaken for depth of understanding. It is specifically dangerous for situation model construction because it produces a false comprehension signal that terminates comprehension effort before the situation model is built. A reader can fluently build a textbase, experience the ease of processing as understanding, and never engage the active inference generation that situation model construction requires.

**Source:** Phase III, Evidence Area 2; Phase IV, Mechanism 2
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Definition
**Tags:** #fluency-illusion #textbase-trap #metacognition #comprehension-monitoring

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 4 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What distinguishes comprehension failure from the Textbase Trap from comprehension failure from cognitive overload?

**A:** Cognitive overload failure (Mechanism 1) is experienced as difficulty — the reader knows they are struggling. The Textbase Trap (Mechanism 2) is experienced as confidence — the reader feels they understood. This is the critical diagnostic distinction: a reader who feels confused after reading may have an overload problem; a reader who feels confident but cannot answer inference questions has a Textbase Trap problem. The Textbase Trap produces "confident incomprehension."

**Source:** Phase IV, Mechanisms 1 and 2
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** #textbase-trap #cognitive-load #comprehension-failure #metacognition

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 5 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Generation Constraint on situation model construction?

**A:** The Generation Constraint states that situation models cannot be received through passive reading — they can only be produced through active construction. The cognitive work of generating causal inferences, bridging implicit gaps, and integrating prior knowledge is specifically the work that passive reading allows readers to avoid. This is why re-reading fails to repair comprehension and why post-reading free recall significantly outperforms re-reading for retention: retrieval practice forces the generative work that passive reading bypasses.

**Source:** Phase III, Evidence Area 3; Phase IV, Mechanism 4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Process
**Tags:** #generation-effect #generation-constraint #active-reading #retrieval-practice

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 6 — Application
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry, and what practical reading intervention does it most directly support?

**A:** The Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry states that situation model construction is directionally one-way: a structural frame can accept and organize details, but an accumulation of unframed details cannot generate a structural frame. This directly supports pre-reading scaffolding as a prerequisite (not merely a helpful addition) to content reading. Scanning structure, writing a prediction sentence, and formulating a purpose question before reading build the partial structural frame into which content-level propositions can attach. Without this frame, proposition accumulation produces a textbase but not a situation model.

**Source:** Phase VI, Original Synthesis; Phase V, Practical Applications
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Application
**Tags:** #frame-before-fill #advance-organizer #pre-reading #situation-model-construction

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 7 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does the generation effect in memory research (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) relate to the self-explanation effect in reading comprehension (Chi et al., 1989)?

**A:** Both establish the same structural principle — the Generation Constraint — from different methodological directions. Slamecka & Graf showed that words actively generated by participants were remembered better than identical words passively read, demonstrating the memory advantage of active production over passive reception. Chi et al. showed that students who explained worked examples during study — generating causal accounts rather than reading passively — built substantially better knowledge representations. Both converge on the claim that generation (producing new content from partial cues) is the mechanism that forces active encoding and model construction.

**Source:** Phase III, Evidence Areas 3 and 4
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Connection
**Tags:** #generation-effect #self-explanation #generation-constraint #learning-science

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 8 — Basic
> **A:** **Q:** What is the correct diagnostic question to ask when you feel "lost in details" during reading?

**A:** Not "which detail did I miss?" but "where did I lose the causal chain?" The experience of getting lost in details is the reader's awareness of CATSM incoherence — not a loss of any specific detail, but a loss of the causal spine that would give details their structural positions. The corrective is to locate the specific gap where an implicit causal inference was not generated, generate a tentative bridging inference, and allow the other CATSM dimensions to re-cohere around the reconstructed causal spine.

**Source:** Phase IV, Mechanism 3
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Type:** Application
**Tags:** #causal-spine #CATSM #reading-strategy #situation-model

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 9 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** Describe the Failure Cascade in situation model construction.

**A:** Working memory load from dense text reduces available resources for inference generation (Mechanism 1). Automatic textbase building proceeds fluently, producing a false comprehension signal (Mechanism 2, Textbase Trap). The false signal terminates active monitoring and inference generation before the causal spine is established (Mechanism 3). Without active inference generation, the CATSM dimensions remain incoherent (Mechanism 4). The reader progresses through the text building a rich textbase and a deficient situation model, confident throughout.

**Source:** Phase IV, Dynamic Interaction section
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Process
**Tags:** #failure-cascade #situation-model #mechanism #reading-comprehension

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Q:** Seed 10 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does the Frame-Before-Fill principle operate in software architecture design, and what does this analogy illuminate about reading comprehension?

**A:** In software architecture, developers who begin implementing components without first establishing a system-level architecture face the software equivalent of the textbase trap: working components that don't cohere into a working system. The architectural frame (system diagram, dependency map) gives component implementations a structural position — just as a pre-reading structural frame gives text propositions a structural position. The analogy illuminates that Frame-Before-Fill is a domain-independent cognitive construction principle, not a reading-specific technique. It applies wherever complex mental models must be built from detailed inputs.
