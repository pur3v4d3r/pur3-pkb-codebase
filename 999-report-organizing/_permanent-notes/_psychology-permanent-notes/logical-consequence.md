# Logical Consequence

> [!definition] **Logical Consequence**
> Logical Consequence is a fundamental relation in logic where the truth of premises guarantees the truth of a conclusion based solely on logical form rather than empirical evidence or specific contexts. This concept falls under the broader domain of Logic, and it excludes relations that are contingent upon empirical facts or particular circumstances.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes relations that are contingent upon empirical evidence or specific contexts, such as causation in scientific theories. It should not be confused with entailment in natural language semantics which can differ from formal logic's definition.

## Core Explanation

At its core, Logical Consequence is about ensuring that an argument's structure supports its conclusion in a way that is independent of the content or context. This means that if the premises are true, then the conclusion must also be true due to the logical form alone. The importance of this concept lies in its role as a cornerstone for validating arguments and theories within formal logic.

The precise relation that validity tracks is Logical Consequence; an argument is valid exactly when its conclusion follows from its premises as a matter of logical necessity, not empirical observation or specific circumstances. This relationship is crucial because every theory of logic inherently includes a theory of consequence, making it essential for rigorous reasoning and theoretical development.

Understanding the nuances of Logical Consequence requires delving into its theoretical roots in formal logic systems. Different frameworks such as classical, intuitionistic, and relevance logics offer distinct definitions and operationalizations of this concept, highlighting the variability across logical systems.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Formal Logic**
> In formal logic, Logical Consequence is used to determine whether an argument's conclusion necessarily follows from its premises. This ensures that conclusions are not merely probable but guaranteed by the logical structure of the argument. Ignoring this concept could lead to accepting invalid arguments as valid.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Argument Analysis**
> When analyzing arguments, recognizing Logical Consequence helps in identifying whether an argument is logically sound or if it relies on assumptions that are not supported by its premises. ## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Semantic vs Syntactic Definitions**
> The distinction between semantic and syntactic definitions of Logical Consequence is significant because it affects how we understand the relationship between premises and conclusions. Semantic approaches focus on truth preservation across all possible interpretations, while syntactic approaches rely on formal derivations within a logical system.

> [!key-distinction] **Semantic vs Syntactic Definitions**
> The semantic approach to Logical Consequence focuses on truth preservation across all possible interpretations, ensuring that if premises are true in any interpretation, the conclusion must also be true. In contrast, syntactic approaches rely on formal derivations within a logical system, emphasizing the rules and procedures for deriving conclusions from premises. This distinction is crucial because it affects how we understand the relationship between premises and conclusions, impacting both theoretical foundations and practical applications.

> [!key-distinction] **Classical vs Non-Classical Logics**
> While classical logic assumes bivalence (every statement is either true or false), non-classical logics such as intuitionistic and relevance logics offer alternative frameworks. In these systems, the definition of Logical Consequence can vary significantly, reflecting different philosophical stances on truth and implication. Understanding these differences helps in evaluating the applicability and limitations of logical arguments across various theoretical contexts.

## Common Misconceptions

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-13) -->

> [!warning] **Misconception** — People often think that if an argument is logically valid, it must also be true.
>
> This misconception arises from conflating validity with truth. An argument can be logically valid even if its premises are false or the conclusion is not actually true in reality. Validity only ensures that if the premises were true, then the conclusion would follow necessarily.
