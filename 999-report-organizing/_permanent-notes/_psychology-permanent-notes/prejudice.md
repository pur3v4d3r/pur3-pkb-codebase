# Prejudice

> [!definition] **Prejudice**
> Prejudice involves negatively-valenced attitudes toward individuals or groups based on perceived category membership, integrating affective (felt antipathy), cognitive (stereotyped beliefs), and behavioral (discrimination tendency) components — distinguished from stereotype (the cognitive content) and discrimination (the behavioral expression) by being the evaluative attitude that links them. It falls under [[Social Psychology]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This definition excludes stereotype (cognitive content) and discrimination (behavioral expression), focusing specifically on the evaluative attitude linking them.

## Core Explanation

Prejudice is a complex phenomenon rooted in social psychology, involving not just negative feelings but also stereotyped beliefs and tendencies to discriminate. These attitudes are often formed based on perceived group membership, leading individuals to hold biases that can influence their behavior towards others. The affective component of prejudice includes the emotional antipathy felt toward certain groups, which is closely tied to cognitive stereotypes about those groups.

In practice, prejudice operates in various ways. For instance, it might manifest as explicit beliefs that are consciously held and accessible through introspection, such as a person stating they dislike individuals from a particular racial background due to their perceived characteristics. However, prejudice can also operate implicitly, where automatic associations between certain groups and negative traits occur without conscious awareness. This dual nature of prejudice is crucial for understanding its impact on behavior and social structures.

Theoretical roots of prejudice lie in the study of social identity theory, which posits that individuals derive their self-concept from group membership and categorization. Prejudice arises when these categories are perceived as threatening or different, leading to negative attitudes towards out-groups. Self-categorization theory further elaborates on how individuals form cognitive boundaries between in-groups and out-groups, influencing the formation of prejudice.

Empirical research has shown that prejudice can have significant real-world implications. For example, in educational settings, teachers may unconsciously favor students from certain backgrounds, leading to disparities in academic support and opportunities. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing effective interventions aimed at reducing prejudice.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->
Prejudice often manifests in subtle ways that can be difficult to detect, such as microaggressions or unconscious biases. These forms of prejudice can perpetuate systemic inequalities even when individuals do not intend harm. For example, a hiring manager might unconsciously favor candidates from their own social group due to implicit associations formed through repeated exposure to societal stereotypes.

Recent research has highlighted the role of intergroup contact in reducing prejudice. When members of different groups interact under conditions that promote positive experiences and common goals, they are more likely to form favorable attitudes towards each other. This insight underscores the importance of fostering inclusive environments where diverse individuals can engage meaningfully.

## Mechanism

Prejudice operates through a dual process model where attitudes can be both explicitly endorsed and implicitly held. Explicit prejudice involves conscious beliefs that are accessible to introspection, while implicit prejudice refers to automatic associations that occur without awareness. Contemporary measurement programs treat these two forms as related but separable constructs, with joint patterning predicting behavior better than either alone.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding prejudice is crucial for creating inclusive learning environments. Teachers who are aware of their implicit biases can take steps to ensure that all students receive equal attention and support. For instance, using diverse teaching materials and actively monitoring classroom interactions can help mitigate the impact of unconscious prejudices on student outcomes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Employment practices**
> In employment settings, prejudice can lead to discriminatory hiring and promotion decisions. Companies must implement unbiased recruitment processes and provide training for managers to recognize and address their implicit biases. This ensures a fairer distribution of opportunities and reduces the risk of legal issues related to discrimination.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Healthcare**
> In healthcare, prejudice can affect patient care by leading to differential treatment based on perceived group membership. Healthcare providers must be trained to recognize their own biases and provide equitable care to all patients. This includes addressing implicit biases that may influence diagnostic decisions or treatment recommendations.

> [!example] **Application 4 — Spaced retrieval in MOOCs**
> In Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), spaced retrieval techniques can be used to reduce implicit biases among learners. By presenting diverse role models and scenarios at intervals, the course material helps reinforce positive intergroup attitudes over time. This approach leverages the cognitive benefits of distributed practice to counteract stereotypes that might otherwise persist.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Prejudice vs Stereotype**
> While both prejudice and stereotype involve negative attitudes towards groups, they differ in their mechanisms and impacts. Prejudice is the evaluative attitude that links stereotyped beliefs to behavior, whereas stereotypes are the cognitive content of these beliefs. Understanding this distinction helps in developing targeted interventions aimed at reducing prejudiced behaviors rather than just changing stereotypical thoughts.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!key-distinction] **Explicit vs Implicit Prejudice**
> While explicit prejudice involves conscious beliefs and attitudes, implicit prejudice refers to unconscious biases that influence behavior without awareness. Explicit prejudices are often easier to identify and address through direct interventions like education or training programs. In contrast, implicit prejudices require more subtle approaches such as exposure therapy or cognitive restructuring exercises.

> [!key-distinction] **Reflective vs Reactive Thinking**
> Prejudiced attitudes can be influenced by both reflective thinking, which involves deliberate consideration and evaluation of information, and reactive thinking, which is quick and automatic. Reflective processes are more likely to challenge and modify prejudiced beliefs, whereas reactive processes tend to reinforce existing biases based on immediate emotional responses.

## Common Misconceptions

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!warning] **Misconception** — People think that prejudice only affects individuals with negative attitudes.
>
> Prejudice can influence anyone's behavior and decision-making, regardless of their explicit beliefs. Even well-intentioned people may unconsciously act on implicit biases formed through repeated exposure to societal stereotypes. This misconception arises from the assumption that conscious awareness is sufficient to prevent prejudiced behaviors.

## Synthesis

Understanding prejudice is crucial for addressing social inequalities and promoting inclusive societies. By recognizing its affective, cognitive, and behavioral components, we can develop targeted interventions to reduce prejudiced attitudes and behaviors. This concept intersects with broader issues in social psychology, such as self-categorization theory and stereotype threat, highlighting the need for a multidisciplinary approach to tackle prejudice effectively.

Prejudice also has significant implications for fields like education, healthcare, and employment practices. Addressing this issue requires not only awareness but also concrete actions to create more equitable environments. By integrating theoretical insights with practical applications, we can work towards reducing the negative impacts of prejudice on individuals and society.

### Why these connections matter

> [!connection] **[[Social Identity Theory]]** — *applies-to*
> Social Identity Theory explains how individuals derive a sense of self-worth from their group memberships, which can lead to in-group favoritism and out-group discrimination. This theory provides a framework for understanding the psychological processes underlying prejudice by highlighting the role of social categorization in shaping intergroup attitudes.

> [!connection] **[[Self-Categorization Theory]]** — *applies-to*
> Self-Categorization Theory extends Social Identity Theory by emphasizing how individuals perceive themselves as members of various groups, which can influence their behavior and attitudes. This theory helps explain why people may adopt prejudiced views based on the social categories they identify with, even when these categories are not inherently negative.
