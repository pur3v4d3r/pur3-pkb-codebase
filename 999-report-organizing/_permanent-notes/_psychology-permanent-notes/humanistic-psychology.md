# Humanistic Psychology

> [!definition] **Humanistic Psychology**
> Humanistic Psychology is a mid-20th-century approach that emphasizes subjective experience, agency, meaning-making, self-actualization, and the conditions for psychological flourishing. It falls under [[Psychology]], establishing the conceptual ground from which Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Positive Psychology later emerged with empirical methodology.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes strict behaviorist or psychoanalytic perspectives but includes contemporary theories like Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Positive Psychology which build upon its core principles.

## Core Explanation

At its core, Humanistic Psychology posits that individuals have an inherent drive towards self-actualization, or reaching their full potential. This perspective emphasizes subjective experience and personal growth over external factors like behaviorism's stimulus-response paradigm or psychoanalysis' focus on unconscious conflicts.

The theory operates by recognizing the importance of a person’s subjective experiences in shaping their behavior and well-being. It argues that individuals are capable of making meaningful choices and have an intrinsic motivation to fulfill their potential, which is often stifled by external pressures and societal constraints.

Theoretical roots trace back to Carl Rogers' concept of 'core conditions,' such as empathy, congruence, and unconditional positive regard, which create a supportive environment for personal growth. Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs further delineates the stages through which individuals must progress to achieve self-actualization, emphasizing the importance of fulfilling lower-level needs before moving on to higher ones.

Empirically, while Humanistic Psychology’s classical formulations like Maslow's hierarchy and Rogers' core conditions are widely cited but weakly supported empirically in their original strict form, contemporary theories such as Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Positive Psychology have built upon these ideas with more robust empirical support. SDT, for instance, focuses on the role of intrinsic motivation in fostering psychological well-being.

## Mechanism

The process by which individuals move towards self-actualization involves a series of steps. First, they must create an environment that supports their growth, characterized by conditions like empathy and congruence. Second, they need to identify and address their core needs, starting from the most basic physiological ones and moving upwards through safety, love, esteem, and finally self-actualization.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Humanistic Psychology suggests that learning environments should be supportive and empowering. By fostering a sense of belonging and providing opportunities for personal growth, educators can enhance students' motivation and engagement, leading to better academic outcomes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Therapy**
> In therapy, the principles of Humanistic Psychology encourage therapists to create a non-judgmental space where clients can explore their feelings freely. This approach helps clients develop self-awareness and make meaningful changes in their lives, ultimately promoting psychological well-being.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Education policy**
> At the policy level, incorporating Humanistic Psychology into education systems could lead to more student-centered approaches that prioritize individual needs and growth over standardized testing. Such policies would foster a more holistic educational experience, enhancing students' overall development.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Humanistic Psychology emphasizes intrinsic motivation, which arises from internal desires for personal growth and self-actualization, as opposed to extraneous load, which refers to external pressures that can hinder this process. Understanding the difference is crucial in designing educational and therapeutic interventions that support genuine personal development.

## Key Figures

- **Carl Rogers** — Rogers introduced the concept of 'core conditions,' such as empathy, congruence, and unconditional positive regard, which create a supportive environment for personal growth.
- **Abraham Maslow** — Maslow developed the hierarchy of needs, outlining the stages through which individuals must progress to achieve self-actualization, emphasizing the importance of fulfilling lower-level needs before moving on to higher ones.

## Open Questions

> What is the empirical support for the core concepts of Humanistic Psychology?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical studies that rigorously test and validate the claims made by Humanistic Psychology, such as the role of intrinsic motivation in fostering psychological well-being, would provide clearer evidence.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can the principles of Humanistic Psychology be better integrated into mainstream psychological practice?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Developing more empirical research that demonstrates the practical benefits of applying Humanistic principles in various settings could help integrate these concepts more effectively into professional psychology.

## Synthesis

Humanistic Psychology matters because it provides a framework for understanding and promoting psychological well-being based on individual agency and subjective experience. By emphasizing intrinsic motivation, self-actualization, and the conditions that foster personal growth, this approach complements other psychological theories like SDT and Positive Psychology. Its principles can be applied in various domains such as education, therapy, and policy-making to enhance human potential and quality of life.

The integration of Humanistic Psychology with contemporary empirical research not only enriches our understanding of human behavior but also offers practical tools for improving individual and societal well-being.
