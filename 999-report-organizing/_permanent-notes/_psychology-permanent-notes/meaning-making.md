# Meaning-Making

> [!definition] **Meaning-Making**
> Meaning-Making is the cognitive and narrative process by which a person integrates discrepant or disruptive experiences into a coherent autobiographical and conceptual framework, restoring a sense of comprehensibility, significance, and purpose that these experiences had threatened. It falls under [[Cognitive Architecture]], as it involves the integration of new information with existing knowledge structures to maintain psychological well-being.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes specific therapeutic techniques or interventions but includes broader narrative and cognitive processes involved in making sense of life events.

## Core Explanation

Meaning-Making is a fundamental cognitive process through which individuals make sense of life's disruptions by integrating them into their broader life narratives. This process allows people to restore a sense of purpose and significance, even in the face of challenging experiences such as loss or trauma. By attributing meaning to these events, individuals can maintain psychological resilience and adapt more effectively.

The core mechanism involves both situational and global levels of meaning-making. At the situational level, an individual appraises a specific event, assessing its impact on their life. This appraisal is then integrated into a broader conceptual framework at the global level, where it influences personal beliefs and goals. Successful adaptation often requires updating one or both of these levels rather than maintaining the unchanged validity of either.

Theoretical roots of Meaning-Making can be traced back to cognitive psychology, particularly in the work of John Sweller, who highlighted the importance of working memory in this process. Sweller's research on cognitive load theory suggests that integrating new experiences into existing knowledge structures requires significant mental effort, which is supported by working memory.

Empirical evidence from studies on trauma and loss has shown that individuals who engage in meaningful integration of disruptive events tend to experience better psychological outcomes compared to those who do not. For instance, research indicates that patients who are able to construct coherent narratives around their traumatic experiences often exhibit lower levels of PTSD symptoms.

Meaning-Making is not merely a passive process but an active one, requiring individuals to engage in cognitive effort to construct narratives that make sense of their experiences. This engagement can be seen as a form of problem-solving where the 'problem' is the disruption or anomaly presented by life events. By actively constructing meaning, individuals are able to transform potentially distressing situations into opportunities for personal growth and learning.

## Mechanism

The process of Meaning-Making involves several stages. Initially, an individual must appraise the disruptive event, evaluating its impact on their life and emotional state. This appraisal is then integrated into a broader narrative framework, where it influences personal beliefs and goals. Successful integration requires updating both situational and global levels of meaning, rather than maintaining the unchanged validity of either.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In educational settings, Meaning-Making can be applied to enhance learning and retention by encouraging students to integrate new information into their existing knowledge frameworks. This approach helps students construct coherent narratives around the material they are studying, leading to better long-term recall and understanding.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Therapy**
> In therapeutic contexts, Meaning-Making is crucial for helping clients process traumatic experiences. By guiding clients through a structured narrative integration process, therapists can help them restore a sense of purpose and significance, which is essential for recovery and resilience.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Personal development**
> Individuals can apply Meaning-Making principles in their daily lives to navigate challenges more effectively. By reflecting on disruptive events and integrating them into their life narratives, individuals can maintain psychological well-being and foster personal growth.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Situational vs Global meaning-making**
> Situational Meaning-Making focuses on the appraisal of a specific event, while Global Meaning-Making involves integrating this event into broader beliefs and goals. The distinction is crucial because successful adaptation often requires updating both levels rather than maintaining the unchanged validity of either.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!key-distinction] **Surface vs Deep Processing in Meaning-Making**
> In cognitive psychology, surface processing involves superficial engagement with information, focusing on its immediate characteristics without deeper understanding. In contrast, deep processing entails a more thorough analysis that integrates new information into existing knowledge structures. For Meaning-Making, engaging in deep processing is crucial as it allows individuals to integrate disruptive experiences not just at the surface level but also by connecting them to broader life narratives and personal goals.

## Common Misconceptions

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!warning] **Misconception** — People often believe that meaning-making is solely an individual process, occurring entirely within one's mind.
>
> While Meaning-Making does involve internal cognitive processes, it also heavily relies on social and cultural contexts. Individuals often seek validation or feedback from others to confirm the coherence of their constructed meanings, indicating a significant role for external interactions in this process.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — Sweller's work on cognitive load theory provided foundational insights into how working memory supports Meaning-Making processes, particularly in educational and therapeutic contexts.
