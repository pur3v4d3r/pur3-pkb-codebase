# Critical Thinking

> [!definition] **Critical Thinking**
> Critical Thinking is the disciplined process of actively conceptualising, applying, analysing, synthesising, and evaluating information gathered from observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication as a guide to belief and action — a definition that integrates cognitive skills (analysis, evaluation, inference, interpretation, explanation, self-regulation) with dispositional habits of mind (truth-seeking, open-mindedness, intellectual humility, systematicity, analyticity); it falls under [[cognitive-architecture]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes mere opinion or unstructured thinking but includes structured reasoning processes. It should not be confused with informal logic alone, which focuses more on argument analysis without the broader cognitive skills and dispositions.

## Core Explanation

Critical Thinking is a multifaceted cognitive process that integrates both skills and dispositions. At its core, it involves the active engagement with information to form reasoned beliefs or actions. This integration means that while one can have strong analytical abilities without the necessary truth-seeking disposition, such an individual might produce sophistry rather than sound reasoning.

In practice, Critical Thinking operates through a series of structured steps: gathering and processing information, analysing it for validity and relevance, synthesising insights from diverse sources, evaluating evidence to form reasoned judgments, and then acting on those judgments. These steps are not merely mechanical; they require the individual to engage with their own biases and assumptions, fostering intellectual humility and open-mindedness.

Theoretical roots of Critical Thinking can be traced back to both cognitive skills traditions (such as those proposed by Ennis and Facione) and dispositional traditions (like those developed by Paul and Elder). The former focuses on the procedural aspects of reasoning, while the latter emphasizes the personal qualities that enable effective reasoning. Together, these perspectives provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how individuals can develop robust cognitive skills alongside virtuous dispositions.

Empirical evidence supports the importance of integrating both cognitive skills and dispositional habits in Critical Thinking education. For instance, studies have shown that students who are taught to engage with information critically through structured exercises tend to exhibit better reasoning abilities than those who receive only rote instruction or unstructured thinking training.

Critical Thinking is not merely a set of skills but also requires a mindset that values evidence and reasoning over intuition or bias. This disposition towards truth-seeking can be cultivated through deliberate practice, such as engaging in debates or reflective journaling about one's thought processes.

## Mechanism

The process of Critical Thinking can be broken down into several stages: initial observation and experience, followed by active conceptualisation where the individual forms mental models. Analysis involves breaking down these models to understand their components, while evaluation assesses the validity and reliability of the information. Inference then synthesises this analysis and evaluation to form reasoned judgments or beliefs.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design for Critical Thinking, educators must create environments that encourage both cognitive skills like analysis and evaluation, as well as dispositions such as truth-seeking. For example, case studies can be used to teach students how to apply these skills in real-world scenarios, fostering a deeper understanding of the material.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Decision-making**
> In decision-making processes, Critical Thinking ensures that decisions are based on sound reasoning rather than personal biases or incomplete information. By actively analysing and evaluating options, individuals can make more informed choices that align with their goals and values.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Problem-solving**
> Critical Thinking is essential in problem-solving as it allows individuals to break down complex issues into manageable parts, evaluate different solutions, and synthesise insights to find effective resolutions. This approach ensures that problems are addressed systematically rather than through hasty or superficial means.

> [!example] **Application 4 — Spaced retrieval in MOOCs**
> In Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), spaced retrieval techniques can enhance Critical Thinking by encouraging students to revisit and re-evaluate concepts over time. This approach helps solidify understanding and promotes the application of knowledge across different contexts, thereby fostering deeper critical analysis.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of a task, while extraneous load is the unnecessary cognitive burden introduced by poor instructional design. Critical Thinking involves managing both loads effectively; it is not merely about reducing extraneous load but also enhancing intrinsic cognitive skills and dispositions.

> [!key-distinction] **Critical Thinking vs Sophistry**
> While sophistry can involve clever or persuasive arguments, it often lacks the rigorous analysis and evaluation that characterise Critical Thinking. Sophists may use fallacies to persuade their audience, whereas Critical Thinkers strive for truth and accuracy in their reasoning.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!key-distinction] **Reflective vs Reactive Thinking**
> Reflective thinking involves deliberate consideration and evaluation of information before forming judgments or taking actions. In contrast, reactive thinking is immediate and often based on instinct or habit. Critical Thinking emphasizes reflective processes to ensure that decisions are well-reasoned and evidence-based.

## Common Misconceptions

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!warning] **Misconception** — Critical Thinking means always disagreeing with others.
>
> This misconception arises from the belief that critical thinkers must constantly challenge or refute ideas. In reality, Critical Thinking involves evaluating arguments objectively and constructively, whether they align with one's own views or not.

## Key Figures

- **Richard Paul** — Co-founder of the Paul-Elder framework, which integrates cognitive skills with dispositional habits to define Critical Thinking.
- **Linda Elder** — Co-founder of the Paul-Elder framework, contributing significantly to the development and popularisation of Critical Thinking as a structured approach to reasoning.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->
- **Daniel Kahneman** — Kahneman's work on System 1 and System 2 Thinking provides critical insights into how Critical Thinking operates. His research highlights the importance of engaging the slower, more deliberate System 2 processes to overcome biases inherent in quick, intuitive judgments.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What evidence supports the claim that Critical Thinking instruction produces broad-spectrum gains?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical studies demonstrating long-term transfer effects across different domains would provide robust evidence for this claim.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can educators ensure that Critical Thinking skills are effectively transferred across different domains?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Developing pedagogical strategies that explicitly link critical thinking skills to real-world applications and providing opportunities for practice in diverse contexts could help address this issue.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does cultural context influence the development and expression of Critical Thinking skills?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Cross-cultural studies examining how different societal values and educational practices shape reasoning abilities would provide valuable insights into this question. Such research could help tailor instructional approaches to better support Critical Thinking across diverse populations.

## Synthesis

Critical Thinking matters because it equips individuals with the cognitive tools necessary to navigate complex information environments, make informed decisions, and solve problems effectively. By integrating both cognitive skills and dispositions, Critical Thinking fosters a more nuanced and reflective approach to reasoning that is essential in today's rapidly changing world.

The concept of Critical Thinking also has broader implications for educational psychology, as it underscores the importance of developing not just knowledge but also the ability to apply that knowledge effectively. This aligns with the idea of working memory, which plays a crucial role in processing and retaining information during reasoning tasks.
