# Myside Bias

> [!definition] **Myside Bias**
> Myside Bias is a cognitive tendency where individuals generate, evaluate, and remember evidence in ways that favor their prior opinions, group commitments, or personal stake in an argument. It falls under reasoning biases but should not be confused with belief bias, which specifically targets the confirmation of pre-existing beliefs over logical reasoning.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It should not be confused with belief bias, which specifically refers to a preference for confirming pre-existing beliefs over logical reasoning. Myside Bias encompasses a broader range of cognitive processes that skew towards supporting one's own viewpoint.

## Core Explanation

Myside Bias is a pervasive cognitive phenomenon where individuals selectively seek out and interpret information that aligns with their existing views while disregarding or dismissing contradictory evidence. This selective processing can occur at various stages, from initial data collection to the final evaluation of conclusions. The bias operates not just in conscious decision-making but also influences unconscious thought processes, making it particularly insidious.

Theoretical roots of Myside Bias trace back to cognitive dissonance theory and motivated reasoning, both suggesting that individuals are driven by a need for consistency between their beliefs and actions. This theoretical framework explains why people often exhibit myside bias even when presented with compelling counter-evidence; the psychological discomfort of holding contradictory views can be more aversive than adjusting one's stance.

Empirical studies have shown that Myside Bias is not confined to individuals with lower cognitive abilities but affects all levels of intelligence. A key claim about Myside Bias is that it is dissociable from general cognitive ability; high-IQ reasoners exhibit the bias at roughly the same rate as average reasoners, indicating that intelligence alone does not protect against it and may even equip sophisticated rationalizations to support one's preconceived notions.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional settings, Myside Bias can undermine the effectiveness of learning materials by leading educators and students alike to favor information that aligns with their preconceived notions. For instance, a teacher might unconsciously emphasize evidence supporting their preferred theory while neglecting contradictory data. To mitigate this, instructional designers should incorporate diverse perspectives and encourage critical thinking exercises that challenge learners' existing beliefs.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Policy-making**
> Myside Bias can significantly impact policy decisions by causing policymakers to favor information that supports their pre-existing views or political agendas over objective evidence. This can lead to the implementation of ineffective policies based on flawed reasoning. To address this, it is crucial for decision-makers to engage in structured deliberation processes that include diverse viewpoints and rigorous fact-checking.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Myside Bias vs Belief Bias**
> While Myside Bias encompasses a broader range of cognitive processes that skew towards supporting one's own viewpoint, belief bias specifically refers to the preference for confirming pre-existing beliefs over logical reasoning. Understanding this distinction is crucial as it highlights the need for different strategies in addressing each type of bias.

## Synthesis

Understanding Myside Bias is crucial for cognitive science because it illuminates how deeply ingrained our biases are in shaping not just what we believe but also how we process information. This insight has profound implications across various domains, from education and policy-making to personal decision-making, underscoring the need for strategies that foster critical thinking and open-mindedness.
