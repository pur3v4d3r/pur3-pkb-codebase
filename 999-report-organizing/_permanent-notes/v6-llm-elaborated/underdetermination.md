# Underdetermination

> [!definition] **Underdetermination**
> Underdetermination refers to the philosophical thesis that for any finite body of empirical evidence, there exist multiple incompatible theories that fit the evidence equally well, suggesting data alone cannot uniquely select a theory. It falls under [[Scientific Realism]], challenging the idea that we can have justified beliefs in unobservable entities based solely on empirical evidence.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes specific examples or individual theories but focuses on the broader principle of how empirical evidence and theoretical selection interact in scientific practice.

## Core Explanation

Underdetermination arises from the fit between theories and data, where multiple incompatible theories can explain the same set of observations equally well. This phenomenon is significant for scientific realism because it suggests that even if a theory fits the available data perfectly, we cannot be certain that this theory is true or that its unobservable entities exist.

In practice, underdetermination occurs through curve-fitting and unconceived alternatives. For instance, when fitting a set of experimental results to various mathematical functions, different theories can produce identical predictions, making it impossible to determine which one is correct based on the data alone. Similarly, there may be alternative theories that have not yet been conceived but could also explain the same evidence.

Theoretical roots and conceptual nuances of underdetermination trace back to philosophers like Willard Van Orman Quine and Pierre Duhem. Quine's strong reading posits that all extant scientific theories have serious unconceived rivals, while Duhem’s weaker version suggests any finite data are formally compatible with infinitely many curve-fits. The dispute over which reading the historical record supports remains live.

Empirical or historical grounding includes cases like the Ptolemaic and Copernican systems, where both could explain the same astronomical observations equally well until new evidence emerged to favor one over the other. This highlights that underdetermination is not just a theoretical construct but has real implications for how we understand scientific progress.

## Mechanism

Underdetermination occurs through curve-fitting, where multiple theories can fit the same data equally well. For example, fitting a set of points to different polynomial functions results in various curves that all predict the same outcomes. This demonstrates that empirical evidence alone cannot distinguish between these competing theories.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, underdetermination implies that multiple teaching methods can achieve similar learning outcomes based on the available data. However, choosing one method over another requires considering non-empirical factors such as simplicity and pedagogical effectiveness.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Medical diagnosis**
> In medical diagnosis, different diseases may present with identical symptoms, making it impossible to determine the correct diagnosis based solely on clinical data. This underscores the need for additional diagnostic tools and non-empirical criteria like patient history and physical examination.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Weak vs Strong Readings**
> The weak reading of underdetermination, associated with Duhem, states that any finite data are formally compatible with infinitely many curve-fits. The strong reading, attributed to Quine, posits that all extant scientific theories have serious unconceived rivals that fit the same evidence. The distinction matters because the strong reading challenges scientific realism more directly.

## Key Figures

- **Willard Van Orman Quine** — Quine is known for his strong reading of underdetermination, which posits that all extant scientific theories have serious unconceived rivals. This view has significant implications for the philosophy of science and scientific realism.
- **Pierre Duhem** — Duhem introduced the weaker version of underdetermination, suggesting that any finite data are formally compatible with infinitely many curve-fits. His work laid foundational groundwork for understanding how empirical evidence alone is insufficient to uniquely select a theory.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Which reading of underdetermination (weak or strong) is more supported by historical evidence?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Historical case studies and detailed analyses of scientific developments could provide insights into which version better reflects the actual history of science.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can non-empirical criteria alone justify the selection of one theory over another?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical tests and philosophical arguments that evaluate the role of simplicity, fertility, and conservatism in theory selection could help resolve this question.

## Synthesis

Understanding underdetermination is crucial for evaluating scientific theories because it highlights the limitations of empirical evidence alone. It challenges us to consider non-empirical criteria such as simplicity, fertility, and conservatism when selecting among competing theories. This concept also intersects with related concepts like scientific realism, theory-ladenness of observation, fallibilism, and inference-to-the-best-explanation, underscoring its importance in the broader landscape of philosophy of science.
