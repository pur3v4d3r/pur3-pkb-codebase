# Heuristics and Biases

> [!definition] **Heuristics and Biases**
> Heuristics and Biases is a research program initiated by Tversky and Kahneman in the early 1970s, which documents how human judgment systematically deviates from probability theory due to reliance on mental shortcuts (heuristics) like representativeness or availability. It falls under cognitive architecture as it elucidates the mechanisms of decision-making that are both efficient and prone to systematic errors.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes individual heuristics like representativeness or availability but focuses on their collective impact on decision-making. It also does not cover all aspects of cognitive psychology, such as motivation or emotion.

## Core Explanation

Heuristics and Biases fundamentally challenges the notion that human judgment is always rational and unbiased. By relying on mental shortcuts, individuals often make decisions based on heuristics such as representativeness (judging probability by how similar a case is to a prototype) or availability (estimating frequency or likelihood based on how easily examples come to mind). These heuristics are efficient but can lead to systematic biases and errors in judgment.

The program's empirical findings, detailed in Kahneman’s *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, show that even trained professionals like statisticians, physicians, and judges fall prey to these biases. For instance, the anchoring heuristic causes people to rely too heavily on initial information when making decisions, leading to overestimation or underestimation of probabilities. This is not due to a lack of education but rather a structural feature of how our minds process information.

Theoretical roots of Heuristics and Biases can be traced back to the dual-process theory, which posits that decision-making involves two types of cognitive processes: System 1 (fast, intuitive) and System 2 (slow, analytical). Heuristics are primarily associated with System 1 thinking, which is prone to biases due to its reliance on mental shortcuts. This duality explains why even experts can be misled by heuristics in certain contexts.

Empirically, the program has been grounded in numerous experiments that demonstrate these biases across various domains. For example, the famous Linda problem shows how people tend to overestimate the probability of a specific scenario (Linda is a bank teller and feminist) compared to a more general one (Linda is a bank teller), illustrating the representativeness heuristic at work.

## Mechanism

The mechanism behind heuristics involves cognitive shortcuts that simplify complex decision-making processes. For instance, the availability heuristic relies on the ease with which examples come to mind, often leading to overestimation of rare events because they are more memorable. Similarly, the anchoring heuristic causes individuals to fixate on initial information and adjust their judgments accordingly, even if this adjustment is insufficient.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding heuristics can help create more effective learning materials. For example, avoiding anchoring biases by providing balanced information and not presenting initial data that could unduly influence learners' judgments is crucial.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Risk assessment in finance**
> Financial analysts must be aware of the representativeness heuristic to avoid overestimating the likelihood of rare but impactful events. By recognizing this bias, they can develop more robust risk management strategies and avoid underestimating potential losses.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Heuristics and Biases is not about intrinsic cognitive load (the inherent difficulty of a task) but rather the extraneous load introduced by heuristics. In contrast, intrinsic load affects all tasks equally, whereas extraneous load can be mitigated through better instructional design or decision-making strategies.

## Key Figures

- **Amos Tversky** — Tversky was a co-initiator of the Heuristics and Biases program, conducting extensive research on cognitive biases and their impact on judgment. His work laid the empirical foundation for understanding how heuristics lead to systematic errors.
- **Daniel Kahneman** — Kahneman is another co-initiator who popularized Heuristics and Biases through his book *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. His work has been instrumental in bringing these findings into the public domain and influencing various fields from psychology to economics.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term effects of heuristics and biases on decision-making?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies tracking individuals over extended periods could provide insights into how repeated exposure to cognitive biases shapes decision-making patterns.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can we design interventions to mitigate cognitive biases?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Experimental designs that test the efficacy of various cognitive forcing functions and mindware in real-world settings would help identify effective strategies for mitigating biases.

## Synthesis

Heuristics and Biases is a foundational concept in cognitive psychology because it provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how human judgment systematically deviates from rationality. By elucidating the mechanisms of heuristics, this program has paved the way for dual-process theory and bounded rationality, which are crucial for developing more accurate models of decision-making. Its practical implications span various fields, from education to finance, making it essential knowledge for anyone involved in human-centered design or analysis.

The ongoing research into Heuristics and Biases continues to refine our understanding of cognitive biases and their impact on judgment. By addressing open questions such as the long-term effects of these biases and developing effective interventions, we can improve decision-making processes across diverse domains.
