# Overjustification Effect

> [!definition] **Overjustification Effect**
> The Overjustification Effect is the phenomenon where offering tangible rewards for an already intrinsically motivated activity can reduce subsequent intrinsic motivation when the reward is removed. It falls under [[Motivation Research]], as it explores how external incentives can undermine internal engagement, a key area of study in understanding human behavior and motivation.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept focuses on the reduction of intrinsic motivation due to external rewards and does not cover other motivational theories or phenomena like self-efficacy or goal-setting theory.

## Core Explanation

The Overjustification Effect was first demonstrated by Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973) through experiments where children who were initially intrinsically motivated to draw were later given rewards for their drawings. When the reward was removed, these children showed a decrease in intrinsic motivation compared to those who had not received any tangible incentives. This suggests that when an activity is already driven by internal satisfaction, external rewards can shift its perceived value from being self-endorsed to instrumentally motivated.

This effect operates on the principle of cognitive reframing: once an activity becomes associated with a reward, it loses some of its intrinsic appeal. The children in Lepper et al.'s study began to view drawing as something they did for the prize rather than for the joy of creating art itself. This shift from self-endorsed to instrumentally motivated behavior can be observed when the external incentive is removed, leading to a decline in engagement.

Theoretical roots of this effect lie within Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which posits that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are fundamental needs for intrinsic motivation. The Overjustification Effect challenges the assumption that rewards always enhance behavior by showing how they can undermine these core needs when applied to already intrinsically motivated activities.

Empirical evidence supporting this effect comes from meta-analyses such as those conducted by Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999), which indicate that while tangible rewards can sometimes decrease intrinsic motivation, informational feedback for high-quality performance does not produce the same negative effects. This suggests that the nature of the reward—whether it is controlling or informational—is crucial in determining its impact on intrinsic motivation.

## Mechanism

The mechanism behind the Overjustification Effect involves cognitive reframing, where an activity becomes perceived as a means to an end (instrumentally motivated) rather than an end in itself (self-endorsed). When rewards are introduced, they can alter how individuals perceive their actions, leading to a shift from intrinsic to extrinsic motivation.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In educational settings, the Overjustification Effect implies that offering tangible rewards for already intrinsically motivated tasks can backfire. For instance, if students are initially engaged in a project because they find it interesting but then receive grades or prizes, their intrinsic motivation might decrease when these incentives are removed. Educators should therefore focus on maintaining intrinsic engagement through meaningful and challenging activities rather than relying solely on external rewards.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Workplace management**
> Managers in the workplace can apply this concept by recognizing that offering bonuses or other tangible rewards for tasks employees already enjoy can diminish their intrinsic motivation. Instead, they should focus on fostering a work environment where tasks are engaging and rewarding in themselves, thereby maintaining high levels of intrinsic motivation.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> The Overjustification Effect is distinct from the concept of intrinsic versus extraneous load. Intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of a task, while extraneous load includes any unnecessary cognitive demands imposed by external factors like rewards or instructions. The effect specifically addresses how external rewards can increase extraneous load and thus reduce intrinsic motivation.

## Key Figures

- **Mark Lepper** — Lepper was a key researcher in the initial demonstration of the Overjustification Effect through his experiments with children's drawing activities, which provided empirical evidence for this phenomenon.
- **David Greene** — Greene contributed to the foundational research on the Overjustification Effect by co-authoring studies that explored how external rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation in various contexts.
- **Richard Nisbett** — Nisbett played a crucial role in establishing the Overjustification Effect through his collaborative work with Lepper and Greene, which highlighted the importance of cognitive reframing in understanding motivational dynamics.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term effects of the Overjustification Effect?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies tracking individuals over extended periods could provide insights into whether and how the Overjustification Effect influences long-term motivation.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can the effect be mitigated by certain types of rewards?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Research examining different types of rewards, such as informational versus controlling feedback, could help determine if some forms of reward are less likely to produce the Overjustification Effect.

## Synthesis

Understanding the Overjustification Effect is crucial for educators and managers because it highlights the importance of fostering intrinsic motivation rather than relying solely on external rewards. By recognizing how external incentives can undermine internal engagement, professionals in these fields can design more effective strategies to maintain high levels of motivation among students and employees.

The Overjustification Effect also intersects with broader concepts like Self-Determination Theory and Cognitive Evaluation Theory, which provide frameworks for understanding the complex interplay between intrinsic and extrinsic motivators. By integrating insights from these theories, researchers and practitioners can develop more nuanced approaches to enhancing motivation in various contexts.
