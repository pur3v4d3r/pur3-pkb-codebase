# Knowledge of Cognition

> [!definition] **Knowledge of Cognition**
> Knowledge of cognition is the durable, long-term-memory representation a learner carries about cognitive functioning, partitioned into person, task, and strategy knowledge. It falls under [[Self-Regulated Learning]], providing the foundation for strategic reading but distinct from real-time regulation of cognition or introspective accuracy in laboratory settings.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It does not include real-time regulation of cognition or introspective accuracy in laboratory settings. It is distinct from metacognitive monitoring alone, which must be grounded in this knowledge base to produce strategic reading.

## Core Explanation

Knowledge of cognition encompasses three subtypes: person knowledge (understanding oneself as a learner), task knowledge (grasping the demands different cognitive tasks place on the reader), and strategy knowledge (knowing when to apply specific strategies). This knowledge is crucial for effective reading, as it enables readers to match their monitoring signals with appropriate regulatory responses.

In practice, person knowledge helps readers recognize their strengths and weaknesses, allowing them to adapt their approach based on their current state. Task knowledge informs readers about the cognitive demands of different texts, enabling them to adjust their strategies accordingly. Strategy knowledge is particularly important as it involves knowing when and why each strategy should be used, which is often the most challenging aspect to acquire.

Theoretical roots of this concept can be traced back to John Flavell's 1979 work on metacognitive awareness, where he introduced the idea that learners need a robust knowledge base to engage in strategic reading. Later, Brown (1987) extended this framework by distinguishing between declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge, with conditional knowledge being the most critical for effective reading comprehension.

Empirical evidence supports the importance of these subtypes. For instance, reciprocal teaching strategies, developed by Palincsar & Brown (1984), demonstrate how explicit instruction in person, task, and strategy knowledge can significantly enhance reading comprehension. These strategies involve predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing, all of which rely on a solid foundation of cognitive knowledge.

## Mechanism

Conditional knowledge — knowing when and why each strategy fits — is the most predictive of outcomes because it requires accurate calibration through feedback. However, this feedback is rarely supplied by ordinary reading, making conditional knowledge particularly resistant to acquisition. The architecture of knowledge of cognition serves as a diagnostic tool for identifying reading failures, allowing readers to ask structured questions about their monitoring processes and strategies.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, incorporating explicit instruction in person, task, and strategy knowledge can significantly enhance reading comprehension. For example, reciprocal teaching strategies that include predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing help students develop a deeper understanding of cognitive processes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Reading interventions**
> For reading interventions aimed at struggling readers, focusing on building person, task, and strategy knowledge can lead to more effective outcomes. By providing targeted instruction in these areas, educators can help learners become more strategic readers who are better equipped to handle a variety of texts.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Teacher training**
> Teacher training programs should include modules on knowledge of cognition to ensure that teachers have the necessary skills to teach their students effectively. This includes understanding how to assess and develop each type of cognitive knowledge in their students.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Knowledge of Cognition vs. Metacognitive Monitoring**
> While metacognitive monitoring involves real-time regulation of cognition, knowledge of cognition is the durable representation a learner carries about cognitive functioning. Knowledge of cognition provides the basis for effective metacognitive monitoring but operates on different timescales and serves distinct functions.

## Key Figures

- **John Flavell** — Flavell is credited with originating the concept of knowledge of cognition in his 1979 work, which laid the foundation for understanding how learners can engage in strategic reading.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can conditional knowledge be effectively taught and acquired?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further research on instructional methods that provide consistent feedback and opportunities for practice could help resolve this question.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the best methods for assessing a learner's knowledge of cognition?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Developing standardized assessment tools that accurately measure declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge would be crucial in addressing this open question.

## Synthesis

Understanding knowledge of cognition is crucial for improving reading comprehension and strategic learning because it provides the necessary foundation for effective metacognitive monitoring. By integrating person, task, and strategy knowledge into instructional practices, educators can help learners become more self-regulated readers who are better equipped to handle a variety of texts.

This concept also has broader implications across cognitive psychology and education, as it highlights the importance of explicit instruction in cognitive processes for developing strategic reading skills. By addressing open questions related to teaching and assessment methods, we can further enhance our understanding and application of knowledge of cognition.
