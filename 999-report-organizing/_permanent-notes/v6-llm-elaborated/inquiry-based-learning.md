# Inquiry-Based Learning

> [!definition] **Inquiry-Based Learning**
> Inquiry-Based Learning (IBL) is an instructional approach where learners drive their own learning by formulating questions, gathering evidence, and constructing explanations under varying degrees of teacher guidance, ranging from highly structured guided inquiry to open inquiry that approaches authentic disciplinary practice. It falls under [[self-regulated-learning]], emphasizing the learner's active role in the educational process.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes other forms of student-centered learning such as problem-based learning or project-based learning, which may not involve the same level of structured inquiry processes.

## Core Explanation

At its core, IBL is a student-centered approach where learners take an active role in their own learning by formulating questions and seeking answers through investigation and analysis. This method encourages critical thinking, problem-solving skills, and self-directed learning. The level of teacher guidance can vary widely, from highly structured guided inquiry to more open-ended exploration that mimics real-world research scenarios.

In practice, IBL operates in a cyclical manner: learners begin by posing questions or problems, gather relevant information through various means such as experiments, observations, and literature reviews, analyze the data collected, and then construct explanations based on their findings. This process often involves collaboration with peers and feedback from instructors to refine understanding and improve the quality of inquiry.

Theoretical roots of IBL can be traced back to constructivist learning theories, which posit that learners build knowledge through experience and reflection. IBL aligns closely with these principles by fostering an environment where students actively engage in constructing their own understanding rather than passively receiving information from a teacher. The concept also draws on the idea of self-regulated learning, where learners take responsibility for managing their own learning processes.

Empirical evidence supports the effectiveness of IBL, particularly when it includes explicit scaffolding and guidance. Meta-analyses (Furtak et al., 2012; Lazonder & Harmsen, 2016) have shown that structured and guided variants of IBL consistently outperform unguided inquiry in terms of learning outcomes.

## Mechanism

The cognitive processes involved in IBL include metacognition (thinking about one's own thinking), self-regulation (managing the learning process effectively), and problem-solving. Learners must engage in these processes to formulate questions, gather evidence, and construct explanations. The level of guidance provided by instructors plays a crucial role in facilitating or hindering these cognitive activities.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In IBL classrooms, instructional design must balance the need for structured guidance with opportunities for open inquiry. Teachers should provide scaffolding to help students navigate complex problems and ensure that they are on track to achieve learning objectives. For example, a teacher might start by providing a framework or guiding questions before allowing students to explore topics independently.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Student engagement**
> IBL can significantly enhance student engagement as it aligns with the natural curiosity and desire for discovery that many learners possess. However, this approach requires careful planning to ensure that learning outcomes are not compromised by a focus solely on engagement. Teachers must design activities that challenge students while providing sufficient support.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Assessment**
> Assessing student performance in IBL settings can be more complex than traditional methods, as it involves evaluating the quality of inquiry processes and the depth of understanding rather than just correct answers. Teachers need to develop rubrics that assess both the process (e.g., formulation of questions, data collection) and the product (e.g., explanations based on evidence).

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> In IBL, intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of the task itself, while extraneous load includes unnecessary cognitive demands imposed by the learning environment. Structured inquiry helps manage extraneous load by providing clear guidance and support, whereas unstructured inquiry can lead to confusion and frustration due to excessive extraneous load.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is credited with the origin of IBL in his work on cognitive load theory (1988). His research highlighted the importance of structured guidance to facilitate effective learning, which has been foundational for understanding how to implement IBL effectively.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can we better measure the long-term impact of IBL on student learning?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies that track students' academic performance and career trajectories over several years could provide more comprehensive insights into the lasting effects of IBL.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What is the optimal level of guidance for different types of learners in IBL?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical research comparing various levels of guidance across diverse learner populations would help identify best practices for tailoring IBL to individual needs.

## Synthesis

IBL is a vital concept in educational practice and research because it promotes deep learning, critical thinking, and self-regulation. By engaging students in active inquiry, IBL enhances their ability to apply knowledge in real-world contexts. This approach aligns with the goals of problem-based learning (PBL) and project-based learning (PjBL), which also emphasize student-centered learning but focus on different aspects such as problem-solving or project outcomes.

The importance of IBL extends beyond individual classrooms; it contributes to broader educational reforms aimed at preparing students for an increasingly complex world. By fostering independent learners who can navigate uncertainty, IBL prepares students for lifelong learning and adaptability in a rapidly changing society.

## Evidence

Empirical evidence supports the effectiveness of structured and guided inquiry within IBL, as meta-analyses (Furtak et al., 2012; Lazonder & Harmsen, 2016) have shown that explicit scaffolding enhances learning outcomes compared to unguided inquiry. This suggests that well-designed IBL activities can lead to better academic performance and deeper understanding.
