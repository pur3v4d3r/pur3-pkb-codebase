# Coherence Principle

> [!definition] **Coherence Principle**
> The Coherence Principle states that excluding extraneous words, pictures, and sounds from multimedia presentations enhances deep learning by reducing cognitive load; it falls under [[Cognitive Architecture]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This principle excludes the inclusion of additional elements that do not contribute to the core message or learning objectives. It is distinct from principles that focus on intrinsic versus extraneous cognitive load.

## Core Explanation

The Coherence Principle asserts that the removal of non-essential elements in multimedia presentations improves learning outcomes. By minimizing distractions, learners can focus more effectively on the core content, thereby enhancing their ability to retain and transfer information. This principle is grounded in cognitive architecture theory, which posits that working memory has limited capacity, making it crucial to avoid overloading it with unnecessary information.

In practice, applying the Coherence Principle involves carefully curating multimedia materials to ensure that every element serves a specific learning objective. For instance, if an instructional video includes irrelevant animations or sounds, these can compete for learners' attention and cognitive resources, potentially hindering their ability to process and retain key concepts. Empirical studies have shown that adhering to the Coherence Principle results in better retention and transfer of knowledge, with effect sizes typically ranging from d = 0.3 to 0.5 across various multimedia learning contexts.

The theoretical roots of the Coherence Principle can be traced back to cognitive load theory, which distinguishes between intrinsic and extraneous cognitive loads. Intrinsic load is inherent in the task itself, while extraneous load arises from how information is presented. The Coherence Principle specifically targets reducing extraneous load by eliminating elements that do not contribute to the core message or learning objectives. This approach ensures that learners' working memory is used more efficiently, allowing them to focus on what truly matters.

Historically, the principle was first introduced in 1988 by John Sweller, who emphasized the importance of instructional design that minimizes cognitive load. His work laid the foundation for understanding how multimedia presentations can be optimized to enhance learning outcomes.

## Mechanism

The mechanism behind the Coherence Principle involves the competition for working memory capacity between essential and extraneous elements. When learners are exposed to multimedia content, their working memory must process both relevant and irrelevant information simultaneously. This competition can lead to cognitive overload, particularly when extraneous elements are visually or auditorily appealing but do not contribute to learning objectives.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, the Coherence Principle suggests that designers should carefully select and present only those multimedia elements that directly support the learning goals. For example, a video tutorial on how to use a software tool should focus solely on demonstrating key features without including unnecessary animations or background music.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Worked examples**
> When applying worked examples in teaching, the Coherence Principle implies that each step and explanation should be clear and concise. Including too many extraneous details can distract learners from understanding the core problem-solving process. For instance, a physics problem solved step-by-step should focus on the relevant equations and principles without adding irrelevant commentary or visual distractions.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Interactive learning materials**
> In interactive learning materials, such as simulations or quizzes, the Coherence Principle suggests that designers should avoid cluttering the interface with unnecessary elements. A clean and uncluttered design helps learners focus on the task at hand, reducing cognitive load and improving their ability to engage with the material.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> The Coherence Principle focuses specifically on extraneous cognitive load, which includes elements that do not contribute to learning objectives. In contrast, intrinsic cognitive load is inherent in the task itself and cannot be reduced by instructional design. By distinguishing between these types of cognitive load, educators can better understand how to optimize their teaching strategies.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is credited with the origin of the Coherence Principle in his work on cognitive load theory. His research highlighted the importance of instructional design that minimizes extraneous cognitive load, thereby enhancing learning outcomes.

## Synthesis

The Coherence Principle is crucial for instructional design because it provides a clear framework for optimizing multimedia presentations to enhance learning outcomes. By reducing extraneous cognitive load, educators can help learners focus more effectively on the core content, leading to better retention and transfer of knowledge. This principle also aligns with broader theories in cognitive psychology, such as working memory theory and cognitive load theory, reinforcing its importance in both research and practice.

The Coherence Principle has significant implications for various learning contexts, from online courses to traditional classrooms. Its application can be extended beyond multimedia presentations to other forms of instructional materials, further enhancing the effectiveness of educational strategies.
