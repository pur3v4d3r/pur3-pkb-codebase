# Foundationalism

> [!definition] **Foundationalism**
> Foundationalism is an epistemological theory that posits justified beliefs have a foundational layer of basic beliefs, which are non-inferentially justified (by experience, self-evidence, or some non-doxastic ground), upon which all other beliefs rest. It falls under [[epistemology]], addressing the structure of justified beliefs but excluding alternative theories like coherentism and not delving into psychological mechanisms behind belief formation.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes alternative theories like coherentism and does not address the psychological mechanisms behind belief formation or justification processes in detail.

## Core Explanation

Foundationalism seeks to resolve the regress problem in epistemology, where every belief requires justification from another. If this process were infinite or circular (coherentism), it would lead to skepticism, as no beliefs could be truly justified. Foundationalism proposes that basic beliefs are non-inferentially justified and serve as a stable foundation upon which other beliefs can rest.

The foundational structure operates by identifying certain beliefs as basic, which do not require further justification because they are self-evident or derived from direct experience. These basic beliefs act as the bedrock of knowledge, providing a secure starting point for inferentially justified beliefs. For example, if one believes 'the sun will rise tomorrow' based on past experiences and current evidence, this belief is non-inferentially justified and serves as a foundational belief.

The regress argument motivates foundationalism by highlighting that every belief must be justified to avoid infinite or circular reasoning. Foundationalists argue that basic beliefs are the only viable solution because they terminate the justification chain without requiring further support. This approach aligns with the idea of self-evident truths, such as 'I am thinking,' which cannot be doubted and serve as a foundation for other knowledge claims.

Theoretical roots of foundationalism can be traced back to René Descartes' work on epistemology, where he posited that certain beliefs are indubitable and serve as the basis for all other knowledge. However, modern foundationalism has evolved from its classical Cartesian form, which required infallible basic beliefs, to a more modest version where basic beliefs are merely prima facie justified and can be defeasibly overturned.

## Mechanism

The mechanism of foundationalism involves identifying certain beliefs as non-inferentially justified (basic beliefs) that serve as the foundation for other inferentially justified beliefs. These basic beliefs are typically derived from direct experience, self-evidence, or some non-doxastic ground and do not require further justification.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, foundationalism implies that educational materials should build upon clearly justified basic beliefs. For instance, a course on physics might start with the basic belief that 'objects in motion tend to stay in motion unless acted upon by an external force,' which is non-inferentially justified and serves as a foundation for more complex concepts like Newton's laws.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Legal reasoning**
> In legal contexts, foundationalism suggests that certain principles or axioms are non-inferentially justified and serve as the basis for all other legal arguments. For example, the principle of 'innocent until proven guilty' is a basic belief in criminal law, which supports inferentially justified arguments about guilt based on evidence.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Foundationalism distinguishes between intrinsic and extraneous load. Intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of a task, while extraneous load is introduced by poor instructional design. Foundational beliefs are non-inferentially justified and do not introduce extraneous cognitive load, making them more effective as a foundation for other knowledge.

## Key Figures

- **René Descartes** — Descartes is considered the originator of foundationalist epistemology. His work on indubitable truths and clear and distinct perceptions laid the groundwork for modern foundationalism.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the criteria for identifying basic beliefs?
>
> *What would resolve it:* A consensus on the criteria for identifying non-inferentially justified beliefs would help resolve this question. Philosophical and psychological research could provide clearer guidelines.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can foundationalism account for all forms of knowledge?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical studies examining the limits of basic beliefs in various domains, such as mathematics or ethics, could shed light on whether foundationalism can fully account for all forms of knowledge.

## Synthesis

Foundationalism is a significant concept in epistemology because it provides a clear and structured approach to understanding justified beliefs. By identifying basic beliefs as the foundation upon which other beliefs rest, it offers a solution to the regress problem that has been central to philosophical debates for centuries. Its ongoing relevance lies in its ability to guide practical applications such as instructional design and legal reasoning, while also prompting further inquiry into the nature of basic beliefs and their justification.

The concept of foundationalism intersects with other epistemological theories like coherentism, offering a rich field for comparative analysis. Understanding foundationalism helps clarify the differences between intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load in educational settings, making it an essential tool for both theoretical and practical applications.
