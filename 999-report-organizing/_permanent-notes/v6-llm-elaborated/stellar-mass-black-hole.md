# Stellar Mass Black Hole

> [!definition] **Stellar Mass Black Hole**
> A Stellar Mass Black Hole is a black hole that forms from the gravitational collapse of a massive star at the end of its life, typically ranging between ~3 to ~100 solar masses. It falls under the category of Black Holes by Mass Class and excludes supermassive or primordial black holes.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes supermassive black holes and primordial black holes. It should not be confused with neutron stars or other stellar remnants.

## Core Explanation

Stellar Mass Black Holes are formed when a massive star exhausts its nuclear fuel, leading to core-collapse supernovae that can result in the formation of these dense objects. The process begins with the star's core becoming unable to support itself against gravitational collapse due to electron degeneracy pressure and eventually reaching the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV) limit, beyond which no known force can prevent further collapse into a black hole.

Observational evidence for Stellar Mass Black Holes comes from various sources including X-ray binaries where they accrete matter from companion stars, gravitational wave detections from binary mergers by LIGO/Virgo, and microlensing events. These methods provide different perspectives on the existence and properties of these objects, confirming their masses up to around 50 solar masses.

The study of Stellar Mass Black Holes contributes significantly to our understanding of stellar evolution and gravitational wave astronomy. For instance, the detection of black hole mergers by LIGO/Virgo has provided insights into the dynamics and populations of these objects in binary systems, while also challenging some aspects of stellar-evolution models.

Despite extensive research, there remains an apparent 'upper mass gap' (~50–130 solar masses) where few Stellar Mass Black Holes are observed. This gap suggests that either pair-instability supernovae prevent the formation of black holes in this range or that some black holes may have non-stellar origins.

## Mechanism

The process leading to a Stellar Mass Black Hole involves core-collapse supernovae, where the star's iron core collapses under gravity. If the remnant mass exceeds the TOV limit, no known force can halt the collapse, and it continues until a black hole forms. This mechanism is well-established for Stellar Mass Black Holes but remains an area of active research regarding its applicability to other types of black holes.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Stellar Evolution Studies**
> Understanding Stellar Mass Black Holes provides critical insights into the end stages of massive star evolution. Observations and theoretical models help refine our understanding of stellar mass loss, core-collapse mechanisms, and the conditions under which black holes form. Ignoring these objects could lead to incomplete or inaccurate models of stellar life cycles.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Gravitational Wave Astronomy**
> Stellar Mass Black Holes are key sources for gravitational wave detections by LIGO/Virgo, contributing significantly to the field's development and providing direct evidence for black hole mergers. This has implications for understanding binary star systems, black hole populations, and the dynamics of these objects in space.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Stellar Mass Black Holes vs Supermassive Black Holes**
> While Stellar Mass Black Holes form from individual massive stars, supermassive black holes are much larger and thought to originate through different mechanisms. The distinction is crucial for understanding the diversity of black hole populations in the universe.

> [!key-distinction] **Core-collapse supernova remnants vs Neutron Stars**
> Both can be end products of massive star collapse, but differ significantly in mass and structure. Stellar Mass Black Holes form when the remnant exceeds the TOV limit, while neutron stars are supported by neutron degeneracy pressure.

## Key Figures

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What explains the apparent 'upper mass gap' in Stellar Mass Black Hole populations?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Observational evidence of black holes within this mass range or theoretical models explaining why they are not formed would resolve this question.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Are there non-stellar origins for some of these black holes?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Detection of Stellar Mass Black Holes with unique characteristics, such as those resulting from mergers of smaller black holes or primordial formation, could provide evidence supporting this hypothesis.

## Synthesis

Understanding Stellar Mass Black Holes is crucial for advancing astrophysics by providing insights into stellar evolution and gravitational wave astronomy. These objects serve as key test cases for theories about the end stages of massive stars and the dynamics of black hole mergers, contributing to a broader understanding of cosmic phenomena.
