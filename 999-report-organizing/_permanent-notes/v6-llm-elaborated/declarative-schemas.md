# Declarative Schemas

> [!definition] **Declarative Schemas**
> Declarative Schemas are organised structures of factual and conceptual knowledge — categories, hierarchies, propositional networks, and conceptual frames — that represent what a learner knows about a domain, supporting recognition, classification, and verbal description. They fall under [[cognitive-architecture]], contrasting with procedural schemas which focus on how to perform tasks.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes procedural schemas, which focus on how to perform tasks, and instead focuses on the declarative aspect of knowledge representation.

## Core Explanation

Declarative Schemas are foundational knowledge structures that novices typically build through explicit instruction. These schemas serve as the conceptual anchors onto which more complex procedural schemas later attach, providing a framework for understanding and reasoning about specific domains. For instance, in learning to solve algebraic equations, declarative schemas might include basic definitions of variables, operations, and properties, enabling learners to recognize and classify different types of problems.

In practice, Declarative Schemas are crucial for effective learning because they provide the necessary background knowledge that allows learners to reason about new information. Without a solid foundation in declarative knowledge, learners may struggle with applying procedural skills correctly, especially when faced with edge cases or novel situations. For example, a student who has not fully grasped the concept of variables and their manipulation might incorrectly apply algebraic rules, leading to errors.

Theoretical roots of Declarative Schemas can be traced back to cognitive psychology, where they are seen as part of declarative memory. This form of memory is distinct from procedural memory, which focuses on how-to knowledge. Declarative schemas are often formed through explicit instruction and practice, with the goal of building a robust network of interconnected concepts that facilitate deeper understanding.

Empirical evidence supports the importance of Declarative Schemas in learning. John Sweller's research (1988) highlighted the role of these schemas in cognitive load theory, demonstrating how well-structured declarative knowledge can reduce the intrinsic and extraneous cognitive loads on learners, leading to more effective problem-solving.

## Mechanism

Declarative Schemas are formed through a process that involves explicit instruction and practice. Initially, learners encounter new information and begin to categorize it into meaningful chunks. Over time, these chunks become interconnected, forming a network of related concepts. This network is further strengthened through repeated exposure and application in various contexts.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> Understanding Declarative Schemas can significantly improve instructional design by emphasizing the importance of building robust foundational knowledge before introducing procedural skills. For example, a math curriculum that starts with clear definitions and examples of algebraic concepts will better prepare students to apply these concepts in problem-solving tasks.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Educational assessment**
> Assessments should be designed to evaluate both declarative knowledge and the ability to apply it. For instance, a test that requires students to explain their reasoning behind solving an algebraic equation demonstrates a deeper understanding of Declarative Schemas.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Learning environments**
> Creating learning environments that encourage exploration and application of declarative knowledge can enhance long-term retention and transfer. For example, interactive simulations that allow students to manipulate variables in real-time provide practical applications of algebraic concepts.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Declarative Schemas are associated with intrinsic load, which is inherent to the task itself. In contrast, extraneous load arises from how information is presented and can be reduced by well-structured Declarative Schemas. For example, a clear explanation of algebraic concepts reduces cognitive strain compared to an ambiguous or poorly structured presentation.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is credited with the development of cognitive load theory in 1988, which emphasizes the role of Declarative Schemas in reducing intrinsic and extraneous cognitive loads during learning.
