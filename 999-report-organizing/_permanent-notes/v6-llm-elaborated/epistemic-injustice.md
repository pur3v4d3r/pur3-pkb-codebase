# Epistemic Injustice

> [!definition] **Epistemic Injustice**
> Epistemic Injustice is a family of distinctively epistemic wrongs that occur when individuals are wronged in their capacity as knowers — most prominently through testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, which fall under [[social-epistemology]]. These injustices extend moral and political philosophy into the epistemic domain by identifying wrongs that are not reducible to material harm or general moral wrong but are constitutively epistemic — being wronged as a knower.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept focuses on the specific harms to knowledge production and reception but does not encompass broader social or material injustices.

## Core Explanation

Epistemic Injustice is a concept developed by Miranda Fricker, which focuses on the specific harms to knowledge production and reception. It encompasses two main forms: testimonial injustice, where a hearer accords reduced credibility to a speaker due to identity prejudice, and hermeneutical injustice, where marginalized groups are unable to interpret their own experiences because of a lack of resources in the collective conceptual repertoire.

In practice, testimonial injustice often manifests when a person's testimony is dismissed or discredited simply because they belong to a marginalized group. For example, a woman might be told her scientific findings are less credible than those of a man due to gender bias. Hermeneutical injustice occurs in contexts where the language and frameworks used to describe experiences exclude certain groups, making it difficult for them to articulate their own realities accurately.

Theoretical roots of epistemic injustice can be traced back to feminist epistemology and critical race theory, which emphasize the importance of diverse perspectives in knowledge production. These theories argue that epistemic injustices undermine the legitimacy of marginalized voices and perpetuate systemic biases within academic and social discourse.

Empirically, epistemic injustice has been observed in various fields such as clinical communication research, where healthcare providers may misinterpret patients' symptoms due to cultural or racial biases. This can lead to misdiagnosis and inadequate treatment, highlighting the practical implications of these injustices.

## Mechanism

Testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer's credibility assessment is influenced by identity prejudice. For instance, if a person from a marginalized group is speaking about their experience, a non-marginalized listener might automatically doubt or dismiss the speaker's account due to preconceived biases. This process can be exacerbated in situations where the speaker lacks visibility or representation within the dominant discourse.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, epistemic injustice can manifest when course materials and teaching methods fail to include diverse perspectives. For example, a curriculum that predominantly features historical narratives from Western European authors may marginalize students from other cultural backgrounds, leading to hermeneutical injustice as these students struggle to find their experiences represented in the learning material.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Clinical communication**
> In clinical settings, epistemic injustice can occur when healthcare providers misinterpret patients' symptoms due to racial or cultural biases. For instance, a patient from an ethnic minority might be dismissed for presenting with vague complaints that are stereotypically associated with their background, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Academic publishing**
> In academic publishing, epistemic injustice can arise when peer reviewers unconsciously favor submissions from authors who share similar identities or backgrounds. This can result in the marginalization of voices that challenge dominant paradigms, thereby perpetuating hermeneutical injustice and limiting the diversity of knowledge produced.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Epistemic vs Non-Epistemic Injustice**
> While epistemic injustice specifically targets wrongs to one's capacity as a knower, non-epistemic injustices can encompass broader social and material harms. For example, economic inequality or physical violence are not reducible to epistemic concerns but still significantly impact individuals' lives.

> [!key-distinction] **Testimonial vs Hermeneutical Injustice**
> Testimonial injustice involves a hearer's credibility assessment based on identity prejudice, while hermeneutical injustice occurs when marginalized groups lack the resources and frameworks necessary to articulate their own experiences. Testimonial injustice is more immediate and visible, whereas hermeneutical injustice can be more subtle but equally damaging over time.

## Key Figures

- **Miranda Fricker** — Miranda Fricker is the originator of epistemic injustice. She introduced the concept in her work to highlight the specific harms to knowledge production and reception, particularly through testimonial and hermeneutical injustices.

## Synthesis

Epistemic Injustice matters because it provides a framework for understanding and addressing specific harms to knowledge production and reception. By focusing on testimonial and hermeneutical injustices, the concept highlights the importance of diverse perspectives in academic and social discourse. This has significant implications across fields such as feminist epistemology, critical race theory, disability studies, and clinical communication research, where marginalized voices are often silenced or misinterpreted.

The broader impact of epistemic injustice extends beyond these specific domains to challenge existing power structures and promote more inclusive knowledge production. By recognizing and addressing these injustices, we can work towards a more equitable society where all individuals have the opportunity to contribute their unique perspectives and experiences.
