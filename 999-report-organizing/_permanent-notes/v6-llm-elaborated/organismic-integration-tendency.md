# Organismic Integration Tendency

> [!definition] **Organismic Integration Tendency**
> The inherent, natural human tendency to internalize and integrate ambient social values, practices, and regulatory norms into a coherent, unified self-structure. This process is not passive absorption but an active one where external material becomes genuinely part of the individual's own beliefs and behaviors. It falls under [[self-determination-theory]], which treats human beings as inherently active, growth-oriented organisms. It falls under [[motivational-psychology]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept does not include passive absorption of external material or conformity. It specifically refers to the active processing and integration of these elements by the individual.

## Core Explanation

The organismic integration tendency operates as a fundamental mechanism in Self-Determination Theory (SDT), where individuals naturally seek to integrate external values into their self-structure. This process is not merely about conforming but involves an active internalization that transforms external norms and practices into personal beliefs and behaviors.

In practice, this means that when conditions permit, such as supportive social environments or meaningful goals, the integration tendency allows individuals to adopt and embrace new values and practices seamlessly. Conversely, when conditions obstruct, like in coercive or unsupportive settings, the tendency may falter, leading to motivational dysfunction.

Theoretical roots of this concept can be traced back to Maslow's need hierarchy, Rogers' organismic valuing process, White's effectance motivation, and deCharms' personal causation. These theories collectively frame human beings as inherently active and growth-oriented, setting the stage for SDT’s exploration of how existing motivation is supported or undermined.

Empirically, this tendency has been observed in various contexts, from educational settings where supportive environments foster intrinsic motivation to therapeutic practices that aim to help clients integrate new values and behaviors into their self-structure. Understanding this process can inform strategies to enhance psychological well-being and promote adaptive behavior.

## Mechanism

The integration of external values occurs through a series of steps: first, individuals encounter and become aware of the external value or practice. Next, they evaluate its relevance and compatibility with their existing self-structure. If deemed valuable, they then process it actively, integrating it into their beliefs and behaviors.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding the organismic integration tendency can lead to more effective learning environments. By creating supportive and meaningful contexts, educators can facilitate students' internalization of new knowledge and skills, leading to deeper and more lasting learning outcomes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Therapeutic practices**
> In therapeutic settings, integrating the organismic integration tendency helps clients adopt healthier behaviors by making them genuinely their own. Therapists can guide clients through a process of active processing and internalization, fostering long-term change rather than superficial compliance.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Organizational culture**
> In organizational settings, promoting an environment that supports autonomy, competence, and relatedness can enhance employees' integration of company values. This leads to higher job satisfaction and more committed behavior among staff members.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Integration vs Introjection**
> Integration involves making external material genuinely one's own through active processing, whereas introjection is a failure of integration where an individual takes in a regulation without fully processing it. The key difference lies in the depth and authenticity of internalization.

> [!key-distinction] **Integration vs Assimilation**
> While assimilation refers to incorporating new information into existing cognitive structures, often without significant change, integration involves a more transformative process where external values become part of an individual's core self-structure. The distinction lies in the depth and authenticity of internalization.

## Key Figures

- **Richard M. Ryan** — Co-developer of Self-Determination Theory, Richard M. Ryan has significantly contributed to understanding the organismic integration tendency as a core mechanism in SDT.
- **Edward L. Deci** — Alongside Richard M. Ryan, Edward L. Deci co-developed Self-Determination Theory and has extensively researched the role of intrinsic motivation and the organismic integration tendency in human behavior.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does the organismic integration tendency interact with different cultural contexts?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further research exploring how cultural values influence the process of integration could provide insights into its variability across different societies.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term effects of successful and unsuccessful integration?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies tracking individuals over extended periods would help clarify the lasting impacts of effective versus ineffective integration on psychological well-being and behavior.

## Synthesis

The organismic integration tendency is crucial for understanding human motivation as it bridges theoretical concepts with practical applications. By integrating external values into a coherent self-structure, individuals can experience deeper satisfaction and more adaptive behaviors. This concept has significant implications across motivational psychology, education, therapy, and organizational behavior, making it a foundational element in SDT.

Moreover, the integration tendency connects intrinsic motivation (a key component of SDT) with broader psychological needs like autonomy, competence, and relatedness. By fostering these needs, organizations and educators can create environments that support effective integration, leading to more meaningful and sustainable changes.
