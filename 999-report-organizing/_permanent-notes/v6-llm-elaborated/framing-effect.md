# Framing Effect

> [!definition] **Framing Effect**
> The Framing Effect is the tendency for people to make different choices based on how a problem is presented, even when the outcomes are logically equivalent. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], demonstrating that choice is influenced by reference-dependent value functions rather than expected-utility maximization over absolute outcomes.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes specific examples of framing and focuses on the general phenomenon where reference-dependent value functions influence choice over absolute outcomes.

## Core Explanation

The Framing Effect illustrates how the way information is framed can significantly alter decision-making processes, even when the underlying outcomes are identical. For instance, Tversky and Kahneman's Asian disease problem shows that people prefer a certain loss (e.g., saving 200 out of 600 lives) over a probabilistic gain (e.g., a 33% chance of saving all 600 lives), indicating that the framing of choices can lead to different preferences. This effect challenges classical decision theory, which assumes that individuals make decisions based on objective probabilities and utilities.

In practice, the Framing Effect operates through reference points and value functions. When a problem is framed in terms of gains, people tend to be more risk-averse; conversely, when it is framed in terms of losses, they become more willing to take risks. This phenomenon suggests that decision-makers do not always act as rational utility maximizers but are instead influenced by the context and framing of their choices.

Theoretical roots of the Framing Effect can be traced back to prospect theory, which posits that people evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point rather than absolute outcomes. This means that the same decision-maker may prefer different options depending on whether they are described as gains or losses. For example, framing a scenario where saving lives is framed as a gain versus a loss can lead to significantly different choices.

Empirical evidence supporting the Framing Effect comes from numerous studies, including Tversky and Kahneman's original Asian disease problem. These experiments demonstrate that people’s preferences are not stable but shift based on how problems are presented, indicating that reference-dependent value functions play a crucial role in decision-making.

## Mechanism

The Framing Effect operates through the cognitive processes of reference points and value functions. When a problem is framed as a gain, individuals tend to focus on avoiding losses, leading to risk-averse behavior. Conversely, when framed as a loss, people are more likely to take risks in an attempt to avoid further negative outcomes.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, the Framing Effect can be leveraged to enhance learning outcomes. For example, framing educational content as a gain (e.g., mastering new skills) rather than a loss (e.g., failing to learn something) can increase student motivation and engagement.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Marketing**
> In marketing, the Framing Effect is used to influence consumer behavior. For instance, framing a product as a gain (e.g., offering a discount on a purchase) rather than a loss (e.g., increasing prices) can make it more appealing to potential customers.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Policy design**
> In policy design, the Framing Effect can be applied to improve public health initiatives. For example, framing vaccination campaigns as a gain (e.g., protecting oneself and others from diseases) rather than a loss (e.g., risking infection) can increase participation rates.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> While the Framing Effect involves how problems are presented, intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of the task itself. The key distinction is that framing manipulates the presentation of choices rather than altering the cognitive demands of the task.

## Key Figures

- **Amos Tversky** — Tversky co-discovered the Framing Effect in 1981, contributing significantly to our understanding of how reference-dependent value functions influence decision-making.
- **Daniel Kahneman** — Kahneman was a co-discoverer of the Framing Effect and further developed its implications for cognitive biases and decision theory.

>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical research on effective strategies to reduce the impact of framing effects, such as reflective decision-making techniques or cognitive training programs, could provide insights into mitigation.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the limits of reference-dependent value functions?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further experimental studies exploring how and when individuals rely on reference points versus absolute outcomes would help clarify these limitations.

## Synthesis

Understanding the Framing Effect is crucial for decision-making because it reveals that choices are not always rational or stable but can be significantly influenced by context. This concept has profound implications across cognitive science, economics, and psychology, challenging traditional views of human behavior and decision processes.

The Framing Effect also highlights the importance of framing in various fields such as marketing, policy design, and instructional design. By recognizing how different framings can influence choices, practitioners can make more informed decisions that align with intended outcomes.
