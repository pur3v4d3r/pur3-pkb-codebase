# Self-Efficacy Theory

> [!definition] **Self-Efficacy Theory**
> Self-Efficacy Theory, formalized within Social-Cognitive Theory by Albert Bandura, explains how an individual's belief in their capability to execute necessary actions to achieve a goal influences their behavior and performance. It falls under [[social-cognitive-theory]], where efficacy beliefs are seen as the proximal mediator through which biographical experience translates into self-regulated action.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This theory focuses on the role of perceived self-efficacy in motivation and does not encompass broader constructs like general confidence or self-esteem.

## Core Explanation

Self-efficacy is not merely about having the skills or knowledge to perform a task; it's about believing in one’s ability to do so. This belief significantly influences goal-setting, effort allocation, persistence during setbacks, and emotional responses throughout the process. For instance, students with high self-efficacy are more likely to set challenging academic goals, employ effective study strategies, recover quickly from failures, and ultimately achieve higher grades.

The impact of self-efficacy is evident in various domains such as education, clinical recovery, and occupational performance. In educational settings, for example, students who believe they can succeed tend to engage more deeply with their studies, seek out challenging tasks, and persist through difficulties, leading to better academic outcomes. Similarly, in clinical contexts, patients with high self-efficacy are more likely to adhere to treatment plans and recover faster.

The theory posits that efficacy beliefs operate independently of actual capabilities. At equivalent skill levels, learners with higher perceived self-efficacy set more ambitious goals, employ more sophisticated strategies, and recover more effectively from setbacks. This makes self-efficacy a stronger predictor of behavior change than mere outcome expectations alone, underscoring its importance in motivational psychology.

The concept of self-efficacy is rooted in the idea that individuals are not passive recipients of their environment but active agents who shape their experiences through their beliefs and actions. Bandura’s theory emphasizes the role of four canonical sources of efficacy information: enactive mastery experience, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological/affective states. These sources collectively influence an individual's self-efficacy beliefs.

## Mechanism

Self-efficacy is shaped by a combination of direct experiences (enactive mastery), observing others succeed (vicarious experience), receiving encouragement from others (verbal persuasion), and physiological or emotional states (physiological/affective states). These sources work together to build or undermine an individual's confidence in their ability to perform tasks effectively.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding self-efficacy can lead to more effective learning environments. For example, providing students with opportunities for mastery experiences (such as hands-on projects) and offering constructive feedback can boost their confidence in their abilities. This, in turn, enhances engagement and persistence, leading to better academic outcomes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Clinical settings**
> In clinical settings, self-efficacy plays a crucial role in patient adherence to treatment plans. Healthcare providers can enhance patients' self-efficacy by demonstrating successful recovery cases (vicarious experience), offering encouragement and support (verbal persuasion), and creating a supportive environment that reduces stress and anxiety (physiological/affective states).

> [!example] **Application 3 — Occupational performance**
> In the workplace, managers can improve employee performance by fostering a culture of continuous learning and providing opportunities for mastery experiences. Verbal encouragement from supervisors and colleagues also plays a significant role in building self-efficacy among employees.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Self-Efficacy vs. Self-Esteem**
> While both self-efficacy and self-esteem involve confidence, they differ in their focus and application. Self-efficacy is task-specific and forward-looking, focusing on an individual's belief in their ability to perform specific tasks. In contrast, self-esteem encompasses a broader sense of personal worth and can be more global or diffuse.

## Key Figures

- **Albert Bandura** — Albert Bandura is the originator of Self-Efficacy Theory, formalizing it within Social-Cognitive Theory in 1977. His work laid the foundation for understanding how self-efficacy influences behavior and performance across various domains.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does self-efficacy vary across different domains and contexts?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further research that systematically compares self-efficacy levels in diverse settings could help clarify how context influences the development and application of self-efficacy beliefs.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can self-efficacy be effectively measured and quantified?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Developing standardized measures that accurately capture self-efficacy across different domains would provide more reliable data for empirical studies. This could involve creating validated scales or tools that assess self-efficacy in various contexts.

## Synthesis

Self-Efficacy Theory is a foundational concept in motivational psychology because it provides a robust framework for understanding how individuals' beliefs about their capabilities influence their behavior and performance. By integrating direct experiences, observational learning, verbal persuasion, and physiological/affective states, the theory offers practical insights into enhancing motivation and achievement across educational, clinical, and occupational settings.

The theory's emphasis on task-specific efficacy beliefs highlights its importance in addressing individual differences and promoting self-regulated learning. Its application in diverse contexts underscores its relevance to broader motivational constructs such as outcome expectations and triadic reciprocal determinism within Social-Cognitive Theory.
