# Naturalistic Decision Making

> [!definition] **Naturalistic Decision Making**
> Naturalistic Decision Making is a research program that studies how experienced practitioners make decisions in real-world settings characterized by time pressure and incomplete information, focusing on expertise rather than population-average choice. It falls under [[Decision Science]], as it seeks to understand the practical decision-making processes of experts in dynamic conditions, distinct from classical decision theory's prescriptive models.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes decision-making scenarios that do not involve rapid, unambiguous feedback or complex, dynamic conditions. It should not be confused with classical decision theory's prescriptive models of rational choice.

## Core Explanation

Naturalistic Decision Making emphasizes that expert decision-making is not a step-by-step process of comparing options but rather involves rapid situation assessment and recognition-driven action selection. This approach has been empirically demonstrated through fieldwork in domains such as firefighting, where experts make split-second decisions based on their experience and the current situation.

The research program highlights that these experts rely heavily on mental simulations to predict outcomes and recognize patterns that guide their actions. For instance, firefighters might quickly assess a burning building's layout and potential hazards before deciding on the best course of action, often without explicitly weighing all possible options.

This method contrasts with classical decision theory, which assumes that decisions are made through a rational process involving extensive information processing and option comparison. Naturalistic Decision Making challenges this by showing that in real-world settings, experts use heuristics and intuition to make effective decisions quickly, even when information is incomplete or ambiguous.

The findings of Naturalistic Decision Making have significant implications for training programs aimed at developing expertise. By understanding how experienced practitioners make decisions, educators can design more effective training methods that focus on building situation awareness and recognition skills rather than just theoretical knowledge.

## Mechanism

Naturalistic Decision Making involves several key mechanisms. Experts first assess the current situation rapidly to identify critical elements and potential risks. This assessment is often based on pattern recognition, where experts draw upon their extensive experience to quickly categorize situations into familiar patterns or scenarios. Once a pattern is recognized, experts can predict likely outcomes and take appropriate action without needing to deliberate over all possible options.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design for emergency response training, Naturalistic Decision Making suggests that simulations should focus on rapid situation assessment and recognition of critical elements. Trainees should be exposed to a variety of real-world scenarios where they must quickly recognize patterns and make decisions based on their experience rather than detailed analysis.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Business strategy**
> For business leaders, Naturalistic Decision Making implies that training programs should emphasize developing intuition and pattern recognition skills. Leaders can learn to assess market conditions swiftly and make strategic decisions based on recognizing familiar patterns in the competitive landscape.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Naturalistic Decision Making focuses on intrinsic load, which is the inherent complexity of a task that cannot be reduced. In contrast, classical decision theory often deals with extraneous load, or unnecessary cognitive burden introduced by the decision-making process itself. The distinction matters because Naturalistic Decision Making shows that experts can handle high intrinsic loads effectively through pattern recognition and intuition, whereas classical models may struggle in such conditions.

## Key Figures

- **Gary Klein** — Gary Klein is a key researcher in Naturalistic Decision Making who has extensively studied how experts make decisions in real-world settings. His work on recognition-primed decision models and case studies of expert performance have significantly advanced the field.
- **Daniel Kahneman** — Daniel Kahneman, a collaborator with Gary Klein, has contributed to Naturalistic Decision Making by integrating insights from cognitive psychology into understanding expert judgment. His work on heuristics and biases complements the focus on rapid decision-making in dynamic environments.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can Naturalistic Decision Making be applied to improve decision-making in less structured and more ambiguous domains?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further research is needed to identify specific heuristics and patterns that experts use in ambiguous situations, which could then inform training programs for these domains.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the limitations of relying on expert intuition without rapid feedback?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical studies comparing decision-making outcomes with and without rapid feedback would help clarify when and how expert intuition is reliable or unreliable.

## Synthesis

Naturalistic Decision Making provides a valuable framework for understanding the practical aspects of decision-making in real-world settings. By focusing on expertise, situation awareness, and recognition-driven action selection, it challenges classical decision theory's assumptions about rational choice. This research has significant implications for training programs across various fields, from emergency response to business strategy. Moreover, its insights into ecological rationality and fast-and-frugal heuristics offer practical tools for improving decision-making processes in complex and dynamic environments.
