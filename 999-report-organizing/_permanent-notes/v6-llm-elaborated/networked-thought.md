# Networked Thought

> [!definition] **Networked Thought**
> Networked Thought is a model of thinking where ideas are treated as nodes in an associative graph rather than a hierarchical structure, designed to enhance cognitive processes through externalized and interconnected knowledge storage. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], focusing on associative relationships between ideas while excluding traditional hierarchical models.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes traditional hierarchical models of thought and memory, focusing instead on associative relationships between ideas. It should not be confused with linear or categorical thinking methods.

## Core Explanation

Networked Thought operates by treating ideas as nodes within a complex web of associations, allowing for more flexible and efficient retrieval of information. This model is theoretically grounded in associative network models of long-term memory, where the strength of connections between concepts influences their accessibility during recall. By mirroring this structure in note-taking and knowledge management systems, Networked Thought aims to extend the native cognitive architecture by providing a reliable, queryable storage system that complements unaided human memory.

In practice, Networked Thought emphasizes the importance of dense interconnections between ideas, which can facilitate deeper understanding and more nuanced thinking. For instance, when studying complex subjects, creating detailed notes with numerous links to related concepts can help in building a robust mental framework. This approach contrasts with traditional hierarchical models, where information is organized into rigid categories, limiting the flexibility of thought processes.

The core mechanism behind Networked Thought involves spreading activation across linked nodes. When one idea is activated, it triggers associated ideas through these connections, enhancing the overall cognitive network's efficiency and depth. This process can be likened to a web search where clicking on related links leads to more relevant information, making it easier to navigate complex topics.

Empirically, Networked Thought has roots in cognitive psychology, particularly in theories of memory and learning. John Sweller, an influential figure in this field, introduced the concept of intrinsic vs extraneous load, which is closely related to how associative networks can reduce cognitive overload by distributing information across multiple nodes.

## Mechanism

The mechanism behind Networked Thought involves spreading activation, where activating one node (idea) triggers associated nodes through a process akin to a ripple effect. This allows for the rapid retrieval of related concepts and enhances the overall interconnectedness of knowledge.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Networked Thought can be applied by creating detailed lesson plans with extensive interconnections between topics. This approach helps students build a more comprehensive understanding and facilitates better retention through the associative links.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Knowledge management systems**
> Networked Thought informs the design of knowledge management systems that allow for flexible, interconnected storage of information. Such systems can enhance retrieval efficiency by providing multiple pathways to access related data, improving overall cognitive processing.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Tool design**
> When designing tools for note-taking and knowledge management, incorporating Networked Thought principles ensures that the system supports dense interconnections between ideas. This leads to more effective information retrieval and better cognitive engagement with the material.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Networked Thought is not just about associative thinking but also involves managing intrinsic load, which refers to the inherent difficulty of a task. In contrast, extraneous load pertains to unnecessary cognitive effort introduced by poor design or lack of structure. By reducing extraneous load through well-structured interconnections, Networked Thought enhances overall cognitive efficiency.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is a key figure in the development of Networked Thought, particularly for his work on intrinsic vs extraneous load. His research has significantly influenced how we understand and apply associative networks to enhance cognitive processes.
