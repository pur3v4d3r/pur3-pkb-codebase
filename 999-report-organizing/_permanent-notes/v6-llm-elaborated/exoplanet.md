# Exoplanet

> [!definition] **Exoplanet**
> An Exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun, marking it distinct from the planets within our solar system. This concept falls under planetary science and has expanded our understanding of celestial bodies beyond our immediate cosmic neighborhood.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> The concept excludes planets within our solar system and focuses on those orbiting stars outside it. It should not be confused with other celestial bodies like moons or asteroids that do not meet this criterion.

## Core Explanation

The discovery of exoplanets began in earnest with the confirmation of 51 Pegasi b by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz in 1995, a milestone that heralded an era of planetary exploration outside our solar system. This finding not only confirmed the existence of planets around other stars but also opened up new avenues for studying planetary systems beyond Earth's immediate vicinity.

Since then, over 5,500 exoplanets have been catalogued, each offering unique insights into the diversity and complexity of planetary systems in our galaxy. The Kepler mission has played a pivotal role in this discovery process, revealing that planets are common around stars and that their configurations can be vastly different from those found within our solar system.

The detection methods used to find exoplanets, such as the transit method and radial velocity technique, have biases that must be accounted for when interpreting data. These biases favor the identification of short-period and massive planets, respectively, which means that raw catalogue counts do not accurately represent the true population of exoplanets.

The variety of exoplanet types discovered, including hot Jupiters, super-Earths, mini-Neptunes, and ultra-short-period planets, challenges our understanding of planetary formation and evolution. This diversity suggests that while our solar system is one possible configuration, it may not be representative of the typical architecture found in other star systems.

## Mechanism

The transit method detects exoplanets by observing periodic dips in a star's brightness as planets pass in front of it. This dimming effect can reveal information about the planet’s size and orbit, providing crucial data for cataloging new discoveries.

The radial velocity method measures the gravitational pull that an orbiting planet exerts on its host star, causing slight wobbles in the star's position. By analyzing these wobbles, scientists can infer the presence of planets and estimate their mass.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Understanding Planetary Habitability**
> The discovery of exoplanets has profound implications for our understanding of planetary habitability. By identifying planets within a star's habitable zone, where conditions might be suitable for liquid water and potentially life, we can explore the possibility of extraterrestrial life beyond Earth.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Planetary Formation Theories**
> Exoplanet discoveries challenge existing theories about planetary formation. For instance, the presence of hot Jupiters—gas giants orbiting very close to their stars—contradicts models that predict such planets should form far from their host stars and migrate inward over time.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Exoplanets vs. Solar System Planets**
> While both exoplanets and solar system planets are celestial bodies orbiting a star, the key distinction lies in their origin and characteristics. Exoplanets offer a broader spectrum of planetary types not seen within our solar system, such as super-Earths and mini-Neptunes.

> [!key-distinction] **Hot Jupiters vs. Other Exoplanet Types**
> Hot Jupiters are gas giant planets that orbit very close to their stars, unlike the more distant orbits of Jupiter in our solar system. This proximity leads to extreme temperatures and unique atmospheric conditions, making them distinct from other exoplanets.

Studying exoplanets is crucial for advancing our understanding of planetary science.
