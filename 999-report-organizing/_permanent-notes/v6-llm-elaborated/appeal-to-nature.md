# Appeal To Nature

> [!definition] **Appeal To Nature**
> The Appeal To Nature is a logical fallacy where one assumes that something is inherently good or correct simply because it is natural, without providing independent evidence to support this claim. This concept excludes contexts where the distinction between natural and unnatural carries relevant information, such as in ecology or regulatory frameworks. It falls under informal fallacies.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes situations where naturalness carries relevant information in specific contexts such as ecology or regulation. It should not be confused with other informal fallacies like appeal to tradition or genetic fallacy.

## Core Explanation

The Appeal To Nature operates on a fundamental misunderstanding of what it means for something to be 'natural.' At its core, this fallacy conflates the idea that something occurs without human intervention with the notion that it is inherently desirable or beneficial. This confusion can lead individuals to overlook significant evidence and reasoning when evaluating claims about health, morality, or correctness.

In practice, the Appeal To Nature often manifests in arguments where proponents assert that a product, behavior, or belief must be superior simply because it aligns with some notion of 'nature.' For instance, one might argue that organic foods are healthier solely because they are natural, ignoring scientific evidence about nutrition and safety. This fallacy is particularly insidious as it can lead to harmful decisions based on unfounded assumptions.

Theoretical roots of the Appeal To Nature lie in cognitive biases such as confirmation bias and the availability heuristic, which make people more likely to accept claims that align with their preconceived notions of what 'natural' means. These biases are compounded by a lack of critical thinking about the relationship between naturalness and desirability.

Empirical studies have shown that individuals often rely on intuitive judgments when making decisions, leading them to favor products or practices labeled as 'natural.' This reliance can be problematic in fields such as medicine, where treatments based solely on their perceived naturalness may not be effective or safe.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Health and Wellness**
> In the health and wellness sector, the Appeal To Nature fallacy can lead consumers to prefer products labeled as 'natural' over those that are scientifically proven to be effective. For example, a company might market a supplement as '100% natural,' implying it is inherently better than synthetic alternatives without providing evidence of its efficacy or safety.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Environmental Policy**
> In environmental policy discussions, the Appeal To Nature fallacy can mislead policymakers into prioritizing conservation efforts based on a simplistic view of what constitutes 'natural' ecosystems. This could result in neglecting areas that have been altered by human activity but still provide critical ecological services.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Natural vs Unnatural**
> While the Appeal To Nature fallacy often assumes that natural things are inherently good, it is crucial to distinguish between what is naturally occurring and what is beneficial. Many harmful substances occur in nature (e.g., arsenic), while many beneficial products are engineered by humans.
