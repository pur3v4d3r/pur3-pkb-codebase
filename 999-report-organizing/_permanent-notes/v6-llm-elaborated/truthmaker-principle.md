# Truthmaker Principle

> [!definition] **Truthmaker Principle**
> The Truthmaker Principle is a metaphysical thesis asserting that every true proposition must have something in the world — a truthmaker — in virtue of which it is true. It falls under [[Metaphysics]], excluding positions that affirm truths without any corresponding worldly basis and does not address theories of truth beyond the requirement for truthmakers.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This principle excludes positions that affirm truths without any corresponding worldly basis and does not address theories of truth beyond the requirement for truthmakers.

## Core Explanation

At its core, the Truthmaker Principle posits that every true statement must be grounded in some aspect of reality. This principle serves as a methodological constraint on ontology by requiring theorists to specify what makes their preferred truths true. For instance, if one claims that 'there is a cat on the mat,' the principle demands an explanation for why this proposition is true, such as the actual presence of a cat and a mat in the room.

The principle operates in practice by forcing philosophers and ontologists to justify their assertions with concrete entities or states of affairs. This can be seen in debates over abstract objects: if one argues that numbers exist independently of physical reality, they must provide a truthmaker for these abstract entities. The principle thus acts as a rigorous test for the existence of such entities, ensuring that every claim is backed by something real.

Theoretical roots and conceptual nuances of the Truthmaker Principle can be traced back to discussions in metaphysics about the nature of truth and reality. It intersects with other theories like the Correspondence Theory of Truth, which asserts that a statement is true if it corresponds to an aspect of reality. However, while both theories address the nature of truth, the Truthmaker Principle focuses more on the existence of entities that make truths true.

Empirically, the principle has been influential in various philosophical debates. For example, in discussions about scientific realism, the Truthmaker Principle can be used to evaluate whether a theory is realist by requiring that all its claims have corresponding truthmakers in reality.

## Mechanism

The mechanism of the Truthmaker Principle involves identifying and specifying what makes true propositions true. This process can vary depending on whether one adopts a maximalist or restricted version of the principle. Maximalist versions require that every truth, including those about absences and necessary truths, have corresponding truthmakers, while restricted versions only apply to positive contingent truths.

> The principle is often used to evaluate scientific theories, requiring that all claims about the world have corresponding truthmakers in reality. This helps prevent the acceptance of unverifiable or unsupported hypotheses, ensuring that scientific knowledge remains grounded in empirical evidence.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Philosophical ontology**
> In philosophical ontology, the Truthmaker Principle serves as a methodological constraint by forcing theorists to justify their ontological commitments. This ensures that every entity posited exists for a reason and is not merely an arbitrary addition to one's ontology.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Maximalist vs Restricted Versions**
> The maximalist version of the Truthmaker Principle requires that all truths, including those about absences and necessary truths, have corresponding truthmakers. In contrast, restricted versions only apply to positive contingent truths. The distinction is crucial because maximalist versions face well-known difficulties in explaining truths about absences and negative existentials, while restricted versions may not prevent cheap ontological commitments as effectively.
