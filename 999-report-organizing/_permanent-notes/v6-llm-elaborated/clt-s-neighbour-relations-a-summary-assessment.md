# Cognitive Load Theory

> [!definition] **Cognitive Load Theory**
> Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) is a framework that explains how the human mind processes information, focusing on the limits of working memory and the impact of instructional design on cognitive load. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], specifically addressing the mechanisms and limitations of working memory during learning without delving into long-term memory consolidation or other psychological phenomena outside immediate processing constraints. It falls under [[Cognitive Architecture]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> CLT focuses specifically on the mechanisms and limitations of working memory during learning. It does not address long-term memory consolidation or other psychological phenomena outside of immediate processing constraints.

## Core Explanation

CLT posits that human cognition is constrained by the capacity of working memory, which has a limited ability to process information. This theory suggests that instructional design should aim to minimize extraneous cognitive load and maximize germane cognitive load to enhance learning efficiency. For instance, when teaching complex mathematical concepts, instructors can use worked examples to reduce the intrinsic cognitive load associated with understanding new material.

In practice, CLT emphasizes the importance of carefully structuring information to align with how working memory processes it. This involves breaking down complex tasks into manageable components and providing learners with opportunities to practice these components before integrating them into a larger whole. By doing so, instructors can help students build schemas that facilitate deeper understanding and retention.

The theoretical roots of CLT lie in the study of cognitive architectures, particularly schema theory, which posits that knowledge is organized into interconnected structures or schemas. CLT builds upon this by focusing on how these schemas are formed and utilized during learning. The theory also draws from empirical research showing that learners benefit from well-structured instructional materials that reduce unnecessary mental effort.

Historically, CLT emerged in the 1980s with John Sweller's seminal work, which highlighted the importance of minimizing extraneous cognitive load to enhance learning outcomes. Since then, the theory has been applied across various educational settings, leading to significant improvements in instructional design and pedagogical practices.

## Mechanism

CLT distinguishes between three types of cognitive load: intrinsic, extraneous, and germane. Intrinsic cognitive load refers to the inherent difficulty of the material itself, which cannot be reduced by instructional design. Extraneous cognitive load is imposed by the way information is presented, such as through poorly designed multimedia presentations that overwhelm working memory. Germane cognitive load, on the other hand, involves the mental effort required to form new schemas and integrate new knowledge into existing ones.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, CLT suggests that worked examples can be used to reduce intrinsic cognitive load by providing learners with a clear model of how to solve problems. For example, when teaching algebra, instructors might provide step-by-step solutions to sample problems before asking students to attempt similar tasks on their own.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Problem spacing**
> CLT also supports the use of problem spacing, where learning is distributed over time rather than crammed into a single session. This approach helps reduce cognitive load by allowing learners to consolidate knowledge and form schemas more effectively. For instance, instead of teaching all algebraic concepts in one day, instructors might spread out lessons over several weeks.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Multimedia presentations**
> When designing multimedia presentations, CLT advises minimizing extraneous cognitive load by using clear, concise language and avoiding cluttered visual elements. For example, a presentation on cellular biology should use simple diagrams and avoid excessive text to keep the working memory from being overloaded.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Intrinsic cognitive load is inherent in the material itself and cannot be reduced by instructional design, whereas extraneous cognitive load is imposed by how information is presented. For example, a complex mathematical problem has an intrinsic cognitive load that cannot be altered, but the way it is explained can affect the extraneous cognitive load.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller originated CLT in 1988 and has been a key figure in its development. His work laid the foundation for understanding how working memory processes information, leading to significant improvements in instructional design.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> When does load management yield to difficulty enhancement?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further empirical research is needed to determine under what conditions managing cognitive load enhances learning versus making tasks too easy and thus less challenging for students.

## Synthesis

CLT is a critical framework for understanding cognitive processing in learning because it provides a clear model of how working memory operates during the acquisition of new knowledge. By focusing on reducing extraneous cognitive load and maximizing germane cognitive load, educators can design more effective instructional materials that enhance learning outcomes. The theory's implications extend beyond education into fields such as software development and user interface design, where minimizing cognitive load is crucial for usability.

The relationships between CLT and other theories like Multimedia Learning Theory, Desirable Difficulties, and Metacognition highlight the complexity of cognitive processing during learning. While CLT emphasizes managing cognitive load to facilitate schema formation, Multimedia Learning Theory focuses on how different media types can be used effectively in instruction. The undertheorized relationship with metacognition suggests that future research should explore how these concepts interact to better understand the full arc of learning.
