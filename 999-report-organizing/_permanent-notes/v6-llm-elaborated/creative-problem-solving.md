# Creative Problem-Solving

> [!definition] **Creative Problem-Solving**
> Creative Problem-Solving is a structured process that alternates divergent and convergent thinking phases to generate innovative solutions, originating from the Osborn-Parnes model. It falls under [[Cognitive Architecture]], as it involves systematic cognitive processes designed to enhance creative production toward viable solutions.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes unstructured brainstorming sessions without a defined problem statement or implementation plan. It should not be confused with spontaneous creative activities that lack systematic structure.

## Core Explanation

Creative Problem-Solving is a methodical approach that begins with problem clarification, where the nature and scope of the issue are defined clearly. This stage sets the foundation for subsequent phases by ensuring that all participants have a shared understanding of what needs to be solved. Following this, ideation involves generating as many ideas as possible without immediate evaluation, fostering an environment conducive to divergent thinking.

The development phase then transitions into convergent thinking, where solutions are critically evaluated and refined based on feasibility and practicality. This alternation between divergent and convergent phases is crucial; mixing the two prematurely can limit creativity by prematurely narrowing down options. The implementation stage ensures that selected solutions are put into action, completing the cycle of problem-solving.

The Osborn-Parnes model, developed by Alex Osborn and Sidney Parnes, provides a framework for this process, emphasizing the importance of structured stages to channel creative production effectively. This method is particularly valuable in educational settings and professional environments where innovation and adaptability are key.

Empirically, studies have shown that Creative Problem-Solving can enhance learning outcomes by fostering productive struggle and failure, which are essential for deep understanding and retention. In professional contexts, it has been linked to increased innovation and problem-solving efficiency.

## Mechanism

The process of Creative Problem-Solving alternates between divergent and convergent thinking at each stage. Divergent thinking is characterized by generating a wide range of ideas without immediate evaluation, while convergent thinking involves narrowing down these ideas to the most viable solutions through critical analysis.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Creative Problem-Solving can enhance learning outcomes by encouraging students to engage in productive struggle and failure. By allowing them to generate multiple ideas before evaluating their feasibility, educators can foster a deeper understanding of the subject matter.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Product development**
> In product development, applying Creative Problem-Solving ensures that teams consider a wide range of potential solutions before narrowing down to the most practical ones. This approach helps in creating innovative products that meet user needs more effectively.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Divergent Thinking vs Convergent Thinking**
> While divergent thinking focuses on generating a wide range of ideas, Creative Problem-Solving integrates both divergence and convergence. Divergence is crucial for ideation, while convergence ensures that only the most viable solutions are selected for implementation.
