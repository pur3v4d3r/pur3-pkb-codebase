# Cognitively Constructed Information

> [!definition] **Cognitively Constructed Information**
> Source information is not passively recorded but actively constructed through cognitive processes that vary among individuals and over time. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], as the way we process and interpret raw material from various sources shapes our beliefs and understanding.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes the idea of mechanical recording, focusing on the active construction of beliefs from raw material provided by various sources.

## Core Explanation

The core idea of source information being cognitively constructed rather than mechanically recorded is rooted in the notion that our minds do not simply store data like a computer. Instead, cognitive processes such as filtering, weighting, integrating, and interpreting raw material from sources play a crucial role in shaping our beliefs and perceptions. This active construction means that even identical experiences can lead to different outcomes based on individual differences in how these experiences are processed.

In practice, this concept operates through various stages of cognitive processing. For instance, when we encounter new information, it first enters our working memory where initial filtering occurs. Depending on the relevance and salience of the information, some details may be discarded while others are retained for further processing. Subsequently, these selected pieces of information undergo weighting based on prior knowledge and beliefs, leading to a more integrated understanding or belief formation.

Theoretical roots of this concept can be traced back to social-cognitive psychology, particularly through Albert Bandura's self-efficacy theory. According to Bandura, the four sources of efficacy information—performance accomplishments, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and emotional states—do not directly deposit beliefs into our minds but provide raw material that is then processed through cognitive operations. This processing varies across individuals due to differences in cognitive architectures and prior experiences.

Empirical evidence supporting this concept comes from studies on working memory and self-regulated learning (SRL). For example, research has shown that the way information is processed in working memory can significantly influence how it is later recalled or applied. Similarly, SRL models highlight the importance of metacognitive strategies in managing cognitive resources effectively, further emphasizing the active role of cognition in constructing knowledge.

## Mechanism

The process of cognitive construction involves several stages. Initially, raw information enters our working memory where it is filtered based on relevance and salience. This filtering ensures that only the most pertinent details are retained for further processing. Next, these selected pieces undergo weighting, a process influenced by prior knowledge and beliefs. Finally, integration occurs as new information is combined with existing knowledge to form coherent understanding or belief.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding that source information is cognitively constructed can lead to more effective learning materials. By tailoring content to the cognitive processes of learners, educators can enhance retention and application of knowledge. For instance, providing multiple examples and allowing for self-explanation can support better integration of new information with existing knowledge.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Self-regulated learning**
> For students engaging in SRL, recognizing that their beliefs are actively constructed helps them develop metacognitive strategies. By monitoring their own cognitive processes, learners can adjust their study habits and seek out additional resources to fill gaps in understanding. This self-awareness fosters a more adaptive approach to learning.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Mental health interventions**
> In mental health contexts, the concept of cognitive construction is crucial for developing therapeutic strategies. By helping clients understand how their beliefs are formed through cognitive processes, therapists can guide them towards healthier thought patterns and behaviors. Techniques such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) rely on this understanding to challenge and reframe maladaptive thoughts.

> [!example] **Application 4 — Marketing and advertising**
> For marketers, recognizing that consumers actively construct their beliefs from raw information allows for more effective communication strategies. By providing compelling narratives and aligning messages with existing values, advertisers can influence consumer perceptions and behaviors more effectively. This approach emphasizes the importance of storytelling in marketing campaigns.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Cognitive construction vs. Mechanical recording**
> While mechanical recording involves passively storing data without interpretation, cognitive construction actively processes raw material through filtering, weighting, and integrating to form beliefs. This distinction is crucial because it highlights the dynamic nature of information processing in the human mind compared to static storage systems.

## Key Figures

- **Albert Bandura** — Bandura is a key figure in this concept's development, particularly through his self-efficacy theory. His work emphasizes that beliefs are not passively received but actively constructed through cognitive processes involving performance accomplishments, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and emotional states.

## Synthesis

Understanding that source information is cognitively constructed rather than mechanically recorded has significant implications across various domains. In education, it underscores the importance of metacognitive strategies and personalized learning approaches. In mental health, it highlights the role of cognitive-behavioral interventions in reshaping maladaptive thought patterns. Moreover, this concept challenges traditional views on information storage and processing, emphasizing the dynamic and individualized nature of human cognition.

By integrating this understanding into broader cognitive architectures, we can develop more effective strategies for learning, therapy, and communication. This perspective also invites further research to explore how different cognitive architectures influence belief formation and how mechanical recording might be a useful analogy in specific contexts.
