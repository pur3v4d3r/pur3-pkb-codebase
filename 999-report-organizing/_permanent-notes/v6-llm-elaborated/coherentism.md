# Coherentism

> [!definition] **Coherentism**
> Coherentism is a structural thesis in epistemology that holds a belief is justified by virtue of its membership in a coherent system of beliefs — where coherence involves positive mutual support and explanatory connections, rather than mere consistency — and it excludes the idea of basic or self-justifying beliefs. It falls under [[epistemology]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> Coherentism excludes the idea of basic or self-justifying beliefs and focuses on the interconnectedness and relational structure within belief systems. It is distinct from foundationalism but related in that both address the problem of epistemic justification.

## Core Explanation

Coherentism challenges foundationalist views by proposing that a belief's justification is not based on its direct connection to reality but rather on how well it fits into a broader, interconnected network of beliefs. This holistic approach dissolves the regress problem, which arises when each belief seems to require another belief to justify it, leading to an infinite chain. Instead, coherentism suggests that each belief can be justified by its role within this system, where positive mutual support and explanatory connections ensure coherence.

In practice, a belief is considered justified if it coheres with other beliefs in the system, meaning it supports them and is supported by them. This interconnectedness ensures that no single belief stands alone but rather contributes to a larger, unified framework of knowledge. The concept of positive mutual support implies that each belief should enhance or be enhanced by others within the system, fostering a robust and integrated network.

Theoretical roots of coherentism can be traced back to the idea that knowledge is not built from isolated, self-justifying blocks but rather emerges from a complex web of interrelated beliefs. This perspective aligns with the holistic justification model, which emphasizes the relational structure within belief systems over individual foundational elements. Coherentists argue that this approach provides a more realistic and comprehensive account of how we actually come to believe things.

Empirically, coherentism has been influenced by cognitive science, particularly in understanding how humans process information and form beliefs. Research suggests that our minds naturally seek coherence when processing new information, leading us to integrate new beliefs into existing systems or adjust them to maintain consistency. This aligns with the idea that justification is a relational property, dependent on the overall structure of our belief system.

## Mechanism

Coherentist justification operates through positive mutual support and explanatory connections within a belief network. Positive mutual support means each belief strengthens others in the system, creating a reinforcing cycle where beliefs bolster one another. Explanatory connections involve beliefs contributing to or being explained by other beliefs, forming a web of interdependent knowledge.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, coherentism implies that educational materials should be structured in a way that promotes the integration of new information into existing knowledge. This means designing curricula and learning environments where students can see how new concepts fit with what they already know, fostering a coherent understanding rather than isolated facts.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Philosophical debate**
> In philosophical debates, coherentism suggests that the strength of an argument lies in its coherence within the broader context. Participants should aim to construct arguments that are well-integrated with their existing beliefs and those of others, rather than relying on isolated, self-justifying premises.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Coherentism vs Foundationalism**
> Coherentism and foundationalism differ fundamentally in how they address justification. Coherentists reject the idea of basic or self-justifying beliefs, instead proposing a holistic model where each belief is justified by its place within a coherent system. In contrast, foundationalists argue that some beliefs are self-justifying and require no further justification, forming an arbitrary stopping point in the regress problem.

## Key Figures

- **Laurence BonJour** — Laurence BonJour is a prominent proponent of coherentism who has developed influential arguments for its superiority over foundationalist views, emphasizing the importance of coherence in justifying beliefs.
- **Keith Lehrer** — Keith Lehrer is another key figure in coherentism, known for his work on the isolation objection and developing strategies to address it within a coherentist framework.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does coherentism address the isolation objection?
>
> *What would resolve it:* The isolation objection challenges whether coherence alone can be truth-conducive. Evidence from empirical studies on belief systems and cognitive processes could help resolve this issue by showing how coherence relates to truth.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can coherence alone be a truth-conducive property?
>
> *What would resolve it:* To determine if coherence is sufficient for truth, further research into the relationship between coherent belief systems and empirical accuracy would be necessary. This could involve analyzing case studies where beliefs are highly coherent but may not align with reality.

## Synthesis

Coherentism matters because it offers a compelling alternative to foundationalist views by emphasizing the interconnectedness of our beliefs. It provides a more realistic model of how we actually form and justify our beliefs, reflecting the way human cognition naturally operates. By integrating coherentism into broader philosophical discussions about knowledge and belief, it challenges us to rethink traditional epistemological frameworks and consider new ways of understanding justification.

The concept's significance extends beyond epistemology into cognitive science and educational theory, where its principles can inform more effective learning strategies and instructional design. Coherentism also has implications for practical applications in fields such as law and politics, where coherent argumentation is crucial.
