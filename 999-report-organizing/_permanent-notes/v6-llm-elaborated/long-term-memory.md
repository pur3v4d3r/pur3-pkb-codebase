# Long-Term Memory

> [!definition] **Long-Term Memory**
> Long-Term Memory (LTM) is the cognitive store of effectively unlimited capacity and duration that holds schemas, episodes, semantic knowledge, and procedural skills, constituting a person's accumulated learning. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], where its organized contents—schemas—circumvent working-memory limits, making expertise possible.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes short-term or sensory memory and focuses on the storage and organization of learned information that can be retrieved for long periods.

## Core Explanation

Long-Term Memory serves as the repository for our acquired knowledge and skills, ranging from factual information to complex procedural tasks. Schemas, which are mental frameworks that organize related concepts, play a crucial role in this process by allowing us to understand new information within familiar contexts. Episodes, or specific instances of events, contribute to episodic memory, enabling detailed recollection of personal experiences.

The organization of LTM is not merely passive; it involves active construction and modification through repeated retrieval. This constructive nature means that every time we recall a piece of information, we partially reconstruct and modify the stored content, which can lead to both consolidation and distortion over time. This process underscores why Long-Term Memory is often described as the substrate of expertise: experts do not necessarily have larger working memories but possess more efficient schemas and retrieval structures.

The theoretical roots of LTM are deeply intertwined with schema theory, which provides a framework for understanding how knowledge is organized in memory. Schema theory posits that our experiences and learned information are structured into schemas, which help us process new information efficiently by fitting it into existing frameworks. This organization allows for faster retrieval and more effective use of working memory when dealing with complex tasks.

Empirical evidence supports the constructive nature of LTM through studies on retrieval practice. Repeatedly retrieving information from Long-Term Memory strengthens neural connections, leading to better retention and transfer of knowledge. However, this also means that repeated exposure can lead to distortions in memory, as evidenced by phenomena like the misinformation effect.

## Mechanism

The constructive nature of retrieval from LTM involves a process where every act of recall partially reconstructs and modifies the stored content. This is why repeated retrieval practice not only consolidates information but also leads to potential distortions in memory, as new information can alter existing schemas.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, leveraging Long-Term Memory through retrieval practice is crucial. By regularly testing students on previously learned material, educators can enhance long-term retention and transfer of knowledge. This approach bypasses the limitations of working memory by focusing on strengthening schemas in LTM.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Expertise development**
> For developing expertise, investing instructional effort into constructing robust schemas is more effective than training working memory directly. Experts possess well-organized Long-Term Memory contents that allow them to bypass the working-memory bottleneck, enabling efficient processing of complex tasks.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Memory consolidation**
> Memory consolidation can be improved by spacing out retrieval practice over time. This technique helps in strengthening neural connections and enhancing long-term retention without leading to significant distortions in memory content.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of a task, while extraneous load is imposed by instructional design. Long-Term Memory plays a role in managing intrinsic load by providing pre-organized schemas that make complex tasks more manageable. In contrast, extraneous load can overwhelm working memory and hinder learning.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is a key contributor to the understanding of Long-Term Memory through his work on cognitive load theory. His research highlighted how schemas in LTM can circumvent working-memory limitations, making expertise possible.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does the constructive nature of retrieval affect long-term retention?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further empirical studies on the mechanisms of memory consolidation and distortion could provide insights into how repeated retrieval practice affects long-term retention.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the limits of LTM in terms of capacity and organization?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Research exploring the boundaries of LTM's capacity and the factors that influence its organization, such as age and individual differences, could help clarify these limitations.

## Synthesis

Understanding Long-Term Memory is crucial for grasping how knowledge is acquired, stored, and retrieved. It connects with schema theory to explain how our experiences are organized into mental frameworks that facilitate learning and expertise development. By leveraging instructional strategies like retrieval practice, educators can enhance long-term retention and transfer of knowledge, while also considering the constructive nature of memory retrieval.

Long-Term Memory's role in bypassing working-memory limitations is particularly important for developing expertise across various domains. Its organization into schemas allows experts to process complex tasks more efficiently, making it a central concept in cognitive psychology.
