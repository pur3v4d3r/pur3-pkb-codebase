# Reliabilism

> [!definition] **Reliabilism**
> Reliabilism is an externalist theory of justification that posits a belief is justified if it results from a reliable process — one that produces a sufficiently high ratio of true to false beliefs across relevant counterfactual circumstances — making justification a function of the actual reliability of cognitive mechanisms rather than the believer's reflective access to evidence. It falls under [[epistemology]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes internalist theories and focuses on the reliability of cognitive processes rather than the believer's reflective access to evidence.

## Core Explanation

At its core, Reliabilism shifts the focus from the internal states and processes of an individual to the external properties of their belief-forming mechanisms. This theory suggests that a belief is justified not by the believer's introspective awareness or reflective access to evidence but by the reliability of the process through which it was formed. For instance, if someone believes it will rain based on a reliable weather forecast system, this belief is justified according to Reliabilism because the forecasting system has historically produced true beliefs about the weather.

This approach aligns epistemology with empirical cognitive science by emphasizing accuracy and reliability over subjective introspection. By grounding justification in external properties like reliability, Reliabilism provides a framework that can account for the justified beliefs of young children and non-human animals who lack reflective access to their cognitive mechanisms. This makes it particularly useful in understanding how knowledge is formed without conscious awareness or explicit evidence.

The theory's reliance on empirical measures of reliability also means that it can be tested and refined through scientific inquiry, offering a bridge between philosophical theories of justification and psychological research. For example, studies in cognitive psychology might demonstrate the reliability of certain perceptual processes, thereby justifying beliefs formed via those processes according to Reliabilism.

However, this shift towards external properties also introduces challenges. It requires specifying which process-type's reliability matters for a given belief, as any token belief can be produced by multiple types of processes with varying degrees of reliability.

## Mechanism

Reliabilism operates on the principle that justification is determined by the reliability of cognitive mechanisms. For example, if someone perceives an object as red based on a reliable visual process, their belief in the object's color is justified. This mechanism ensures that beliefs formed through accurate and consistent processes are more likely to be true.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Reliabilism suggests that educational materials should focus on fostering reliable cognitive processes rather than merely providing evidence or information. For instance, designing curricula that enhance students' ability to form accurate and consistent beliefs about mathematical concepts can lead to more justified knowledge acquisition.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Cognitive science**
> In cognitive science, Reliabilism provides a framework for understanding how reliable processes contribute to the formation of justified beliefs. Researchers can use this theory to study the reliability of various perceptual and cognitive mechanisms, thereby gaining insights into the conditions under which beliefs are likely to be true.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Philosophical debates**
> In philosophical debates about justification, Reliabilism offers a way to address the generality problem by focusing on the reliability of belief-forming processes rather than individual instances. This approach can help resolve disputes between internalists and externalists by providing a clear criterion for justified beliefs.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Reliabilism vs Internalism**
> While Reliabilism focuses on the reliability of cognitive processes, internalist theories emphasize the believer's reflective access to evidence. For example, an internalist might argue that a belief is justified if it is based on introspective awareness or direct perception, whereas Reliabilism would consider whether the process through which the belief was formed is reliable.

## Key Figures

- **Alvin Goldman** — Alvin Goldman is credited with developing Reliabilism and providing a robust framework for understanding justification in terms of the reliability of cognitive processes. His work has significantly influenced epistemology by offering a clear alternative to internalist theories.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does Reliabilism address the generality problem?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further empirical research into the reliability of specific cognitive processes could help clarify which process-types matter for justification, thereby addressing the generality problem.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the implications of Reliabilism for understanding justified beliefs in non-human animals?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Studying the reliability of perceptual and cognitive mechanisms in non-human animals could provide insights into how they form justified beliefs, potentially validating or refuting Reliabilism's claims.

## Synthesis

Reliabilism matters because it offers a bridge between philosophical theories of justification and empirical research in psychology. By grounding justification in the reliability of cognitive processes, it provides a practical framework for understanding how knowledge is formed and justified across different contexts, from young children to non-human animals. This theory also challenges traditional internalist views by emphasizing external factors over subjective introspection, thereby enriching our understanding of epistemology.
