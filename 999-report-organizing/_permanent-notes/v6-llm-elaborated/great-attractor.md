# Great Attractor

> [!definition] **Great Attractor**
> The Great Attractor is a massive gravitational anomaly that pulls galaxies towards it at an approximate speed of 600 km/s, influencing the motion of the Local Group and Laniakea Supercluster. It lies behind the Zone of Avoidance in Centaurus, some 150–250 million parsecs away from us. This phenomenon is a local gravitational feature unrelated to cosmic expansion; it represents a peculiar velocity flow rather than an aspect of the universe's overall expansion. It falls under [[Large-Scale Gravitational Features]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It should not be confused with cosmic expansion; it represents a local gravitational anomaly rather than a cosmological phenomenon.

## Core Explanation

The Great Attractor was first identified in the late 1970s and early 1980s when astronomers noticed that galaxies were moving towards a common point with velocities higher than could be explained by known mass concentrations like the Virgo cluster. This anomaly suggested the presence of an unseen, massive structure exerting gravitational pull on surrounding regions.

Infrared and X-ray surveys have since helped to map this region more accurately, revealing that the Great Attractor is not a single galaxy but rather a concentration of galaxies and dark matter. These observations have allowed scientists to estimate its mass and direction with greater precision, confirming it as one of the most significant gravitational influences in our cosmic neighborhood.

Understanding the Great Attractor's role has been crucial for cosmologists studying large-scale structure formation and peculiar velocity fields. It highlights how local gravitational dynamics can significantly affect galactic motions on scales much larger than individual clusters or superclusters.

## Mechanism

Infrared surveys, which are less affected by dust in the Milky Way's plane, have been instrumental in mapping the Great Attractor’s influence. These observations revealed that galaxies within a vast region of space were moving towards a common point with velocities higher than expected from known mass concentrations like the Virgo cluster.

X-ray surveys further confirmed this finding by detecting hot gas associated with clusters of galaxies along the line of sight to the Great Attractor, indicating the presence of an unseen massive structure. Together, these techniques have provided a clearer picture of the Great Attractor’s gravitational influence and its mass.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Cosmological Models**
> The discovery of the Great Attractor has significant implications for cosmological models that aim to describe large-scale structure formation. It demonstrates that local gravitational anomalies can have a profound impact on galactic motions, challenging simplistic models that assume uniformity in mass distribution.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Peculiar Velocity Studies**
> Understanding the Great Attractor is crucial for studies of peculiar velocities, which are deviations from the smooth Hubble flow expected due to cosmic expansion. By accounting for the influence of the Great Attractor, researchers can more accurately measure and model these deviations, providing insights into the distribution of dark matter in our universe.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Local Gravitational Feature vs Cosmic Expansion**
> The Great Attractor is a local gravitational anomaly that influences galactic motions within its vicinity. It should not be confused with cosmic expansion, which describes the overall increase in distance between galaxies due to the universe's expansion. While both phenomena affect galaxy velocities, they operate on different scales and mechanisms.
