# Underdetermination Thesis

> [!definition] **Underdetermination Thesis**
> The Underdetermination Thesis is the claim that for any body of empirical evidence, there are multiple theories that can equally explain it, making theory selection not solely dependent on empirical evidence alone. It falls under [[epistemology]], as it concerns how we justify beliefs and knowledge claims in science.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This thesis does not include cases where theories are empirically distinguishable or where extra-empirical criteria (like simplicity) are used to select among them. It focuses specifically on the logical possibility of multiple theories being equally consistent with a given set of evidence.

## Core Explanation

At its core, the Underdetermination Thesis challenges the idea that empirical evidence is sufficient to determine which scientific theory is true. This thesis suggests that given a set of observations or data, there can be several theories that are equally consistent with them. For instance, Duhem and Quine have shown through their work that no single observation can definitively confirm or falsify a hypothesis; instead, it often requires a network of auxiliary hypotheses to support the theory.

In practice, this means that when faced with empirical evidence, scientists cannot rely solely on data to choose between competing theories. Theories must be evaluated based on additional criteria such as simplicity, coherence, and fruitfulness. These extra-empirical factors play a crucial role in theory selection, even though they are not directly derived from the empirical evidence itself.

Theoretical roots of this thesis can be traced back to logical positivism and its critique of verificationism. Philosophers like Duhem argued that no single observation can confirm or falsify a hypothesis because it is always embedded within a larger theoretical framework. Quine further developed these ideas, emphasizing the interconnectedness of scientific theories and the holistic nature of empirical evidence.

Historically, this thesis has been supported by numerous examples in science where multiple theories have been equally consistent with observed data. For example, in the early days of quantum mechanics, there were several competing interpretations that could account for the same experimental results.

## Mechanism

The mechanism behind underdetermination involves the interplay between a theory and its auxiliary hypotheses. According to Duhem's thesis, no single observation can confirm or falsify a hypothesis because it is always part of a larger set of assumptions and theoretical constructs. This means that when an observation seems to support a particular theory, it could equally be explained by another theory if the auxiliary hypotheses are adjusted accordingly.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding underdetermination can lead educators to consider multiple theories of learning and choose the one that best fits their context. For example, while both behaviorism and constructivism might explain student performance equally well in certain scenarios, the choice between them would depend on additional criteria such as pedagogical effectiveness or alignment with educational goals.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Scientific realism**
> For scientific realists, underdetermination poses a challenge because it suggests that empirical evidence alone cannot justify belief in the truth of a theory. This implies that scientists must rely on other factors like simplicity and fruitfulness to select theories, which may not be purely rational.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Theory selection criteria**
> In practice, scientists often use extra-empirical criteria such as simplicity or coherence to choose between equally supported theories. These criteria are essential for theory selection but require justification themselves, leading to a complex interplay of empirical and non-empirical factors in scientific practice.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Underdetermination vs Relativism**
> While the Underdetermination Thesis suggests that multiple theories can be equally consistent with evidence, it does not imply that any theory is as good as any other. In contrast, relativism posits that truth is relative to a particular perspective or context, which goes beyond the logical possibility of empirical equivalence.

## Key Figures

- **Pierre Duhem** — Duhem was a proponent of underdetermination and argued that no single observation can confirm or falsify a hypothesis because it is always part of a larger theoretical framework.
- **W. V. O. Quine** — Quine further developed the idea of underdetermination, emphasizing the interconnectedness of scientific theories and the holistic nature of empirical evidence.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How often do genuinely empirically-equivalent theories arise in scientific practice?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further empirical studies could provide insights into how frequently genuine empirical equivalents occur, helping to clarify the practical implications of underdetermination.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can extra-empirical criteria ever justify theory selection?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Philosophical and empirical research on the justification of non-empirical criteria in scientific practice would help resolve this question.

## Synthesis

Understanding underdetermination is crucial for epistemology and philosophy of science because it highlights the limitations of empirical evidence alone in justifying scientific theories. It challenges scientists to consider a broader range of factors, including simplicity and coherence, which have significant implications for how we interpret and justify our beliefs about the world.

The concept also intersects with other areas such as instructional design, where multiple theories can be equally supported by data, and scientific realism, which grapples with the relationship between empirical evidence and truth. By recognizing underdetermination, scientists and philosophers can better navigate the complexities of theory selection and knowledge justification.
