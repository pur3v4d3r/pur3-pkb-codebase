# Scientific Realism

> [!definition] **Scientific Realism**
> Scientific Realism is the philosophical thesis that mature, predictively successful scientific theories should be interpreted as approximately true descriptions of an observer-independent reality, including the unobservable entities they posit — contrasted with anti-realist positions (instrumentalism, constructive empiricism) that treat theories as predictive instruments without ontological commitment to their unobservable posits. It falls under [[Philosophy]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes anti-realist positions like instrumentalism and constructive empiricism, which do not commit to the ontological status of unobservable entities posited by scientific theories.

## Core Explanation

Scientific Realism asserts that the successful scientific theories we have are not merely useful tools but provide us with a genuine understanding of the world, including its unobservable aspects. This view is supported by the no-miracles argument, which posits that if our theories were not approximately true, their remarkable predictive and technological success would be inexplicable.

However, Scientific Realism faces significant challenges from pessimistic meta-induction, a critique that points out how past scientific theories have been superseded by new ones, suggesting that current successful theories might also be false. This raises doubts about the reliability of our current understanding of reality.

Theoretical roots of Scientific Realism can be traced back to the logical positivists and their verificationist criteria for meaningful statements. However, contemporary versions like structural realism and entity realism have modified this position by acknowledging that while we might not fully understand all aspects of a theory, we can still make sense of its structure or certain entities it posits.

Empirically, Scientific Realism is grounded in the historical success of scientific theories. For instance, the development of quantum mechanics and general relativity has led to profound technological advancements that would be impossible if these theories were not at least approximately true.

## Mechanism

Scientific Realism operates by interpreting mature scientific theories as providing a partial but genuine description of reality. This interpretation is based on the success of these theories in making accurate predictions and driving technological innovations, which would be difficult to explain if they were merely instrumental tools.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Scientific Realism implies that educational materials should reflect the best current scientific understanding of a subject. For example, teaching evolution as a well-supported theory rather than just a useful model would align with this view and ensure students are exposed to accurate knowledge.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Policy-making**
> When formulating policies based on scientific advice, policymakers should consider the realist interpretation of these theories. For instance, climate change policies should be grounded in the best available scientific understanding rather than treating it as a mere predictive tool.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Scientific Realism vs Constructive Empiricism**
> While Scientific Realism holds that mature theories are approximately true, constructive empiricism argues that we should only commit to the empirical success of a theory and not its unobservable entities. This distinction matters because it affects how one interprets the role of scientific theories in understanding reality.

> [!key-distinction] **Scientific Realism vs Structural Realism**
> Structural realism is a more qualified form of realism that retreats from full-theoretic realism by focusing on the structural aspects of theories rather than their ontological commitments. This distinction highlights how different versions of realism address the problem of unobservable entities in scientific theories.

## Key Figures

- **Hilary Putnam** — A prominent proponent of Scientific Realism, Hilary Putnam argued that mature scientific theories provide us with a genuine understanding of the world and its unobservable aspects.
- **Bas van Fraassen** — Bas van Fraassen is a key critic of unrestricted Scientific Realism. He advocates for constructive empiricism, arguing that we should only commit to the empirical success of theories without ontological commitment to their unobservable entities.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What is the nature of scientific progress in relation to Scientific Realism?
>
> *What would resolve it:* A clear understanding of how scientific progress can be reconciled with the limitations and fallibility of current theories would help resolve this question.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does Scientific Realism address the problem of underdetermination?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further empirical evidence or philosophical arguments that clarify the relationship between theory and observation could provide insights into how underdetermined theories can still be considered approximately true.

## Synthesis

Scientific Realism is a significant concept in the philosophy of science because it provides a framework for understanding the nature of scientific theories and their relationship to reality. By asserting that mature, successful theories are at least approximately true descriptions of an observer-independent reality, Scientific Realism offers a robust account of scientific knowledge. This view has implications for various domains, including education, policy-making, and the broader philosophical discourse on the nature of scientific progress.
