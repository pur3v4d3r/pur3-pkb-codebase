# Relatedness

> [!definition] **Relatedness**
> Relatedness is the need to feel connected and significant within relationships, characterized by mutual warmth and genuine care rather than mere social contact. It falls under Self-Determination Theory (SDT), where it is essential for intrinsic motivation and well-being.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> Relatedness is not satisfied by superficial or conditional interactions. It requires authentic connection and care, distinguishing it from general social interaction.

## Core Explanation

At its core, relatedness involves feeling valued and cared for in our interpersonal connections. This need is not merely about having companions but about experiencing genuine warmth and support from others. In SDT, relatedness is one of the three fundamental psychological needs that drive human motivation, alongside competence and autonomy.

In practice, relatedness manifests through meaningful interactions where individuals feel understood and supported by their social environment. For instance, in educational settings, students who experience a sense of belonging are more likely to engage actively with learning materials and persist through challenges. This underscores the importance of fostering supportive relationships between teachers and students.

Theoretical roots of relatedness trace back to SDT's emphasis on intrinsic motivation. Deci and Ryan argue that when individuals feel connected, they are more likely to pursue activities for their inherent value rather than external rewards. This aligns with empirical findings showing that social support enhances academic performance and psychological well-being.

Historically, the concept of relatedness has evolved from early theories of human needs, such as Maslow's hierarchy of needs, which also recognized the importance of love and belonging. However, SDT provides a more nuanced framework by integrating relatedness with other motivational needs like competence and autonomy.

## Mechanism

Relatedness is satisfied through mutual warmth and genuine care, distinguishing it from superficial interactions. For example, in therapy, a therapist's empathetic response can significantly enhance the client's sense of relatedness, leading to better therapeutic outcomes. This mechanism underscores the importance of authentic connections in fostering intrinsic motivation.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, incorporating elements that promote a sense of community and support can enhance student engagement and learning outcomes. For instance, group projects and peer feedback mechanisms can foster relatedness among students, leading to more collaborative and effective learning environments.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Therapeutic settings**
> In therapeutic settings, building strong, supportive relationships between therapists and clients is crucial for successful treatment. Therapists who provide a warm, caring environment can help clients feel understood and valued, which is essential for their psychological well-being.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Workplace settings**
> In the workplace, fostering a sense of relatedness among employees can improve job satisfaction and productivity. Managers who create an inclusive work culture where employees feel supported and valued are more likely to see higher levels of engagement and commitment from their teams.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Relatedness vs Autonomy**
> While both relatedness and autonomy are core needs in SDT, they differ in focus. Relatedness is about feeling connected and cared for by others, whereas autonomy pertains to the need for personal control and self-determination. For example, a student who feels supported by their teacher (relatedness) may be more motivated to take initiative on projects (autonomy).

> [!key-distinction] **Relatedness vs Competence**
> Competence involves feeling effective in one's actions, while relatedness is about feeling connected and significant within relationships. A person who feels competent might excel at a task but still struggle with feelings of isolation if they lack social connections. Conversely, someone with strong relatedness may feel supported even if their competence varies.

## Key Figures

- **Edward Deci** — Co-originator of SDT, Edward Deci has been instrumental in developing the theory and its applications. His research on intrinsic motivation and relatedness has significantly contributed to our understanding of human behavior.
- **Richard Ryan** — Also a co-originator of SDT, Richard Ryan has extensively researched the role of relatedness in psychological well-being and motivation. His work has highlighted the importance of supportive relationships for intrinsic motivation.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How does relatedness interact with other motivational needs?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further research exploring the interplay between relatedness, competence, and autonomy could provide insights into how these needs complement or conflict with each other in different contexts.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can relatedness be satisfied through technology?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical studies examining the effectiveness of digital platforms in fostering meaningful connections would help determine whether virtual interactions can satisfy the need for relatedness as effectively as face-to-face relationships.

## Synthesis

Understanding and addressing the need for relatedness is crucial across various domains, from education to therapy and workplace settings. By promoting supportive relationships, educators, therapists, and managers can enhance intrinsic motivation and overall well-being. The integration of relatedness with other motivational needs like competence and autonomy provides a comprehensive framework for understanding human behavior and fostering positive outcomes.

The broader implications of relatedness extend beyond individual psychology into organizational and societal contexts. Organizations that prioritize building supportive relationships among employees are more likely to create thriving work environments, while societies that foster strong social connections can promote greater happiness and resilience.
