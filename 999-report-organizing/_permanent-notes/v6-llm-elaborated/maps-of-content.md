# Maps of Content

> [!definition] **Maps of Content**
> Maps of Content are curated index notes that group, sequence, and contextualize a set of related atomic notes, providing the navigational scaffolding that pure tag-and-link networks lack while preserving the atomic structure of the underlying notes. This concept falls under [[knowledge-management]], as it enhances the organization and accessibility of knowledge within systems like V6 Pipeline.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes pure tag-and-link networks but includes more structured note-taking methods like LYT framework.

## Core Explanation

Maps of Content serve a crucial role in knowledge management by offering a structured way to navigate through a vast array of atomic notes. These index notes are designed to guide users through the content, ensuring that related information is easily accessible and logically sequenced. By curating these maps, one can create a coherent narrative or instructional pathway that enhances understanding and retention.

In practice, Maps of Content operate by first identifying key atomic notes that contain essential knowledge. These notes are then grouped into thematic categories based on their content and relevance. The map itself is created through a process of synthesis, where the creator weaves together these atomic notes to form a logical flow. This approach ensures that users can follow a clear path from one piece of information to another, making it easier to understand complex topics.

Theoretical roots of Maps of Content can be traced back to cognitive load theory, particularly the work of John Sweller, who emphasized the importance of minimizing extraneous cognitive load by structuring information in a way that supports learning. By providing a clear and organized path through atomic notes, Maps of Content help reduce the mental effort required to understand complex subjects.

Historically, the concept has been popularized by Nick Milo's LYT framework, which advocates for the use of these maps as an intermediate layer between raw atomic notes and higher-level summaries. This approach ensures that the underlying knowledge remains intact while providing a navigational aid that enhances user experience.

## Mechanism

The creation process begins with identifying key atomic notes that contain essential information. These notes are then grouped into thematic categories based on their content and relevance. The map is constructed by weaving together these atomic notes to form a logical flow, ensuring that users can follow a clear path from one piece of information to another.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Maps of Content provide a structured pathway for learners to navigate through complex topics. By organizing atomic notes into a coherent narrative, instructors can ensure that students follow a logical sequence of information, enhancing their understanding and retention.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Project management**
> For project managers, Maps of Content offer a way to organize project documentation and resources. This ensures that team members can easily find relevant information, reducing the time spent searching for specific details and improving overall efficiency.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Maps of Content vs Evergreen Notes**
> While both aim to manage knowledge, Maps of Content focus on providing navigational scaffolding through curated index notes, whereas Evergreen Notes are more about maintaining a single, evolving document that captures the latest information.

> [!key-distinction] **Maps of Content vs Atomic Notes**
> Atomic Notes contain the raw, granular pieces of knowledge, while Maps of Content build upon these by providing a higher-level structure for navigation and organization. This distinction ensures that both layers serve their unique purposes without conflating them.
