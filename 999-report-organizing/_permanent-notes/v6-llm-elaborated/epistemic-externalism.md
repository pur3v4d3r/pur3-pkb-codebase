# Epistemic Externalism

> [!definition] **Epistemic Externalism**
> Epistemic Externalism posits that justification for beliefs can depend on factors external to the believer's mental states, such as the reliability of belief-forming processes or the actual relation between a belief and its truth-maker. This view contrasts with internalist theories which require justification to be based solely on introspectively accessible mental states. It falls under Theories of Justification in epistemology.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes internalist theories which hold that justification must be based solely on internal states accessible through introspection. It should not be confused with epistemological views that deny any role for external factors in justification.

## Core Explanation

Epistemic Externalism challenges traditional views by asserting that one can have justified beliefs even if the believer is not aware of or cannot articulate why those beliefs are justified. This perspective allows for a broader understanding of knowledge acquisition, particularly in cases where individuals, such as animals and young children, lack reflective access to their justifications.

The core tenet of Epistemic Externalism is that justification can be grounded in factors outside the believer's mind, such as the reliability of cognitive processes or environmental interactions. This contrasts sharply with internalist theories which demand that justification must stem from mental states accessible through introspection alone.

Epistemic Externalism has theoretical roots in discussions about the nature and scope of knowledge, particularly how it can be justified without relying solely on internal mental states. The theory introduces nuances to epistemological debates by emphasizing the role of external factors in determining whether a belief is justified.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Epistemic Externalism suggests that learning can occur through reliable processes even if learners are not aware of the underlying mechanisms. This implies that educational strategies should focus on creating environments and activities that reliably lead to knowledge acquisition, rather than solely relying on students' ability to articulate their understanding.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Understanding animal cognition**
> Epistemic Externalism provides a framework for attributing justified beliefs to animals based on the reliability of their cognitive processes. This can help in designing experiments and interpreting behaviors that demonstrate knowledge acquisition without requiring introspective access, thus broadening our understanding of non-human cognition.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Reliability-based vs Truth-tracking approaches**
> Within Epistemic Externalism, there is a distinction between reliability-based and truth-tracking approaches. Reliability-based theories focus on the process by which beliefs are formed, emphasizing that justified beliefs arise from reliable processes regardless of their actual relation to truth. In contrast, truth-tracking theories require that justified beliefs must be true or closely related to the truth.

## Key Figures

- **Alvin Goldman** — Alvin Goldman is a key figure in Epistemic Externalism, particularly known for his work on reliabilism. He argues that justified beliefs are those formed by reliable processes, thus emphasizing the role of external factors in justification.

## Synthesis

Understanding Epistemic Externalism is crucial as it broadens our epistemological framework by incorporating external factors into theories of justification. This perspective not only challenges traditional internalist views but also offers a more inclusive approach to understanding knowledge acquisition across different cognitive capacities and contexts.
