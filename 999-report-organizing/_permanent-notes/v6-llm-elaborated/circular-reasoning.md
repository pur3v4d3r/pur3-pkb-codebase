# Circular Reasoning

> [!definition] **Circular Reasoning**
> Circular Reasoning is a logical fallacy where the conclusion of an argument is assumed in one of its premises, making it invalid for providing independent support to the conclusion. It falls under [[Informal Fallacy]], as it fails to offer a rational basis for accepting the conclusion that is not already presupposed.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes valid arguments that are logically sound and do not presuppose the conclusion. It should not be confused with other forms of circularity or redundancy in reasoning.

## Core Explanation

Circular Reasoning occurs when an argument's conclusion is assumed in one of its premises, rendering the argument trivially valid but uninformative. For instance, claiming 'God exists because the Bible says so' and 'the Bible is true because God wrote it' exemplifies this fallacy. The argument does not provide independent support for its conclusion; instead, it presupposes what it aims to prove.

In practice, Circular Reasoning can be subtle, often disguised through paraphrase or synonym substitution. For example, stating that 'All humans are mortal because they will eventually die' and then rephrasing it as 'All humans are mortal since their mortality is a defining characteristic of humanity' still presupposes the conclusion within the premise.

Theoretical roots of Circular Reasoning lie in the nature of logical arguments and the requirement for independent support. Valid arguments must be constructed such that each premise provides new information leading to the conclusion, whereas Circular Reasoning fails this criterion by relying on circularity rather than logical inference.

Empirically, Circular Reasoning is a common pitfall in everyday discourse and academic writing. Identifying it requires reconstructing the argument in its normal form, separating surface presentation from underlying logic. This process highlights how easily arguments can be disguised to appear valid when they are not.

## Mechanism

Circular Reasoning can be disguised through paraphrase or synonym substitution. For example, stating 'All humans are mortal because they will eventually die' and then rephrasing it as 'All humans are mortal since their mortality is a defining characteristic of humanity' still presupposes the conclusion within the premise.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Circular Reasoning can undermine effective learning. If an argument assumes what it aims to prove, learners may not gain new insights or understanding. For instance, explaining a scientific concept by assuming the very principle being taught would fail to provide meaningful information.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Legal reasoning**
> In legal contexts, Circular Reasoning can lead to flawed arguments and decisions. If judges rely on circular logic in their rulings, it undermines the integrity of the judicial process and the rule of law. For example, a judge might argue that a statute is valid because it has been enacted by a legitimate legislative body, which itself is assumed to be legitimate.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Academic research**
> In academic research, Circular Reasoning can hinder the advancement of knowledge. If researchers rely on circular arguments in their methodologies or conclusions, they may fail to provide new insights or evidence. This can lead to a cycle where existing beliefs are reinforced without genuine progress in understanding.

> [!example] **Application 4 — Spaced retrieval in MOOCs**
> In Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), spaced retrieval can help mitigate the effects of Circular Reasoning by reinforcing understanding through repeated exposure over time. When learners encounter circular arguments, they may initially accept them due to lack of critical thinking skills or familiarity with logical fallacies. By spacing out encounters with these concepts and providing opportunities for reflection and discussion, MOOC designers can enhance learners' ability to detect and reject circular reasoning.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Circular Reasoning is not about the amount of cognitive load but rather about the logical structure of an argument. In contrast, intrinsic and extraneous loads pertain to how information is processed in working memory. Circular arguments fail because they presuppose the conclusion, whereas issues with intrinsic or extraneous load relate to the efficiency and effectiveness of processing new information.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!key-distinction] **Reflective vs Reactive Thinking**
> Circular Reasoning often relies on reactive thinking, where individuals respond quickly without fully analyzing the logical structure of an argument. In contrast, reflective thinking involves a deliberate examination and questioning of premises and conclusions. Engaging in reflective thinking can help identify circular reasoning by prompting deeper analysis of whether the conclusion is truly supported independently.

## Common Misconceptions

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!warning] **Misconception** — People think that Circular Reasoning only occurs when an argument explicitly states its conclusion as a premise.
>
> Circular Reasoning can be more subtle, occurring through paraphrasing or synonym substitution where the conclusion is implicitly assumed in the premises. This form of circularity can be harder to detect because it does not directly state the conclusion but rather restates it in different terms.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller, a cognitive psychologist, is credited with formalizing the concept of Circular Reasoning in his work on cognitive load theory. His research highlighted how circular arguments can be particularly misleading and ineffective in educational settings.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can we reliably detect disguised forms of Circular Reasoning?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Developing more robust methods for reconstructing and analyzing the logical structure of arguments could help identify hidden circularities.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term effects of engaging in or being exposed to circular arguments?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies examining the impact of circular reasoning on critical thinking skills and decision-making processes would provide valuable insights.

<!-- enhancement-pass:1 (2026-05-02) -->

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What strategies can educators use to help students recognize and avoid Circular Reasoning in their own arguments?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Educators could develop exercises that require students to break down arguments into premises and conclusions, then evaluate whether the conclusion is independently supported. This would foster critical thinking skills necessary for identifying circular reasoning.

## Synthesis

Circular Reasoning is crucial for argument analysis and critical thinking because it highlights the importance of logical coherence. By recognizing and avoiding Circular Reasoning, individuals can ensure that their arguments are based on independent evidence rather than circular assumptions. This concept intersects with working memory by emphasizing how information processing can be misled if not carefully analyzed. Through worked examples in instructional design, learners can better identify and avoid circular reasoning, enhancing the effectiveness of educational materials.

Circular Reasoning also plays a significant role in legal and academic contexts, where clear and logical arguments are essential for sound decision-making and knowledge advancement. Understanding this fallacy helps maintain the integrity of these domains by ensuring that arguments are based on independent evidence rather than circular assumptions.
