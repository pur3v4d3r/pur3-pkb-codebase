# Intertemporal Choice

> [!definition] **Intertemporal Choice**
> Intertemporal Choice involves making decisions between options that have different outcomes at different times, often leading to preferences for immediate rewards over larger future ones. It falls under [[decision-science]], encompassing both the descriptive question of how people actually discount the future and the normative question of how they ought to.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes other decision-making processes not involving time, such as risk assessment or preference for certainty. It also does not encompass all forms of delayed gratification but focuses on the discounting of future rewards relative to present ones.

## Core Explanation

Intertemporal Choice is a fundamental concept in behavioral economics that explores how individuals make decisions involving trade-offs between immediate and delayed rewards. This choice often results in a preference for smaller, sooner rewards over larger, later ones, a phenomenon known as present bias. The standard paradigm of 'smaller-sooner vs. larger-later' monetary rewards has been extensively studied, revealing that people tend to discount future rewards more steeply when the delay is closer, leading to predictable preference reversals.

The core mechanism underlying Intertemporal Choice involves a cognitive process where individuals evaluate immediate and delayed outcomes differently based on their proximity in time. This evaluation can lead to suboptimal decisions, such as choosing smaller, sooner rewards despite knowing that larger, later ones would be more beneficial in the long run. The concept of present bias is central here, highlighting how people are often biased towards immediate gratification over future benefits.

Theoretical roots of Intertemporal Choice can be traced back to the work of Richard Thaler and others who introduced the idea that individuals do not always act rationally when making decisions involving time. The standard model assumes exponential discounting, where the value of a reward decreases at a constant rate over time. However, empirical evidence suggests that people often exhibit hyperbolic discounting instead, where the decrease in value accelerates as the delay approaches, leading to more pronounced present bias.

Empirical studies have shown that Intertemporal Choice is not just an abstract concept but has significant real-world implications. For instance, financial planning and health behaviors are heavily influenced by how individuals make decisions involving time. In financial contexts, people may choose to spend rather than save due to hyperbolic discounting, leading to poor long-term financial outcomes. Similarly, in health behaviors, the preference for immediate gratification over future well-being can result in unhealthy lifestyle choices.

## Mechanism

The key mechanism underlying Intertemporal Choice is the difference between exponential and hyperbolic discounting. Exponential discounting assumes a constant rate of decrease in value over time, while hyperbolic discounting shows an accelerating rate as the delay approaches. This means that people tend to prefer immediate rewards more strongly when they are closer in time, leading to preference reversals where larger future rewards become preferred as the earlier option gets closer.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding Intertemporal Choice can help educators create learning materials that account for students' present bias. For example, breaking down complex tasks into smaller, immediate rewards can enhance motivation and engagement, leading to better long-term outcomes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Financial planning**
> Intertemporal Choice plays a crucial role in financial planning. People often make poor financial decisions by choosing immediate gratification over long-term savings. By understanding this concept, individuals can develop strategies to save more effectively, such as setting up automatic transfers or using tools like 529 plans for education.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Health behaviors**
> In health behaviors, Intertemporal Choice affects decisions related to diet and exercise. People may choose immediate pleasure over long-term well-being, leading to poor health outcomes. By recognizing this bias, individuals can develop better habits through techniques like setting up future rewards for healthy choices.

> [!example] **Application 4 — Policy-making**
> Understanding Intertemporal Choice is essential for policymakers in designing effective interventions. For example, policies aimed at reducing smoking or drug use often fail because they do not account for the present bias of individuals who prefer immediate pleasure over long-term health benefits. By addressing this bias, more effective public health campaigns can be developed.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Hyperbolic vs Exponential Discounting**
> Hyperbolic discounting and exponential discounting differ in how they model the decrease in value of future rewards over time. Hyperbolic discounting shows an accelerating rate as the delay approaches, leading to more pronounced present bias, while exponential discounting assumes a constant rate of decrease. This distinction is crucial for understanding why people often make suboptimal decisions involving time.

> [!key-distinction] **Present Bias vs Other Cognitive Biases**
> While Intertemporal Choice encompasses present bias, it also includes other cognitive biases that affect decision-making over time. Present bias specifically refers to the tendency to prefer immediate rewards over larger future ones, while other biases might involve risk aversion or loss aversion. Understanding these distinctions helps in developing more accurate models of human behavior.

## Synthesis

Understanding Intertemporal Choice is crucial for comprehending how people make decisions involving time. This concept has significant implications across multiple domains, including behavioral economics, psychology, and public policy. By recognizing the role of present bias in decision-making, individuals can develop strategies to overcome it, leading to better long-term outcomes. Moreover, policymakers can design more effective interventions by accounting for these biases, ultimately improving public health and financial well-being.

Intertemporal Choice also bridges the gap between descriptive and normative questions in economics, providing a framework for understanding why people often make suboptimal decisions involving time. This concept is not just an abstract theory but has practical applications that can be observed in everyday life, from personal finance to health behaviors.

## Evidence

Empirical evidence consistently shows that discounting is hyperbolic rather than exponential, meaning the ratio of preferences between two delayed rewards depends on whether both are evaluated from a near or distant present. This generates predictable preference reversals as the earlier option approaches and yields the formal signature of present-biased self-control failure.
