# Qualia

> [!definition] **Qualia**
> Qualia are the intrinsic, subjective properties of conscious experiences that make them what they feel like from the inside; these qualitative aspects of experience constitute 'what it is like' to undergo a mental state and form a central battleground in physicalist ontology, as they challenge reductive accounts of consciousness by highlighting an explanatory gap between functional and neural processes and subjective experience. It falls under [[Consciousness Theories]].

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This definition excludes functional and neural accounts of cognition, focusing on the qualitative aspects of experience.

## Core Explanation

Qualia are the intrinsic properties that make conscious experiences what they feel like from the inside. For example, the redness of red or the painfulness of pain are qualia. These properties cannot be fully captured by functional descriptions or neural correlates alone; instead, they represent a subjective dimension of experience that is central to understanding consciousness.

The challenge posed by qualia arises because even if we have a complete account of how the brain functions and processes information, this does not explain why these processes are accompanied by subjective experiences. This gap between functional descriptions and qualitative experiences is known as the 'hard problem' of consciousness, first articulated by David Chalmers.

Qualia play a crucial role in philosophical debates about the nature of consciousness. For instance, Frank Jackson's Knowledge Argument (Mary the color scientist) illustrates that someone who has complete scientific knowledge but no subjective experience cannot fully understand what it is like to have qualia. This thought experiment highlights the irreducibility of qualitative experiences and their importance in understanding consciousness.

Thomas Nagel's 'What Is It Like to Be a Bat?' further emphasizes the subjective nature of qualia by questioning how we can know what it feels like to be an animal with radically different sensory systems from ours, thus reinforcing the idea that there is more to experience than mere functional or neural processes.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding qualia can help educators create learning experiences that are not just informative but also engaging and meaningful. By focusing on the subjective experience of learners, designers can tailor content to enhance emotional and cognitive engagement, leading to better retention and application of knowledge.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Neuroscience research**
> In neuroscience, studying qualia can inform the development of new methods for understanding brain states that go beyond mere functional descriptions. By integrating qualitative reports from subjects with neuroimaging data, researchers can gain a more comprehensive picture of how subjective experiences arise from neural activity.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Qualia are intrinsic properties that are directly experienced and cannot be reduced to external factors. In contrast, extraneous load refers to additional cognitive demands imposed by the environment or task, which can affect but do not constitute subjective experience.

## Key Figures

- **Frank Jackson** — Jackson introduced the Knowledge Argument (Mary the color scientist) to argue for the existence of qualia and their irreducibility to functional or neural descriptions.
- **Thomas Nagel** — Nagel's 'What Is It Like to Be a Bat?' thought experiment highlights the subjective nature of experience, challenging reductive accounts that focus solely on objective, observable phenomena.
- **David Chalmers** — Chalmers formalized the hard problem of consciousness and introduced the concept of qualia as central to understanding why subjective experiences arise from physical processes.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What is the nature of the explanatory gap between functionalism and qualia?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Resolving this question would require a theory that can bridge the gap between functional descriptions and subjective experiences, potentially through new insights into the neural basis of consciousness.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can qualia be reduced to physical processes?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Evidence from neuroscience and philosophy could help determine whether qualitative experiences are emergent properties of brain activity or if they require additional non-physical factors.

## Synthesis

Qualia matter because they challenge our understanding of consciousness, pushing us to consider the subjective dimension that cannot be fully explained by functional or neural accounts alone. By integrating insights from philosophy and neuroscience, we can develop a more comprehensive theory of consciousness that acknowledges both the objective and subjective aspects of experience.

Understanding qualia has implications across various domains, including instructional design, where it informs the creation of engaging learning experiences, and neuroscience research, which seeks to bridge the gap between qualitative reports and neuroimaging data. This concept also plays a crucial role in broader philosophical debates about the nature of reality and our understanding of subjective experience.
