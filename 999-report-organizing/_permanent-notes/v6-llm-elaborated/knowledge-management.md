# Knowledge Management

> [!definition] **Knowledge Management**
> Knowledge Management is the practice of capturing, organizing, retrieving, sharing, and applying knowledge within an organization to improve performance over time. It falls under [[Organizational Psychology]], distinguishing explicit from tacit knowledge and recognizing that the most valuable organizational knowledge is often tacit; thus, effective Knowledge Management programs integrate document repositories with communities of practice and apprenticeship structures.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes personal knowledge management techniques like Zettelkasten but includes organizational aspects such as communities of practice and transactive memory systems.

## Core Explanation

At its core, Knowledge Management involves a tripartite approach: capturing both explicit (codified) and tacit (personal or contextual) knowledge, organizing this information in accessible ways, retrieving it when needed, sharing it across the organization, and applying it to improve performance. This practice is not merely about storing documents but about creating an environment where knowledge can flow freely among employees.

In practice, Knowledge Management operates through a combination of formal processes and informal practices. For instance, regular meetings or 'knowledge cafes' allow tacit knowledge to be shared informally, while structured documentation systems ensure that explicit knowledge is easily accessible. The success of these initiatives often hinges on the organization's culture, which must support knowledge sharing and collaboration.

Theoretical roots of Knowledge Management can be traced back to organizational psychology, particularly in how it addresses the cognitive load theory proposed by John Sweller. This theory suggests that managing both explicit (easily codified) and tacit (harder to articulate) knowledge requires different strategies; effective Knowledge Management programs balance these approaches.

Empirical evidence supports the importance of integrating technology with social practices. For example, a study found that organizations using only document management systems saw lower improvements in performance compared to those combining such tools with communities of practice and mentorship programs.

## Mechanism

Knowledge Management integrates people, processes, and technology through specific mechanisms. People are engaged through training and incentives to share knowledge effectively. Processes include regular meetings and workshops for informal sharing, as well as structured documentation systems for explicit knowledge. Technology plays a crucial role in facilitating these interactions, such as using collaborative platforms like Slack or Microsoft Teams.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Knowledge Management can enhance the development of training materials by integrating both explicit and tacit knowledge. By capturing expert insights through interviews and workshops, organizations can create comprehensive training programs that not only document procedures but also capture the nuances and best practices of experienced employees.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Project management**
> In project management, Knowledge Management ensures that lessons learned from past projects are systematically captured and shared. This helps in avoiding common pitfalls and accelerating the learning curve for new projects, leading to more efficient and effective project outcomes.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Explicit vs Tacit Knowledge**
> Knowledge Management distinguishes between explicit (codified) and tacit (personal or contextual) knowledge. While explicit knowledge can be easily documented and shared, tacit knowledge is often difficult to articulate and requires more informal methods of sharing, such as mentoring and apprenticeship.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller originated the cognitive load theory in 1988, which has significant implications for Knowledge Management by highlighting the importance of balancing explicit and tacit knowledge management strategies.

## Synthesis

Knowledge Management is crucial for organizational success because it enhances the flow of both explicit and tacit knowledge, leading to improved performance. By integrating people, processes, and technology, organizations can create a culture that values and leverages all forms of knowledge. This aligns with broader information science principles by emphasizing the importance of context and social interaction in managing knowledge.

Knowledge Management also integrates well with other concepts like communities of practice and transactive memory systems, which are essential for capturing and sharing tacit knowledge. By understanding these distinctions and applying Knowledge Management effectively, organizations can achieve sustainable competitive advantages.

## Evidence

Empirical evidence from studies shows that combining document management tools with social practices such as mentoring and workshops leads to better performance outcomes compared to relying solely on technology. This underscores the importance of a holistic approach in Knowledge Management.
