# Critical Reasoning

> [!definition] **Critical Reasoning**
> Critical Reasoning involves the deliberate evaluation of arguments and evidence to assess the strength of conclusions and identify underlying assumptions, making it a core component of [[Critical Thinking]]. It operates by breaking down argument structures into premises, intermediate conclusions, and final conclusions, assessing each premise for plausibility, evaluating inferential steps, and identifying unstated assumptions before reaching a judgment on the conclusion's validity.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This includes making argument structure explicit, assessing each premise for plausibility, evaluating inferential steps, and identifying unstated assumptions. It does not include mere disagreement or skepticism without considering evidence.

## Core Explanation

At its core, critical reasoning is about dissecting an argument to understand its components. This involves recognizing the premises that support a claim, identifying any intermediate conclusions that link these premises to the final conclusion, and evaluating each step in this logical progression for coherence and truthfulness. By making these elements explicit, one can more effectively assess the overall strength of the argument.

In practice, critical reasoning is applied by systematically questioning every aspect of an argument. This includes checking whether the premises are true or plausible, ensuring that there are no gaps in the inferential steps between premises and conclusions, and identifying any hidden assumptions that might be influencing the argument's validity. The goal is to arrive at a well-supported conclusion rather than accepting claims based on surface-level impressions.

Theoretical roots of critical reasoning can be traced back to philosophical traditions such as Socratic questioning and logical analysis. These methods emphasize the importance of clarity, consistency, and evidence in evaluating arguments. Empirical studies have shown that training in critical reasoning enhances one's ability to make sound judgments and solve complex problems by fostering a disciplined approach to argument evaluation.

Historically, John Sweller’s work on cognitive load theory has provided valuable insights into how critical reasoning can be optimized for learning. His research highlights the importance of managing both intrinsic (inherent) and extraneous (unnecessary) cognitive loads during the process of evaluating arguments.

## Mechanism

The mechanism of critical reasoning involves a step-by-step breakdown of an argument into its constituent parts: premises, intermediate conclusions, and final conclusion. Each part is then evaluated for plausibility and logical consistency. This process ensures that no aspect of the argument is overlooked or taken at face value.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, critical reasoning can be applied to create learning materials that encourage students to engage deeply with arguments. By providing tools and techniques for breaking down complex arguments into manageable parts, educators can help learners develop the skills needed to evaluate evidence and identify assumptions.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Legal decision-making**
> In legal contexts, critical reasoning is essential for judges and lawyers to assess the strength of evidence presented in court. By carefully evaluating each piece of evidence and identifying any underlying biases or assumptions, they can make more informed decisions that are based on sound logical reasoning rather than emotional reactions.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Business problem-solving**
> In business settings, critical reasoning helps teams analyze data and arguments to solve complex problems. By breaking down the argument into its components and evaluating each part, businesses can make more informed decisions that are grounded in evidence and logical analysis rather than intuition alone.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Critical reasoning involves managing intrinsic cognitive load (the inherent difficulty of the task) and extraneous cognitive load (unnecessary distractions). In contrast, mere disagreement or skepticism often relies on extraneous factors such as emotional reactions rather than a structured analysis of evidence.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is recognized for his work in cognitive load theory, which has significantly influenced the development of critical reasoning techniques by highlighting the importance of managing both intrinsic and extraneous cognitive loads during argument evaluation.

## Synthesis

Critical Reasoning is crucial for effective thinking and decision-making because it provides a structured approach to evaluating arguments, which enhances our ability to make informed judgments. By integrating critical reasoning into various domains such as education, law, and business, we can foster a culture of evidence-based analysis that leads to better outcomes.

As a subset of informal logic, critical reasoning complements formal logical methods by focusing on the practical application of logical principles in everyday situations. This makes it an indispensable tool for navigating complex problems and making sound decisions.
