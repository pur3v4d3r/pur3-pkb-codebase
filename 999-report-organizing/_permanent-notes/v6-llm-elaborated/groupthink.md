# Groupthink

> [!definition] **Groupthink**
> Groupthink is a phenomenon where the desire for group cohesion leads to flawed decision-making by suppressing dissent and promoting premature consensus, as theorized by Irving Janis. It falls under [[Social Psychology]], focusing on how group dynamics can impair rational decision-making processes.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes individual decision-making processes and focuses on group dynamics in decision-making scenarios. It should not be confused with simple majority rule or democratic processes.

## Core Explanation

At its core, groupthink occurs when a cohesive in-group prioritizes harmony over critical evaluation of alternatives, leading to poor decisions. This happens because the social pressure to conform and avoid conflict creates an environment where dissent is discouraged, and members may self-censor their doubts or criticisms.

In practice, this dynamic can be observed in various settings such as corporate boardrooms, military command centers, and political cabinets. For instance, during the Bay of Pigs invasion planning, President Kennedy's advisors succumbed to groupthink, leading to a disastrous decision that could have been avoided with more diverse viewpoints and critical analysis.

Theoretical roots of groupthink lie in social psychology, particularly in the work of Irving Janis, who identified several antecedents that can lead to this phenomenon. These include an insulated leader, directive leadership style, and time pressure, which collectively create a high-risk environment for flawed decision-making.

Empirical evidence supports the existence of groupthink through studies showing how cohesive groups often make worse decisions than less cohesive ones. For example, in a study by Janis and Mann (1977), they found that groups with strong cohesion were more likely to engage in premature consensus and suppress dissenting opinions.

## Mechanism

The mechanism of groupthink operates through several stages: initial cohesiveness, suppression of dissent, illusion of unanimity, self-censorship, and finally, rationalization. Members of the group may initially feel a strong sense of unity and shared purpose, but as they face challenges or threats, this cohesion can become a barrier to critical thinking.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, groupthink can lead to the creation of overly simplistic or biased curricula if team members avoid challenging each other's ideas. This could result in a lack of diverse perspectives and potentially ineffective learning materials.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Military operations**
> During military planning, groupthink can cause commanders to overlook critical risks and alternative strategies due to the desire for consensus. This was evident during the Iraq War, where the U.S. military's decision-making process may have been influenced by a lack of dissenting voices.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Corporate strategy**
> In corporate settings, groupthink can stifle innovation and strategic planning if team members fear conflict or retribution for expressing differing opinions. This could lead to missed opportunities and suboptimal business decisions.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Groupthink vs Confirmation Bias**
> While both involve flawed decision-making, groupthink focuses on the suppression of dissent within a cohesive group, whereas confirmation bias is about selectively processing information that supports one's preexisting beliefs. Understanding these differences helps in addressing specific cognitive biases more effectively.

## Key Figures

- **Irving Janis** — Janis is credited with the foundational theory of groupthink, identifying its antecedents and symptoms through extensive research and case studies, particularly during his work at Yale University in the 1970s.

## Synthesis

Understanding groupthink is crucial for improving decision-making processes across various domains. By recognizing how cohesion can lead to flawed consensus, organizations can implement strategies to encourage open communication and critical thinking. This concept intersects with other social psychology phenomena like confirmation bias and polarization, highlighting the broader implications of cognitive biases in group settings.

The study of groupthink also has significant implications for fields such as education, where fostering a culture of open dialogue is essential for effective instructional design. In military and political contexts, recognizing the signs of groupthink can prevent disastrous decisions that could have been avoided with more diverse perspectives.
