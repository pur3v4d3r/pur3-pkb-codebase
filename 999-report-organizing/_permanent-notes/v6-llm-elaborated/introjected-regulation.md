# Introjected Regulation

> [!definition] **Introjected Regulation**
> Introjected regulation is a form of controlled motivation where the individual internalizes external contingencies but lacks conscious endorsement, regulating behavior to avoid negative or pursue positive internal experiences (Deci & Ryan, 2018). It falls under [[controlled-motivation]], characterized by pressure and poorer well-being outcomes compared to autonomous motivation.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It stops at behaviors regulated by internalized contingencies without value-endorsement and does not include fully integrated or autonomous motivations.

## Core Explanation

Introjected regulation involves the individual adopting external contingencies as their own, often driven by a desire to avoid negative internal experiences such as shame or anxiety. This form of control is distinct from identified regulation because it lacks conscious value endorsement and does not fully integrate with the self's value structure (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

In practice, introjected regulation manifests in behaviors that are regulated by conditional regard—where an individual’s worth or acceptance depends on meeting certain external standards. For example, a student might study diligently to avoid feeling guilty about disappointing their parents rather than because they value learning for its own sake (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

Theoretical roots of introjected regulation can be traced back to Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which posits that individuals are motivated by three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these needs are thwarted, the individual may adopt introjected regulation as a coping mechanism, leading to poorer well-being outcomes such as decreased intrinsic motivation and increased anxiety (Ryan & Deci, 2017).

Empirical research supports the notion that introjected regulation is associated with lower levels of psychological well-being. For instance, studies have shown that students who regulate their behavior based on conditional regard are more likely to experience stress and burnout compared to those whose motivation is autonomous (Martela & Deci, 2016).

## Mechanism

The process of introjection begins with conditional regard, where the regard of significant others becomes contingent upon specific behaviors. This mechanism is empirically associated with the development of introjected regulation and resistance to subsequent movement up the internalization continuum (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In digital and AI-mediated learning environments, understanding introjected regulation can inform instructional strategies that promote autonomous motivation. For example, designing platforms that provide positive feedback based on effort rather than performance alone can help students develop a more intrinsic sense of value for their learning (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

> [!example] **Application 2 — Controlling teaching styles**
> Teachers who use controlling teaching styles, such as those that rely heavily on external rewards and punishments, may inadvertently foster introjected regulation. By shifting towards more autonomy-supportive practices, teachers can help students develop a stronger sense of self-determination (Martela & Deci, 2016).

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Introjected vs Identified Regulation**
> Introjected regulation differs from identified regulation in that the former lacks conscious value endorsement. While both are forms of controlled motivation, introjected regulation is driven by internalized contingencies without a sense of personal importance or value (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

> [!key-distinction] **Introjected vs External Regulation**
> External regulation involves acting to avoid negative consequences from others rather than internal experiences. Introjected regulation, on the other hand, is driven by internalized contingencies and associated with feelings of shame or guilt (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

## Key Figures

- **Edward L. Deci** — Contributor to Self-Determination Theory, Edward L. Deci has significantly advanced the understanding of introjected regulation through his research on internalization processes and their impact on well-being.
- **Frank Martela** — Researcher in Controlled Motivation, Frank Martela has explored the dynamics of introjected regulation and its role in psychological well-being, contributing to the empirical evidence base (Martela & Deci, 2016).

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can educators and parents promote introjected regulation without fostering negative outcomes?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Further research on effective strategies for promoting autonomous motivation while mitigating the risks of introjected regulation could provide insights into this question.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term effects of introjected regulation on psychological well-being?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies tracking individuals over time and examining the persistence of introjected regulation would help clarify its long-term impacts (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

## Synthesis

Understanding introjected regulation is crucial for advancing Self-Determination Theory and informing interventions aimed at promoting psychological well-being. By recognizing the mechanisms that drive introjected regulation and the conditions under which it can be mitigated, educators and researchers can work towards fostering more autonomous forms of motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2018).

The concept of introjected regulation also has implications for digital and AI-mediated learning environments, where personalized feedback and adaptive systems can either promote or hinder the development of autonomous motivation. By integrating insights from this theory into design practices, we can create more supportive and empowering educational experiences (Martela & Deci, 2016).
