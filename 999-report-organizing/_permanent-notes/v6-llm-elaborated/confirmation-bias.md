# Confirmation Bias

> [!definition] **Confirmation Bias**
> Confirmation Bias is the systematic tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information in ways that confirm pre-existing beliefs while under-weighting, dismissing, or failing to recall disconfirming information — a robust pattern observed across hypothesis testing, belief evaluation, and information consumption. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], as it pertains to how our cognitive systems process and integrate information.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept does not include other cognitive biases like disconfirmation bias but focuses on the specific mechanism of seeking confirmation over disconfirmation.

## Core Explanation

Confirmation Bias operates by selectively seeking out information that aligns with one's preconceived notions while ignoring or downplaying contradictory evidence. This bias is deeply rooted in human cognition, manifesting in various contexts such as politics, science, and everyday decision-making. For instance, during a political debate, supporters of a candidate may only pay attention to positive news about their preferred candidate and dismiss negative information.

The core mechanism behind Confirmation Bias involves selective exposure, where individuals actively seek out information that supports their existing beliefs while avoiding contradictory evidence. This can be seen in the way people choose which news sources to follow or which social media content to engage with. Additionally, biased interpretation of mixed evidence plays a significant role; even when presented with both supportive and conflicting data, individuals tend to interpret the supporting evidence more favorably.

Theoretical roots of Confirmation Bias trace back to cognitive psychology, where it is considered one of the most thoroughly replicated cognitive biases in the literature. This robust pattern across various domains underscores its structural nature rather than being an incidental feature of human cognition. The bias operates through multiple mechanisms, including asymmetric scrutiny of supportive versus contrary information and biased memory retrieval, making it a pervasive influence on our decision-making processes.

Empirical evidence supporting Confirmation Bias comes from numerous studies demonstrating that people are more likely to remember and recall information that aligns with their pre-existing beliefs. For example, in a study by Nickerson (1998), participants were shown ambiguous statements and asked to rate them as true or false; those who initially believed the statement rated it as truer than those who did not believe it.

## Mechanism

Confirmation Bias operates through several concrete mechanisms. Selective exposure involves individuals actively seeking out information that aligns with their beliefs, while avoiding contradictory evidence. Biased interpretation of mixed evidence further exacerbates this bias by leading people to interpret ambiguous or conflicting data in a way that supports their existing views.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Confirmation Bias can lead educators to create materials that reinforce students' preconceived notions rather than challenging them. For example, if a student believes a certain historical narrative, an instructor might inadvertently present only evidence supporting this view, thereby reinforcing the bias and limiting critical thinking.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Science**
> In scientific research, Confirmation Bias can lead to biased data collection or interpretation, where researchers may unconsciously favor results that support their hypotheses. This can result in flawed studies and a distorted understanding of reality. For instance, a study might be designed with specific criteria that only allow for positive outcomes, leading to an overestimation of the effect being studied.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Politics**
> In politics, Confirmation Bias contributes to the polarization of opinions by reinforcing existing beliefs through selective exposure to news and information sources. This can lead to a fragmented society where individuals are less likely to engage in constructive dialogue with those who hold opposing views, exacerbating social divisions.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Confirmation vs Disconfirmation Bias**
> While both biases involve selective processing of information, Confirmation Bias focuses on seeking out and favoring supportive evidence, whereas Disconfirmation Bias involves actively avoiding or dismissing contradictory evidence. The key difference lies in the directionality of the bias: confirmation is about seeking support, while disconfirmation is about avoiding contradiction.

> [!key-distinction] **Confirmation vs Motivated Reasoning**
> Both Confirmation Bias and Motivated Reasoning involve biased reasoning, but they differ in their focus. Confirmation Bias specifically targets the selective processing of information to confirm existing beliefs, whereas Motivated Reasoning can be driven by a wide range of emotional or motivational factors beyond just seeking confirmation.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is credited with coining the term 'Confirmation Bias' in his seminal work on cognitive psychology. His contributions have been instrumental in understanding how our cognitive systems process and integrate information, highlighting the robust nature of this bias across various domains.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can we mitigate the effects of Confirmation Bias in decision-making?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Mitigating Confirmation Bias would require developing effective strategies for individuals to critically evaluate their own reasoning and seek out diverse perspectives. Experimental studies that test these interventions could provide insights into what works.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term consequences of confirmation bias on societal beliefs and actions?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal studies tracking changes in individual beliefs over time, as well as their impact on social behaviors, would help elucidate the broader implications of this cognitive bias.

## Synthesis

Understanding Confirmation Bias is crucial for grasping how our cognitive systems process and integrate information. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], highlighting its role in shaping decision-making across various domains such as politics, science, and everyday life. By recognizing the mechanisms behind this bias, we can develop strategies to mitigate its effects, fostering more informed and balanced reasoning.

The broader impact of Confirmation Bias extends beyond individual cognition into societal dynamics. It contributes to polarization and fragmented social discourse, making it essential for cognitive psychology to address these issues. Integrating insights from related concepts like Working Memory and Motivated Reasoning can provide a comprehensive framework for understanding and addressing the implications of this bias.
