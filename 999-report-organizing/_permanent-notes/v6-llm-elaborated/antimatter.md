# Antimatter

> [!definition] **Antimatter**
> Antimatter is a form of matter composed of antiparticles—particles that share the same mass and spin as their normal-matter counterparts but possess opposite electric charge, additive quantum numbers, and particle number for composite systems. This concept falls under Particle Physics and excludes specific applications or technologies utilizing antimatter, such as propulsion systems or energy sources.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes specific applications or technologies that utilize antimatter, such as propulsion systems or energy sources. It also does not cover speculative theories about antimatter's behavior in extreme conditions beyond current experimental evidence.

## Core Explanation

Antimatter particles are the antiparticles of their normal-matter counterparts, differing primarily in electric charge and additive quantum numbers. For instance, an electron has a negative charge, while its antiparticle, the positron, carries a positive charge. This fundamental difference leads to unique interactions when matter and antimatter meet.

The theoretical underpinnings of antimatter were first proposed by Paul Dirac in 1928 as part of his relativistic quantum theory, which predicted the existence of antiparticles. Subsequently, Carl Anderson discovered positrons—the first observed antiparticle—in cosmic rays in 1932.

Antimatter and matter annihilate upon contact, converting their mass entirely into energy according to Einstein's famous equation E=mc². This process is governed by the conservation laws of quantum field theory, ensuring that both energy and momentum are conserved during annihilation.

## Mechanism

When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate each other in a burst of radiation, typically gamma rays or photons. This process is highly efficient, converting the entire rest mass of the particles into pure energy. The conservation laws ensure that this annihilation respects both energy and momentum balance.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Synthesizing Antihydrogen**
> Current research efforts focus on synthesizing, trapping, and studying antihydrogen atoms to understand antimatter's behavior under gravity. These experiments aim to confirm the equivalence principle for antimatter, ensuring that it falls in the same manner as ordinary matter.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Electric Charge of Particles vs Antiparticles**
> Particles and their antiparticle counterparts differ primarily in electric charge. For example, an electron carries a negative charge while its positron counterpart has a positive charge. This distinction is crucial for understanding the interactions between matter and antimatter.

## Key Figures

- **Paul Dirac** — Predicted the existence of antiparticles in his relativistic quantum theory, laying the theoretical groundwork for antimatter research.
- **Carl Anderson** — Discovered positrons as the first observed antiparticle in cosmic rays, confirming Dirac's theoretical predictions and opening up new avenues in particle physics.

## Synthesis

Understanding antimatter is crucial not only for advancing fundamental physics but also for potential technological applications. The study of antihydrogen atoms and their behavior under gravity provides insights into the nature of matter and could lead to breakthroughs in energy production and propulsion systems.

## Evidence

Experiments such as AEgIS, ALPHA-g, and GBAR have provided direct evidence that antihydrogen falls under gravity in the same direction as ordinary hydrogen. This finding refutes speculative theories about antimatter's gravitational behavior and confirms CPT invariance.
