# Competence Need

> [!definition] **Competence Need**
> The Competence Need is the psychological requirement to feel effective in one's interactions with the environment — to experience oneself as capable of producing intended outcomes — and it falls under [[self-determination-theory]]. It is satisfied by optimally challenging activities that yield clear progress and feedback.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes other basic psychological needs like autonomy and relatedness but focuses on the feeling of efficacy and capability.

## Core Explanation

The Competence Need is fundamentally about feeling competent, which means experiencing a sense of efficacy in one's actions. This need is met when individuals engage with tasks that are neither too easy nor too difficult but offer an optimal level of challenge. When the task is appropriately challenging and provides clear feedback on progress, it fosters a strong sense of competence.

In practice, this translates to environments where learners can see their efforts leading to tangible results. For instance, in educational settings, well-structured assignments with incremental difficulty levels allow students to track their improvement over time, thereby satisfying the Competence Need. Similarly, video games often provide clear feedback and progression indicators, which help players feel competent as they advance through levels.

Theoretical roots of the Competence Need lie within Self-Determination Theory (SDT), a framework that posits that certain psychological needs — including competence — are fundamental to human motivation. SDT suggests that when these needs are met, individuals experience greater well-being and engagement in their activities. The Competence Need is particularly important because it directly influences an individual's belief in their ability to succeed.

Empirical research supports the idea that satisfying the Competence Need enhances motivation and performance. For example, studies have shown that students who feel competent are more likely to persist with challenging tasks and achieve better academic outcomes.

## Mechanism

The Competence Need is supported by what SDT calls *structure* — clear expectations, scaffolded difficulty progression, and informative feedback. Excessive ease or excessive difficulty without scaffolding undermines this need. Clear expectations provide a roadmap for learners, while scaffolded difficulty ensures that challenges are appropriately matched to the learner's current capabilities. Informative feedback helps learners understand their progress and adjust their strategies accordingly.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, creating a curriculum with progressively challenging tasks that provide clear feedback is crucial for satisfying the Competence Need. For example, a math teacher might start with simple problems and gradually increase complexity while offering detailed solutions to each step of the problem-solving process.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Game development**
> In game design, developers can ensure that players feel competent by providing clear objectives, incremental difficulty levels, and real-time feedback on performance. This approach not only enhances player engagement but also keeps them motivated to continue playing.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Workplace training**
> For workplace training programs, offering tasks that are challenging yet achievable with proper guidance can help employees feel competent in their roles. Regular assessments and constructive feedback on performance are essential for sustaining this feeling of competence over time.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Competence Need vs Autonomy-Need**
> While both the Competence Need and Autonomy-Need are fundamental in SDT, they focus on different aspects. The Competence Need is about feeling effective and capable of achieving outcomes, whereas the Autonomy-Need pertains to the desire for freedom and control over one's actions. Understanding these distinctions helps tailor interventions that address specific motivational needs.

## Key Figures

- **Edward Deci** — Co-originator of Self-Determination Theory, Edward Deci has significantly contributed to the understanding and application of the Competence Need in various contexts.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can educators balance challenge and success to sustainably satisfy the Competence Need?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Empirical studies that explore different methods for balancing challenge and success could provide insights into sustainable strategies for satisfying the Competence Need.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> What are the long-term effects of consistently satisfying or failing to satisfy the Competence Need?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Longitudinal research tracking individuals' motivation, well-being, and performance over extended periods could help clarify these effects.

## Synthesis

Understanding and supporting the Competence Need is crucial for effective motivation strategies because it directly impacts an individual's belief in their ability to succeed. By fostering environments that provide clear expectations, scaffolded difficulty progression, and informative feedback, educators and designers can help learners feel competent and motivated. This, in turn, enhances engagement, persistence, and overall performance across various domains such as education, work, and gaming.

The Competence Need is deeply intertwined with other psychological needs like autonomy and relatedness within SDT. By addressing the Competence Need effectively, we can create more holistic and supportive environments that promote well-being and motivation.
