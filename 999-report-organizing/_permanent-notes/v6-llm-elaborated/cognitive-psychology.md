# Cognitive Psychology

> [!definition] **Cognitive Psychology**
> Cognitive Psychology is the branch of psychology that investigates the internal mental processes such as perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making through experimental and computational methods, treating the mind as an information-processing system whose mechanisms can be inferred from behavior and brain measurement. It falls under [[Cognitive Science]], establishing a framework for understanding how these processes work and their implications in various fields.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes purely behavioral approaches like strict behaviorism and focuses on internal mental representations and processes.

## Core Explanation

Cognitive Psychology emerged from the 1950s 'cognitive revolution' as a rejection of strict behaviorism, which focused solely on observable behaviors. Instead, it posits that internal mental representations and processes are legitimate scientific objects, providing a conceptual scaffold for contemporary learning science, human-computer interaction (HCI), and cognitive neuroscience.

The field operates by treating the mind as an information-processing system, where inputs from the environment are transformed into outputs through various stages of processing. This approach allows researchers to model mental operations using computational tools, enabling precise predictions about behavior based on internal processes.

Theoretical roots of Cognitive Psychology can be traced back to early work in cognitive science and psychology, particularly the writings of George Miller, who highlighted the limitations of human information-processing capacity with his famous 'magical number seven.' This foundational research laid the groundwork for more sophisticated models of mental operations.

Empirical grounding comes from a variety of experimental methods, including reaction time studies, memory tasks, and brain imaging techniques. These methods allow researchers to test hypotheses about cognitive processes in controlled settings, providing insights into how these processes unfold over time.

## Mechanism

Cognitive Psychology models mental operations as information-processing systems with distinct stages: input, encoding, storage, retrieval, and output. Each stage involves specific mechanisms such as attentional filters, working memory buffers, and long-term memory structures, which interact to produce behavior.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, Cognitive Psychology informs the creation of effective learning materials by considering how information is processed. For instance, using spaced repetition techniques can enhance long-term memory retention, while minimizing cognitive load through chunking and simplification improves comprehension.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Human-computer interaction**
> In HCI, Cognitive Psychology guides the design of user interfaces that align with how users process information. For example, intuitive navigation systems reduce extraneous cognitive load, allowing users to focus on task goals rather than interface complexities.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> Intrinsic load refers to the inherent difficulty of a task, while extraneous load is introduced by the instructional design. Understanding this distinction helps educators and designers create tasks that are challenging but not overly burdensome, ensuring optimal learning outcomes.

## Key Figures

- **Ulric Neisser** — Ulric Neisser coined the term 'cognitive psychology' in 1967 and is credited with formalizing the field as a distinct branch of psychology, emphasizing internal mental processes over behavior alone.
- **John Sweller** — John Sweller introduced the concept of cognitive load theory, which distinguishes between intrinsic, extraneous, and germane loads to explain how instructional design can affect learning efficiency. His work has had a profound impact on educational practices.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Why do pedagogical claims from Cognitive Psychology often fail to scale?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Replicating cognitive psychology findings in real-world classroom settings with appropriate controls and longitudinal studies could provide evidence for or against the scalability of these claims.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> How can we better integrate cognitive models with real-world educational practices?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Developing more nuanced cognitive models that account for individual differences in learning styles and incorporating technology to personalize instruction could enhance the integration between theory and practice.

## Synthesis

Cognitive Psychology matters because it provides a robust framework for understanding mental processes, which has far-reaching implications across various domains. By informing instructional design, human-computer interaction, and cognitive neuroscience, this field enhances our ability to create effective learning environments and improve user experiences.

Moreover, its focus on internal mental representations challenges traditional behaviorist views, fostering a more comprehensive approach to psychological inquiry that integrates biological, computational, and social factors.
