# Event Horizon Telescope

> [!definition] **Event Horizon Telescope**
> The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) is a global array of radio telescopes that uses interferometry to create images of black holes with unprecedented resolution by synthesizing an Earth-sized aperture. It focuses specifically on imaging the event-horizon-scale shadow of nearby supermassive black holes, excluding other astronomical imaging techniques or unrelated projects in astronomy. This project falls under Radio Astronomy.

> [!attention] **Boundary**

## Core Explanation

The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) is a groundbreaking collaboration that harnesses the power of radio telescopes scattered across the globe to form an Earth-sized virtual telescope. By employing interferometry, EHT can achieve angular resolutions sufficient to image the event-horizon-scale shadow of supermassive black holes, such as M87* and Sgr A*. This technique relies on combining signals from multiple telescopes to simulate a much larger aperture, thereby enhancing resolution far beyond what any single telescope could achieve.

The EHT project is rooted in theoretical predictions about the appearance of black hole shadows based on general relativity. The first image released by EHT in 2019 depicted M87*, revealing a ring-like structure consistent with the shadow cast by a Kerr-like black hole, as predicted by Einstein's theory. This achievement not only confirmed the existence and properties of supermassive black holes but also provided a direct test of general relativity at extreme scales.

The process of imaging black holes involves collecting data from multiple telescopes over several days and then using sophisticated algorithms to reconstruct images from this sparse data set. The reconstruction is non-trivial due to the limited coverage in the uv-plane, necessitating significant prior assumptions about the source structure. Despite these challenges, EHT's findings have been robust enough to withstand independent reanalyses, though slight variations in ring sizes highlight ongoing refinements in image processing techniques.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Understanding Black Hole Physics**
> The EHT's images of M87* and Sgr A* provide critical insights into the physics governing black holes. By observing the ring-like structure around these supermassive objects, scientists can test predictions from general relativity under extreme conditions. This not only confirms theoretical models but also opens avenues for exploring phenomena such as accretion disks and jets emanating from black holes.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Testing General Relativity**
> The EHT's findings offer a unique opportunity to test the predictions of general relativity in extreme gravitational fields. The observed ring sizes are consistent with theoretical expectations, providing quantitative confirmation that these laws hold even under such intense conditions. This validation is crucial for advancing our understanding of gravity and its role in the universe.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **EHT vs Other Radio Telescopes**
> Unlike traditional radio telescopes, which capture signals from a single dish or array, EHT uses interferometry to synthesize an Earth-sized aperture. This approach allows for unprecedented resolution in imaging distant celestial objects like black holes. While other radio telescopes can provide detailed images of nearby galaxies and nebulae, they lack the resolving power necessary to observe the event horizon scale.
