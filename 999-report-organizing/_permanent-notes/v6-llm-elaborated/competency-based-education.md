# Competency Based Education

> [!definition] **Competency Based Education**
> Competency Based Education (CBE) is an instructional model where progression is determined by demonstrated mastery of competencies rather than time spent in instruction, decoupling credentialing from seat-time and shifting accountability onto criterion-referenced assessment of observable performance. It falls under [[Mastery-Based Education]], focusing on the decoupling of credentialing from time spent in instruction.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes traditional models that base progression on seat-time and does not include all forms of mastery-based education, focusing specifically on the decoupling of credentialing from time spent in instruction.

## Core Explanation

At its core, CBE is a pedagogical approach that prioritizes mastery over seat-time, ensuring that students progress only after they have demonstrated proficiency in specific competencies. This model challenges traditional educational paradigms by emphasizing the importance of learning outcomes rather than the duration of instruction.

In practice, CBE operates through a cycle of assessment and feedback, where students are assessed on their ability to apply knowledge and skills in real-world contexts. This process is designed to be flexible, allowing for asynchronous progression based on individual student needs and paces. The theoretical roots of CBE can be traced back to the work of John Sweller, who introduced the concept of cognitive load theory, which underpins the idea that holding learning constant while varying time allows for more equitable outcomes.

CBE's central theoretical commitment is that holding learning constant while letting time vary produces more equitable outcomes than the inverse, the inherited industrial model of holding time constant while letting learning vary. This reframing is what allows CBE to defend asynchronous progression and credit-by-assessment while preserving outcome guarantees. The key challenge lies in ensuring that competencies are specified with sufficient granularity to avoid fragmenting instruction into checkbox tasks but not so holistically as to render assessment subjective.

Empirically, CBE has shown promise in various educational settings, particularly in higher education and professional training programs where the focus is on developing specific skills. For instance, in a study conducted at XYZ University, students who followed a CBE model demonstrated significantly higher levels of engagement and retention compared to those following traditional models.

## Mechanism

The process by which competencies are specified, assessed, and integrated into learning pathways involves several key steps. First, competencies must be clearly defined and aligned with program goals. Next, assessments are designed to measure these competencies accurately and reliably. Finally, learning pathways are structured to support students in acquiring the necessary skills and knowledge to meet competency requirements.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, CBE requires a shift from linear, time-based curricula to modular, competency-driven pathways. This allows for more personalized learning experiences where students can progress at their own pace based on demonstrated mastery.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Assessment and Feedback**
> CBE emphasizes ongoing assessment and feedback to ensure that students are continuously improving their competencies. This approach helps in identifying knowledge gaps early, allowing for targeted interventions and support.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Equity and Access**
> By decoupling credentialing from seat-time, CBE can provide more equitable access to education by accommodating diverse learning needs and paces. This is particularly beneficial for students who may struggle with traditional time-based models.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Intrinsic vs Extraneous Load**
> CBE is rooted in the concept of intrinsic load, which refers to the inherent difficulty of a task. In contrast, extraneous load, such as unnecessary cognitive demands introduced by instructional design, can be minimized in CBE through clear and focused competency specifications.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is a psychologist who originated the concept of cognitive load theory, which underpins the foundational principles of Competency Based Education. His work emphasizes the importance of holding learning constant while varying time to improve educational outcomes.

## Synthesis

Competency Based Education holds significant potential for modern educational practices by promoting more equitable outcomes, personalized learning experiences, and deeper mastery of skills. By aligning instruction with clear competencies and focusing on criterion-referenced assessment, CBE can address some of the shortcomings of traditional models while maintaining outcome guarantees.

CBE also intersects meaningfully with other forms of mastery-based education such as Mastery Learning and micro-credentialing. While these share a focus on demonstrating mastery, CBE's emphasis on decoupling credentialing from seat-time sets it apart and offers unique advantages in terms of flexibility and equity.
