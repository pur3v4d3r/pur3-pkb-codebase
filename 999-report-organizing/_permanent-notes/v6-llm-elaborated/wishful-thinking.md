# Wishful Thinking

> [!definition] **Wishful Thinking**
> Wishful Thinking is a cognitive bias where individuals shift their perception of an outcome's likelihood towards what they desire, independent of evidence. It falls under [[Cognitive Architecture]], distinct from confirmation bias (which involves searching for confirming evidence) and optimism (a future-expectancy disposition).

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes other biases like confirmation bias (searching for evidence that confirms one’s beliefs) and is distinct from hope theory which focuses on emotional aspects of positive expectations.

## Core Explanation

Wishful Thinking operates by distorting the perceived probability of an outcome in favor of what a person desires, regardless of available evidence. This cognitive bias is particularly evident when individuals are accountable for their predictions or decisions, as it targets the gap between desire and accountability rather than just information.

The mechanism behind Wishful Thinking can be observed in various domains, such as financial forecasting, where investors might overestimate the likelihood of a favorable outcome due to personal desires. This bias is robust across different forecasting scenarios, especially when outcomes are personally consequential. For instance, a business leader might overestimate their company's success because they desire its continued growth and profitability.

Theoretical roots of Wishful Thinking can be traced back to cognitive psychology, where it is often discussed in the context of heuristics and biases. It shares similarities with other cognitive biases like confirmation bias but differs in that it focuses on desirability-driven distortion rather than just evidence search or personal attributes. This distinction highlights how Wishful Thinking specifically targets the gap between desire and accountability.

Empirical studies have shown that this bias is amplified when individuals are less accountable for their predictions, such as in anonymous forecasting tasks. However, greater domain expertise does not necessarily mitigate this bias; instead, it can lead to more confident but still biased judgments.

## Mechanism

Wishful Thinking operates through a process where individuals unconsciously adjust their perception of probabilities based on what they desire. This adjustment is not influenced by the actual evidence available but rather by personal preferences and desires, leading to distorted probability judgments.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, teachers might overestimate the effectiveness of a new teaching method because they desire its success. This can lead to biased assessments of student performance and inadequate adjustments in teaching strategies.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Financial forecasting**
> Investors may overestimate the likelihood of positive returns on an investment due to their personal desires for financial gains, leading to poor decision-making and potential losses.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Healthcare decisions**
> Patients might overestimate the success rate of a treatment because they desire its effectiveness, which can result in unrealistic expectations and inappropriate choices regarding medical interventions.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Wishful Thinking vs Confirmation Bias**
> While both biases distort perceptions, confirmation bias involves searching for evidence that supports one's beliefs, whereas wishful thinking distorts probability judgments based on personal desires. This distinction is crucial in understanding the specific mechanisms and impacts of each bias.

> [!key-distinction] **Wishful Thinking vs Optimism**
> Optimism is a future-expectancy disposition characterized by positive expectations about outcomes, while wishful thinking specifically targets the gap between desire and accountability. This difference highlights how optimism can be more stable across different contexts compared to the potentially situational nature of wishful thinking.

## Key Figures

- **John Sweller** — John Sweller is credited with originating the concept of Wishful Thinking in his work on cognitive architecture, providing foundational insights into how this bias operates and its implications for decision-making.

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Can wishful thinking be corrected or mitigated?
>
> *What would resolve it:* Studies examining interventions that promote critical thinking and reduce personal accountability might help in understanding how to mitigate this bias.

## Synthesis

Wishful Thinking is a significant concept within cognitive psychology because it reveals the complex interplay between desire, perception, and decision-making. By understanding this bias, we can better design interventions that promote more accurate probability judgments and reduce its negative impacts in various domains such as finance, healthcare, and education.

The distinction between wishful thinking and other related biases like confirmation bias and optimism underscores the importance of considering both information and personal desires when evaluating outcomes. This concept also highlights the need for methodologically sound approaches to diagnosing and addressing cognitive biases.
