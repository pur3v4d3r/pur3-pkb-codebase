# Skepticism

> [!definition] **Skepticism**
> Skepticism is the philosophical position that knowledge of some target domain (like the external world or other minds) is impossible or unjustified, distinguished from ordinary doubt by its principled character. It falls under [[Epistemology]], functioning as a constraint in modern epistemology where any positive theory of knowledge must address skeptical scenarios and closure principles.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> Skepticism excludes positions like fallibilism, which affirm the possibility of knowledge despite uncertainty. It also does not equate to mere doubt but rather a more structured and philosophical stance against knowledge claims.

## Core Explanation

Skepticism challenges the possibility of certain knowledge claims, particularly those about the external world or other minds. This position is not merely a form of doubt but a structured philosophical stance that questions whether we can ever truly know anything outside our immediate sensory experiences. Skepticism operates by invoking skeptical scenarios such as the brain in a vat and the evil demon to highlight potential paradoxes for theories of knowledge.

In practice, skepticism serves as a critical test for epistemological theories. For instance, if a theory cannot explain away these skeptical scenarios without resorting to ad hoc modifications or circular reasoning, it may be considered inadequate. This is why contemporary epistemologists often engage with skeptical challenges to refine their theories and ensure they are robust against such paradoxes.

Theoretical roots of skepticism can be traced back to ancient philosophers like Pyrrho and Sextus Empiricus, who argued that certainty in knowledge claims was unattainable due to the inherent limitations of human perception. More recently, modern epistemologists have developed sophisticated frameworks to address skeptical challenges, such as coherentism or reliabilism, which attempt to provide alternative accounts of knowledge that can withstand skeptical scrutiny.

Empirically, skepticism has had a profound impact on contemporary epistemology by driving the development of new theories and methodologies. For example, the Gettier problem highlights cases where beliefs are both justified and true but still fail to meet the standards of knowledge, which skepticism addresses more broadly. This has led to ongoing debates about what constitutes genuine knowledge in various domains.

## Mechanism

Skepticism operates through a closure principle that asserts if we know P and P implies Q, then we should also know Q. However, skeptical scenarios like the brain in a vat or the evil demon challenge this by suggesting that even if we believe we have knowledge of some domain (P), it might be false due to external manipulation or deception. These scenarios generate paradoxes for theories of knowledge, forcing them to either accept the possibility of such scenarios or find ways to explain away these skeptical challenges.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, skepticism highlights the need for robust validation methods. For instance, if a theory of learning assumes that students can reliably transfer knowledge from one context to another (P), skeptical scenarios like the brain in a vat challenge this assumption by suggesting that such transfers might be unreliable due to external manipulation or deception. This implies that instructional designers must develop rigorous assessment tools and pedagogical strategies to ensure that learners truly understand and can apply their knowledge.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Scientific research**
> In scientific research, skepticism ensures that theories are tested rigorously against empirical evidence. For example, if a theory of causality assumes that certain variables (P) cause specific outcomes (Q), skeptical scenarios like the evil demon challenge this by suggesting that external factors might be influencing the outcome in ways not accounted for by the theory. This implies that scientists must design experiments and control for potential confounding variables to ensure their theories are robust.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Global vs Local Skepticism**
> Global skepticism asserts that knowledge of any domain is impossible, while local skepticism claims that specific classes of knowledge claims fail to meet the standards required for genuine knowledge. The key difference lies in scope: global skepticism is an all-encompassing doubt about knowledge, whereas local skepticism focuses on particular domains or types of beliefs.

> [!key-distinction] **Skepticism vs Fallibilism**
> While both skepticism and fallibilism challenge the certainty of knowledge claims, they do so in fundamentally different ways. Skepticism denies that any knowledge is possible, whereas fallibilism affirms that knowledge is possible despite uncertainty. This distinction matters because it clarifies why most contemporary epistemologists are fallibilists rather than skeptics: fallibilism allows for a more nuanced understanding of knowledge without rejecting its possibility entirely.

## Synthesis

Skepticism matters because it serves as a critical constraint on positive theories of knowledge, driving their development and refinement. By challenging the possibility of certain knowledge claims, skepticism forces epistemologists to address skeptical scenarios and closure principles, leading to more robust and nuanced theories. Its impact extends beyond philosophy into fields like instructional design and scientific research, where addressing skeptical challenges is essential for validating knowledge claims.

Skepticism also connects to broader debates in epistemology, such as the Gettier problem and fallibilism, highlighting its importance in shaping contemporary philosophical discourse. Understanding skepticism helps us appreciate the complexities of knowledge and the ongoing efforts to develop theories that can withstand skeptical scrutiny.
