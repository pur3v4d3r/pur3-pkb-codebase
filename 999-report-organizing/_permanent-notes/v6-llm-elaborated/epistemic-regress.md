# Epistemic Regress

> [!definition] **Epistemic Regress**
> The Epistemic Regress is a foundational challenge in epistemology that questions the justification of beliefs by requiring each justified belief to be supported by another justified belief, leading to an infinite regress unless halted. It excludes sceptical arguments about the success of responses and focuses on the structural challenge within justificatory chains, falling under Epistemological Theories.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> It excludes sceptical arguments about the success of responses to the regress and focuses on the structural challenge of justificatory chains in epistemology.

## Core Explanation

The core problem of Epistemic Regress is that it challenges the very foundation of justified beliefs by suggesting an endless chain of justification. This regress implies that for any belief to be considered knowledge, there must always be a more fundamental belief providing its justification, leading to an infinite loop unless one finds a way to terminate this cycle.

Foundationalism proposes stopping the regress at self-evident or infallible beliefs, coherentism suggests circular support among beliefs forming a web of interconnected justifications, and infinitism accepts the possibility of an endless chain of reasons. Each theory attempts to address the structural challenge posed by Epistemic Regress in distinct ways.

Theoretical roots of this problem trace back to ancient Greek philosophy where skepticism about knowledge was prevalent. Philosophers like Plato and Aristotle grappled with similar issues, though modern formulations emerged more clearly during the Enlightenment period when systematic approaches to epistemology became prominent.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Instructional design**
> In instructional design, understanding Epistemic Regress can help educators structure learning materials that support students in building a coherent knowledge base. By recognizing the need for foundational beliefs or interconnected justifications, instructors can create curricula that scaffold complex concepts on simpler ones, ensuring each step is justified and understood.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Legal argumentation**
> In legal contexts, Epistemic Regress highlights the importance of establishing a chain of evidence where each piece supports another. Lawyers must ensure that their arguments are well-founded, either by relying on self-evident facts or by creating a coherent web of supporting claims. Ignoring this can lead to weak cases and ineffective advocacy.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Foundationalism vs Coherentism**
> Foundationalism posits that knowledge is built upon self-evident or infallible beliefs, whereas coherentism argues for a web of interconnected justifications where each belief supports others in the network. This distinction matters because it shapes how one views the structure and reliability of knowledge.

## Open Questions

> [!open-question] **Question**
> Is there a viable solution to the Epistemic Regress that is not foundational, coherentist, or infinitist?
>
> *What would resolve it:* A new theory would need to demonstrate how it can terminate the regress without relying on self-evident beliefs (foundationalism), circular support (coherentism), or an endless chain of reasons (infinitism).

## Synthesis

Understanding Epistemic Regress is crucial for epistemological inquiry as it forces a reevaluation of the nature and structure of justified beliefs. It underscores the importance of coherent, well-founded knowledge systems that can withstand scrutiny and challenge.

By grappling with this concept, philosophers and practitioners alike are better equipped to construct robust frameworks for understanding and validating knowledge across various domains.
