# Anchoring Bias

> [!definition] **Anchoring Bias**
> Anchoring Bias is the tendency to rely too heavily on an initial piece of information (the 'anchor') when making decisions, even if that anchor is arbitrary or irrelevant. It falls under [[cognitive-architecture]], where it demonstrates how our judgment systems are influenced by initial pieces of information in a way that can lead to systematic errors.

> [!attention] **Boundary**
> This concept excludes other cognitive biases and heuristics like confirmation bias or availability heuristic, which operate through different mechanisms but can interact with anchoring effects.

## Core Explanation

Anchoring Bias operates through the process of selective accessibility and insufficient adjustment from the anchor. When we receive an initial piece of information, it becomes more accessible or salient in our minds, making subsequent judgments heavily influenced by this initial value. For instance, if a person is asked to estimate the population of a city after being given an arbitrary number, they are likely to adjust their estimate based on that number rather than starting from scratch.

The core mechanism behind Anchoring Bias can be seen in various experimental settings where participants are given different anchors and then asked to make judgments. Even when these initial values are irrelevant or transparently arbitrary, people tend to rely heavily on them. This bias is particularly resilient; it occurs even when participants are warned about the anchor's potential influence, suggesting that it is a fundamental feature of our judgment systems rather than a simple oversight.

Theoretical roots and conceptual nuances of Anchoring Bias can be traced back to the work of Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman. They demonstrated through experiments that people do not adjust their judgments sufficiently from the initial anchor, leading to systematic errors in decision-making. This bias is not just about insufficient cognitive effort but also involves selective accessibility, where information consistent with the anchor becomes more prominent during deliberation.

Empirical evidence supporting Anchoring Bias comes from numerous studies showing its robustness across different contexts and populations. For example, Tversky and Kahneman's experiments involved participants estimating numerical values after being given an arbitrary starting point, which influenced their final estimates significantly.

## Mechanism

The mechanism of Anchoring Bias involves selective accessibility, where information consistent with the anchor becomes more prominent during deliberation. This means that even if a participant is aware of the initial value's arbitrariness, it still influences their subsequent judgments by making related information more accessible in memory.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application 1 — Negotiations**
> In negotiations, anchoring bias can significantly impact outcomes. For example, if one party sets an initial price that is arbitrarily high or low, the other party may adjust their counteroffer based on this anchor rather than starting from a neutral position. This can lead to suboptimal agreements where both parties end up with less favorable terms than they might have otherwise.

> [!example] **Application 2 — Financial Assessments**
> In financial assessments, anchoring bias can affect the valuation of assets or investments. If an initial estimate is set based on flawed data or arbitrary assumptions, subsequent analyses may be heavily influenced by this anchor, leading to incorrect valuations and poor investment decisions.

> [!example] **Application 3 — Instructional Design**
> In instructional design, anchoring bias can influence how learners process information. For instance, if a course starts with an overly complex explanation of a concept, students might struggle to understand simpler related material that follows, as their initial anchor has set a high bar for comprehension.

> [!example] **Application 4 — Survey Design**
> In survey design, anchoring bias can affect responses when questions are framed in a way that includes an arbitrary starting point. For example, if a question asks respondents to estimate the number of people who support a policy after showing them a figure that is either too high or too low, their estimates may be skewed towards this anchor rather than reflecting actual opinions.

## Key Distinctions

> [!key-distinction] **Anchoring Bias vs. Confirmation Bias**
> While both biases involve initial information influencing subsequent judgments, Anchoring Bias focuses on the reliance on an arbitrary starting point, whereas Confirmation Bias involves seeking out and giving more weight to evidence that confirms pre-existing beliefs or hypotheses.

> [!key-distinction] **Anchoring Bias vs. Availability Heuristic**
> Both biases are influenced by readily available information, but anchoring bias specifically refers to the influence of a particular initial value on subsequent judgments, while availability heuristic involves basing judgments on the ease with which relevant instances come to mind.

## Key Figures

- **Amos Tversky** — Co-discovered Anchoring Bias in 1974 and demonstrated its robustness through various experiments, showing that even when participants are warned about the anchor's influence, they still exhibit this bias.
- **Daniel Kahneman** — Co-discovered Anchoring Bias with Tversky and further explored its implications for decision-making processes, integrating it into broader theories of cognitive biases.

## Synthesis

Understanding Anchoring Bias is crucial for improving decision-making processes across various domains. By recognizing the influence of initial anchors, we can develop strategies to mitigate its effects in fields such as finance, negotiation, and education. This bias highlights the importance of being aware of our cognitive shortcuts and actively seeking out alternative perspectives or data points to ensure more accurate judgments.

The study of Anchoring Bias also intersects with other concepts like Heuristics and Biases, where it serves as a foundational example of how mental shortcuts can lead to systematic errors. By integrating insights from Anchoring Bias into broader theories of cognitive architecture, we can better understand the complex interplay between initial information and subsequent judgments.
