# Organisational Decision-Making and Institutional Design

> [!definition] Organisational Decision-Making and Institutional Design
> Organisational Decision-Making and Institutional Design refers to the structured processes within an organisation that guide how decisions are made, with routine procedures handling most cases while deliberate scrutiny is reserved for specific triggering conditions designed to catch critical failures in pattern-matching.

## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] Organisational Decision-Making and Institutional Design
> **Structural principle:** Organisations, like individual minds, exhibit a default-interventionist architecture — most decisions are made by routine pattern-matching against established procedures, and only a small subset are subjected to deliberate scrutiny. The triggering conditions for organisational scrutiny are themselves designed (formally or informally) — escalation procedures, review boards, audit cycles, post-mortem reviews — and the quality of organisational judgment depends on whether these triggering conditions are calibrated to engage at the points where routine pattern-matching is most likely to fail.
> **Concrete application:** A hospital's morbidity and mortality conference functions as an organisational analogue of cognitive forcing functions — it ensures that decisions which produced unexpected outcomes are subjected to retrospective deliberation that the original decision context did not afford. The framework predicts that such conferences will be most effective when the triggering criteria (which cases are reviewed) are themselves designed to capture the conditions under which intuitive clinical judgment is least reliable.
> **Boundary condition:** The transfer holds for organisations whose routine procedures are well-calibrated to the environments in which they operate; it weakens when organisational routines have been calibrated to a different environment than the one currently faced (e.g., regulatory regimes designed for a previous era).
