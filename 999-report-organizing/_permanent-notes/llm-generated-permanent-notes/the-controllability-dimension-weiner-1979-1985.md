# The Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)

> [!definition] The Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)
> The [[controllability]] dimension — the last of the three to be formally incorporated into the taxonomy — classifies causes as either controllable by the individual (effort, strategy choice, attention allocation) or uncontrollable (ability, mood, illness, luck). This dimension cuts across both locus and stability: effort is internal, unstable, and controllable; ability is internal, stable, and uncontrollable; task difficulty is external, stable, and uncontrollable.
>
> **Boundary:** Controllability is a property of the cause, not a judgment about the person. The question is not "Can you control the outcome?" but "Can you control the factor that caused the outcome?" This distinction matters because attributions to uncontrollable causes generate pity and sympathy from observers, while attributions to controllable causes generate anger and blame.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The controllability dimension is the primary bridge between attribution theory and moral judgment. It is through [[the-controllability-dimension]] that explanations for behaviour become evaluations of character — the mechanism through which a failure attributed to laziness (controllable) provokes anger, while the same failure attributed to illness (uncontrollable) provokes compassion.
>
