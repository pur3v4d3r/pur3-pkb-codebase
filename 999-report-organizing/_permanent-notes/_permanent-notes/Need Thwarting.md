# Need Thwarting

> [!definition] Need Thwarting
> Need thwarting refers to partner behaviors that actively undermine the other person's basic psychological needs. Autonomy thwarting includes controlling behavior, psychological pressure, emotional coercion, surveillance, and conditional positive regard (withholding warmth unless the partner behaves as demanded). Competence thwarting includes chronic criticism, deprecation of the partner's abilities, and undermining their efforts in domains that matter to them. Relatedness thwarting includes emotional unavailability, dismissiveness, contempt, and communication patterns that leave the partner feeling fundamentally misunderstood or uncared-for.

Distinct from merely failing to provide need support — it involves positive actions (controlling demands, conditional approval, dismissiveness, criticism) that directly suppress autonomy, competence, or relatedness need satisfaction. Need thwarting is empirically separable from need support absence and predicts worse outcomes (particularly ill-being and psychopathology) over and above what need support alone predicts.

> [!evidence] Need Thwarting
> The active frustration of basic psychological needs — not merely the absence of need satisfaction but the presence of conditions that directly impede need expression and fulfilment. Need thwarting is empirically and theoretically distinct from low need satisfaction: it is predicted by specific aversive contextual features (coercion for autonomy, persistent failure/humiliation for competence, exclusion/conditional regard for relatedness), and it specifically predicts ill-being (depression, anxiety, aggression) rather than simply reduced well-being. This distinction has significant implications for intervention design: creating need-satisfying conditions and removing need-thwarting conditions are partially independent tasks.
> *— [[self-determination-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11_report]]*
