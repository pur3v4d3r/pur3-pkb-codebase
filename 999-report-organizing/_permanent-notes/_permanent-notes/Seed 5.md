> **Q:** What are the three primary mechanisms through which approach-avoidance regulation shapes learning outcomes?
> **A:** (1) Attentional scope modulation — avoidance narrows attention toward self-relevant threat cues at the cost of task-relevant processing. (2) Help-seeking suppression — PAV goals make adaptive help-seeking socially costly, redirecting the learner toward avoidant strategies. (3) Failure attribution patterns — approach goals support controllable, specific failure attributions; avoidance goals support stable, uncontrollable ability attributions that generalize across contexts.
> **Source:** Phase IV, Mechanisms
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Process

> **Q:** What distinguishes guilt from shame in Weiner's framework, and why does this distinction matter for intervention?
> **A:** Guilt is generated by controllable internal causal attributions (I failed because I didn't try hard enough — I could have). Shame is generated by uncontrollable internal attributions (I failed because I lack the ability — I couldn't have). Guilt is motivationally facilitative because the cause is changeable; shame is paralyzing because the cause is fixed. Effective interventions shift negative affect from shame to guilt.
> **Source:** Phase IV, Section 4.2
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Distinction
> **Tags:** #guilt #shame #controllability #attribution-theory

> **Q:** What is the Calibration-Integrated Model's key departure from both the ITM and the simple MCM?
> **A:** It proposes a multiplicative relationship between information quality and calibration receptivity: Effectiveness = f(Information Quality × Calibration Receptivity). This is a departure from both additive accounts (ITM adds information; MCM adds calibration separately) because it predicts that even high-quality information multiplied by near-zero calibration receptivity yields near-zero gain. The multiplicative structure explains why information-rich, gain-poor patterns occur.
> **Source:** Phase VI, original synthesis
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Definition
> **Tags:** #calibration-integrated-model, #original-synthesis, #multiplicative-relationship

> **Q:** Why do life transitions create windows of opportunity for habit change, according to the dual-system architecture?
> **A:** Life transitions disrupt the contextual configurations that serve as cues for established habits. The new environment has not yet acquired cue properties for old habits, weakening the context-action associations. This reduces the habitual system's competitive advantage, making the goal-directed system more influential and new habit formation more accessible. Wendy Wood calls this the "fresh start" window.
> **Source:** Phase III (Wood's research), Phase V (transition windows)
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Application
> **Tags:** #context-disruption, #life-transitions, #habit-change

> **Q:** What does Vygotsky's account of inner speech predict about the source of a reader's inner evaluative vocabulary?
> **A:** Because inner speech is internalized social dialogue, the evaluative vocabulary of a reader's inner speech reflects the evaluative language used in the social environments (teachers, parents, texts, peers) they inhabited during reading development. Negative inner self-talk is not self-generated — it is the internalized residue of social evaluations. This means vocabulary change requires re-internalization: extended contact with new vocabulary in self-relevant, need-satisfying contexts.
> **Source:** Phase IV; Alternative Framework 1
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Process
> **Tags:** #vygotsky #inner-speech #internalization #srl

> **Q:** What is the "agency asymmetry" problem in context regulation?
>
> **A:** Learners have unequal degrees of freedom to regulate context — some have private study spaces, flexible schedules, and multiple resource options; others face shared environments, constrained schedules, and limited resources. MSLQ studies measuring context regulation confound the *capacity* to regulate context with the *available opportunity* to do so. High-performing learners may appear better context regulators partly because they have more regulable contexts.
>
> **Source:** Phase IV, Mechanism 3

> **Q:** What is the Bootstrapping Constraint, and when does it bind most tightly?
>
> **A:** The Bootstrapping Constraint is the structural constraint that schema construction requires existing schemas as inputs. It binds most tightly for *categorically novel* concepts — concepts that do not belong to any existing schema category. For categorically novel content, there is no existing schema to serve as the construction input, and the primary resolution is analogical scaffolding from a structurally isomorphic source domain.
>
> **Source:** Phase IV
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Definition

> **Q**: Under what conditions does schema-activation-first instruction outperform productive failure, and under what conditions does it underperform?
> **A**: Schema-activation-first is appropriate for accretion and tuning goals — adding information to an existing framework or adjusting its parameters. Productive failure (disruption-first) outperforms for restructuring goals — when the prior schema is actively inconsistent with target understanding and must be dismantled and rebuilt. The key skill is diagnosing which goal applies.
> **Source**: Phase V, Pedagogical Inversion Original Synthesis
> **Difficulty**: Advanced
> **Type**: Application

> **Q:** How does the received view survive dialectical scrutiny, and what specifically does it claim that proves false?
>
> **A:** What survives: decoding automaticity is necessary for proficient reading, frees working memory for higher-level processing, and is the binding constraint for early readers and those with genuine decoding deficits. What collapses: the claim that comprehension of the whole follows automatically from comprehension of the parts — the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy. Decoding is necessary but not sufficient; situation model construction requires dedicated inference-generation and model-monitoring operations not derived from sentence-level processing.

> [!flashcard] Seed 5
> **Q:** Why does the Self-Reflection Phase require the Forethought Phase to have produced a written comprehension criterion?
> **A:** Because self-evaluation requires a comparison between actual performance and a pre-set standard. Without a written criterion, (a) the original goal is typically lost to cognitive load interference during the reading session, and (b) self-evaluation defaults to phenomenological assessment (did this feel productive?) rather than epistemic assessment (did I achieve what I set out to understand?). Written criteria are the structural mechanism that preserves evaluability.
> **Source:** Phase IV and V
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Process

> [!flashcard] Seed 5
> **Q:** What is the Fluency Illusion and why does it threaten the Self-Reflection Phase?
> **A:** The Fluency Illusion is the systematic misperception of familiarity as understanding — the subjective sense of "getting it" during reading, even when genuine comprehension is absent. It threatens the Self-Reflection Phase because self-evaluation based on subjective impressions (rather than retrieval-based testing) produces systematically inflated comprehension confidence, leading to poor calibration and misallocated study time.

> [!flashcard] Seed 5
> **Q:** What is the "calibration engine" framing of the SRL cycle, and what does it predict about highly successful students?
>
> **A:** The calibration engine framing holds that the SRL cycle's primary product is not performance outcomes but progressive refinement of the learner's internal models of capability, strategy effectiveness, and task structure. It predicts that highly successful students may have poorly developed SRL if they have never encountered tasks at the genuine edge of their capability — because success without informative failure provides no calibration data and leaves the calibration engine underdeveloped.
>
> **Source:** Phase VI, Calibration Engine synthesis
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Application/Connection

> [!flashcard] Seed 5
> **Q:** What does the reconstructed understanding of the Zimmerman cycle propose as the cycle's proper use?
>
> **A:** The reconstruction proposes that the Zimmerman cycle is most accurately a *normative framework* describing the aspirational architecture of expert SRL — not a universal descriptive account of typical learning, and not a prescriptive recipe. Its greatest practical value is as a *design specification for learning environments*: the cycle's phases specify *environmental requirements* (conditions for calibrated self-efficacy, conditions for accurate monitoring, conditions for non-defensive self-reflection) rather than learner behaviors. This reframing preserves the model's empirical core while addressing the practical and empirical challenges.
>
