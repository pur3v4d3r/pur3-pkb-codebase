# Reading Comprehension

> [!definition] **Reading Comprehension** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Reading comprehension is the process of constructing a coherent mental representation of a text's meaning that goes beyond the literal linguistic content. It requires the integration of information extracted from the text with the reader's existing knowledge, motivational resources, and metacognitive capacities. Comprehension is not a passive reception of meaning but an active, constructive process in which the reader continuously builds, updates, and revises a multi-level internal model of what the text is about.
> 
> **Boundary:** Reading comprehension is NOT equivalent to word recognition, accurate oral reading, or the ability to recall surface-level statements. A reader may reproduce sentences verbatim while failing to comprehend the text at the level of a coherent, integrated representation.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This definition establishes that comprehension is a multi-level construction problem, not a single capacity. All subsequent sections address different components and constraints of this construction process.
> 
> **See also:** [[situation-model]], [[textbase]], [[language-comprehension]], [[simple-view-of-reading]], [[walter-kintsch]]

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Much of what passes for reading instruction targets textbase construction: summarization, main idea identification, vocabulary definition. These are necessary preconditions, not endpoints. The goal of academic reading — and the target of metacognitive regulation — is situation model depth: the richness of the mental simulation constructed from the text. A reader who can tell you what a text said but cannot use that knowledge to reason about novel problems has constructed a textbase, not a situation model. This distinction reframes the entire enterprise of reading instruction and self-regulated reading practice.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> The most comprehensive systematic review of learning strategies (Dunlosky et al., 2013, reviewing 250+ studies) produces a clear hierarchy for academic reading and retention. **High utility strategies**: retrieval practice (practice testing) and spaced repetition. **Moderate utility strategies**: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaving. **Low utility strategies** (despite widespread use): re-reading and highlighting. This hierarchy constitutes the most practically important research finding for students designing academic reading practices. Almost all students allocate disproportionate effort to low-utility strategies (re-reading and passive highlighting) while underinvesting in high-utility strategies (retrieval practice, elaborative interrogation, self-explanation). Metacognitive knowledge of this hierarchy — and the discipline to act against intuition (re-reading *feels* like a stronger strategy than retrieval practice) — is among the highest-leverage interventions for academic reading effectiveness.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Prior knowledge is both the most powerful comprehension facilitator and the most recalcitrant obstacle to developing comprehension in new domains. A reader with strong prior knowledge comprehends academic texts in their domain with apparent ease — their schemas absorb and organize new information automatically. But a reader entering a new academic domain begins without these schemas, and the very texts that would convey domain knowledge are incomprehensible without the domain knowledge they would convey. This is the prior knowledge paradox: you need to know something about a topic to understand texts about it, but texts are the primary vehicle through which you learn about topics. The resolution of this paradox — developing entry-level schemas sufficient to make academic texts accessible — is one of the central challenges of academic reading instruction, particularly for learners encountering new disciplines.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> The most important theoretical claim in the metacognitive reading literature is that comprehension failure and comprehension success are not merely a function of reading ability in the narrow sense, but are substantially determined by whether the reader *detects* failure when it occurs and possesses the regulatory repertoire to respond appropriately. The [[monitoring-gap]] — the systematic discrepancy between actual and monitored comprehension — is the central diagnostic construct of the field.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> Brown's empirical thesis, supported across a substantial body of research, establishes that skilled reading is not merely fluent reading; it is *self-regulated* reading in which the reader continuously evaluates the coherence and adequacy of their comprehension and deploys strategies as comprehension failures are detected. The strategic reading repertoire is the behavioral face of [[regulation-of-cognition]].

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> The relationship between metacognition and inference generation during reading is bidirectional: deliberate inference generation enhances comprehension monitoring by giving the monitoring system more propositions to evaluate for coherence, while comprehension monitoring that detects a coherence gap triggers deliberate inference generation as a remedial strategy. This bidirectionality makes inference generation a core component of the metacognitive reading loop, not merely an auxiliary process.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> The critical implementation requirement for reciprocal teaching is *authentic procedural commitment* to the metacognitive-regulatory logic of the four strategies, not mere sequential execution. Teachers who implement reciprocal teaching as a rotation through four named steps — without connecting each step to its comprehension-monitoring function — produce significantly smaller effect sizes. The value of the intervention lies precisely in its metacognitive scaffolding of the regulatory inner dialogue.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> The cognitive architecture of reading creates a specific vulnerability known as the [[fluency-illusion|Fluency Illusion]] — the subjective experience of comprehension that can accompany smooth reading even when genuine understanding is absent. Because fluent readers process words automatically, the absence of decoding effort generates a metacognitive signal that can be misread as comprehension rather than merely as decoding success. Academic readers are particularly susceptible to this illusion in domains where they recognize vocabulary without understanding it — where the surface form of scientific, legal, or philosophical prose creates a compelling phenomenology of understanding. The Fluency Illusion is the architectural basis of the "I read it but didn't learn it" experience so common in student populations. Metacognitive training directly targets this illusion by teaching readers to distinguish the feeling of processing from the evidence of understanding.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Re-reading is the most common self-regulatory response to detected comprehension difficulty — and among the least effective for improving comprehension and memory. Research by Dunlosky et al. (2013) in their landmark review of learning strategies rated re-reading as having "low utility" despite being overwhelmingly popular among students. The mechanism of re-reading's ineffectiveness is illuminating: it primarily improves fluency — making the text feel more familiar — without substantially deepening situation model construction or correcting the specific comprehension failure. Worse, the improved fluency of re-reading generates a stronger Fluency Illusion, increasing readers' confidence in their comprehension without warranting it. Effective regulation requires not passive re-reading but *active fix-up* strategies — generating specific comprehension questions, attempting guided retrieval, restating in one's own words, or identifying the exact locus of failure.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> The transfer deficit problem deserves particular weight. An instructional program that teaches comprehension monitoring strategies and produces measurable comprehension gains in a structured classroom context may be doing little more than externalizing monitoring through instructional scaffolding, without building the internal metacognitive capacity for students to monitor independently. The ultimate test of metacognitive reading instruction is not performance on structured practice tasks but spontaneous metacognitive engagement in novel, unstructured reading contexts.

## Conceptual Tensions

> [!tension] **Tension 1: Comprehension = Understanding vs. Comprehension = Knowing What to Do** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Position A (mentalist):** Comprehension is the construction of an accurate mental representation — a situation model that corresponds to the text's intended meaning. Success is measured by accuracy of representation. *Strongest advocates:* Kintsch, van Dijk, Johnson-Laird.
> 
> **Position B (pragmatist/functional):** Comprehension is demonstrated through what a reader can DO with a text — solve problems using its content, argue against it, apply it, integrate it with other texts. Representation accuracy is necessary but not sufficient. *Strongest advocates:* Graesser (situation awareness),…

> [!tension] **Tension 2: Strategy Instruction vs. Knowledge Building as the Primary Lever** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Position A (strategy-focused):** Academic reading comprehension improves most through explicit instruction in comprehension strategies — text structure awareness, inference generation prompts, self-explanation, reciprocal teaching roles. Skill is teachable independently of content.
> 
> **Position B (knowledge-focused):** Academic reading comprehension improves most through systematic domain knowledge building — rich content curricula, wide academic reading in domains, vocabulary-embedded content learning. Skills without knowledge have nothing to operate on.
>

> [!tension] **Tension 1: Skills-Based vs. Knowledge-Based Reading Instruction** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> A foundational tension in reading comprehension instruction is between *skills-based* approaches (teaching generalizable reading strategies and metacognitive processes that transfer across topics) and *knowledge-based* approaches (building the domain knowledge and vocabulary that comprehension depends on, rather than teaching strategies for comprehending without knowledge). The tension has intensified since Wexler's (2019) "The Knowledge Gap" and the research of Natalie Wexler and E.D. Hirsch arguing that decades of skills-based instruction have failed because students lack the content knowledge that comprehension requires.
> 
> **Position A (Strategy/Skills):** Reading comprehension is fundamentally a metacognitive skill — once students learn to monitor comprehension, generate inferences, activate prior knowledge, and deploy fix-up strategies, these skills transfer to novel topics. Advocates: Palincsar, Pressley, Pearson.
> 
> **Position B (Knowledge):** Reading comprehension is primarily a knowledge phenomenon — without domain knowledge and breadth vocabulary, no strategy produces genuine comprehension. Strategy instruction cannot substitute for knowledge. Advocates: Hirsch, Willingham, Wexler.
> 
> **Current state:** The evidence suggests the dichotomy is false — both are necessary. Prior knowledge heavily constrains strategy effectiveness; strategy instruction without knowledge building underachieves; knowledge building without metacognitive strategy instruction leaves readers unable to monitor or regulate their comprehension. The synthesis position aligns with research on the Matthew Effect-metacognitive interaction: knowledge and metacognition must be co-developed.
> 
> **This report's stance:** Both positions capture a genuine and important partial truth. The Matthew Effect-metacognitive interaction trap (Section 6.4) provides the theoretical integration: knowledge deficits degrade both comprehension performance and metacognitive monitoring quality, making dual investment necessary.

> [!tension] **Tension 2: Strategy Automatization vs. Deliberate Metacognitive Control** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Research on skill acquisition (Anderson, 1982; Fitts, 1964) documents a universal trajectory from controlled, attention-demanding, deliberate processing (declarative knowledge stage) to automatic, effortless, attention-free execution (procedural knowledge stage). But metacognitive regulation is, by definition, deliberate and attention-demanding — it requires conscious monitoring and effortful strategy deployment. The tension: should skilled academic reading involve automatic, effortless processing or deliberate, metacognitive engagement?
> 
> **Position A:** Expert readers should eventually read academic texts automatically — deep comprehension should become effortless, requiring minimal deliberate strategies. Strategy instruction is a developmental scaffold, not a permanent necessity.
> 
> **Position B:** Academic texts are deliberately designed to require deep processing; automatic processing will perpetually fail to produce situation models for genuinely novel content. Even expert readers require persistent deliberate metacognitive engagement with challenging academic texts.
> 
> **This report's stance:** The tension dissolves when levels of specificity are distinguished. Word recognition and syntactic parsing should be highly automatic (to reduce working memory load at the surface level); text structure knowledge should be highly schematized (genre schemas reduce the meta-level attention required to navigate familiar structures). But situation model construction for novel, complex academic content remains and should remain deliberate — the metacognitive effort of challenging comprehension is constitutive of genuine learning, not a temporary developmental condition.

> [!tension] **Monitoring Accuracy vs. Monitoring Engagement** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **The Tension:** Metacognitive reading research implicitly assumes that the primary problem for poor comprehenders is *metacognitive monitoring failure* — failing to detect comprehension breakdowns. However, an alternative hypothesis holds that the primary problem is *monitoring disengagement* — detecting but not responding to breakdowns, often for motivational reasons (monitoring is threatening when comprehension failure confirms negative self-concepts about reading ability).
> 
> **Position A — Cognitive Failure (Baker, Brown):** Poor comprehenders genuinely lack the monitoring sensitivity to detect comprehension failures; their monitoring system is insufficiently calibrated to the situational-model level.
> 
> **Position B — Motivational Disengagement (Pintrich, Zimmerman):** Poor comprehenders often detect failure but suppress the regulatory response to protect self-efficacy; the deficit is motivational rather than purely cognitive.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** Both positions have empirical support; the research base suggests genuine heterogeneity — some struggling readers exhibit monitoring failure in the cognitive sense, others exhibit motivational disengagement, and many exhibit both.
> 
> **Why It Matters:** The distinction has direct instructional implications: monitoring training will not help a student who monitors accurately but disengages motivationally; motivational intervention will not help a student who genuinely cannot detect coherence failures.
> 
> **This Report's Position:** Both pathways are empirically operative; effective reading comprehension instruction should assess which deficit predominates in individual students rather than assuming a uniform mechanism.
> 
> **See also:** [[metacognitive-monitoring]], [[self-efficacy]], [[monitoring-gap]], [[comprehension-monitoring]]

> [!tension] **Strategy Knowledge vs. Strategy Deployment** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **The Tension:** Research consistently demonstrates that strategy instruction increases metacognitive knowledge of reading strategies without reliably producing spontaneous, autonomous strategy deployment in new reading contexts. This strategy-knowledge/strategy-deployment gap challenges the assumption that knowledge of strategies is the primary lever for improving metacognitive reading.
> 
> **Position A — Knowledge First (Strategy Instruction tradition):** Developing declarative and procedural knowledge of strategies is the necessary first step; conditional knowledge and deployment automaticity develop from practice with explicit strategies.
> 
> **Position B — Motivation First (SDT-aligned perspectives):** Strategy deployment is primarily a motivational problem; readers who are autonomously motivated to comprehend will spontaneously discover and use strategies; explicit strategy instruction may undermine intrinsic curiosity if implemented in a controlling rather than autonomy-supportive manner.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** Longitudinal TSI research shows durable deployment when instruction specifically addresses conditional knowledge and is implemented in autonomy-supportive contexts; purely declarative strategy instruction produces minimal deployment outside instructional settings.
> 
> **Why It Matters:** Instructional time and resources should be allocated based on which deficit is proximal; "knowing strategies but not using them" is a fundamentally different problem from "not knowing strategies at all."
> 
> **See also:** [[conditional-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[metacognitive-knowledge]], [[self-regulated-learning]], [[autonomy-support]]

> [!tension] **Individual Strategy Instruction vs. Integrated Strategy Packages** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **The Tension:** Should metacognitive reading instruction focus depth of practice on a small number of individual strategies, or should it develop a coordinated multi-strategy package that students deploy flexibly?
> 
> **Position A — Depth over Breadth:** Individual strategy training with extensive practice produces stronger comprehension gains per strategy than multi-strategy packages; cognitive load during learning is reduced when students focus on one strategy at a time.
> 
> **Position B — Integration is Necessary (TSI, Reciprocal Teaching):** Single-strategy instruction produces strategy-specific knowledge that students do not spontaneously coordinate; integrated packages are necessary for developing the conditional metacognitive knowledge required for flexible, autonomous reading.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** Meta-analytic evidence slightly favors integrated strategy instruction for long-term reading comprehension outcomes; single-strategy instruction produces larger immediate effects but smaller transfer.
> 
> **See also:** [[conditional-metacognitive-knowledge]], [[metacognitive-scaffolding]], [[self-regulated-learning]]

> [!open-question] **Does Metacognitive Reading Skill Transfer Across Text Types?** *(from [[metacognition-and-reading-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **The Question:** To what extent does metacognitive reading competence developed in one text genre (e.g., expository informational text) transfer to other genres (narrative, digital/hyperlinked, multimodal)?
> 
> **What We Know:** Strategy instruction effects are often genre-specific; reciprocal teaching effects are strongest for informational text. Situation model construction differs substantially between narrative and expository text. Digital reading introduces navigation metacognition not present in linear print reading.
> 
> **What We Don't Know:** The degree to which awareness of the differences between text genres — a form of task metacognitive knowledge — enables cross-genre strategy transfer. Whether there is a "deep" metacognitive reading competence that underlies all text-type-specific monitoring skills.
> 
> **Why It Matters:** Educational systems primarily develop metacognitive reading skills with academic expository text; if these skills do not transfer to the digital, hyperlinked, and multimodal texts that students encounter in non-academic contexts, the educational return on strategy instruction is substantially diminished.
> 
> **See also:** [[transfer-of-learning]], [[metacognitive-knowledge]], [[reading-fluency]], [[silent-reading-critical-analysis-focused-analysis-2026-03-21]]

> [!claude-insight] **The Working Memory Paradox of Academic Reading** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> There is a deep paradox in the relationship between working memory and academic reading. Academic texts are designed by domain experts who have extensive schemas reducing their cognitive load — the text's density and compression reflect the author's automatic processing of concepts that require active working memory management in the reader. The expert's low-load experience of writing the text structurally encodes the novice's high-load experience of reading it. This asymmetry is not simply a result of poor writing (though that sometimes contributes); it is structural. Genuine metacognitive development includes recognizing when cognitive load is high — that feeling of struggling to hold earlier material while processing later material — and responding with deliberate regulatory strategies rather than passive re-reading.

> [!claude-insight] **Academic Reading as a Form of Modal Shift** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Academic reading demands a fundamental *modal shift* from the reading mode naturalized across everyday literacy experiences. Most adults who read for pleasure, information, or practical purposes are habituated to reading modes optimized for relatively low-density, contextualized content: narrative reading (following story events), procedural reading (following instructions), and browsing/scanning for sought-after information. Academic reading requires a fundamentally different mode: *discursive reading*, attentive to the logical and evidential structure of an argument, oriented toward discovering *why* the text claims what it claims and *what evidence* supports those claims. Many readers attempt academic texts with habituated narrative or browsing modes, then conclude that academic reading is simply "hard" — when in fact they are applying the wrong mode. Metacognitive development in this domain begins with recognizing the modal distinction and consciously activating discursive reading mode before beginning an academic text.

> [!claude-insight] **Strategy Selection as the Meta-Strategy** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> A coherent picture emerges from the evidence base on academic reading strategies: **strategy selection is itself the master skill**. No strategy is universally optimal; each has conditions under which it is most powerful. Retrieval practice is most effective for content that must be recalled accurately; elaborative interrogation is most powerful for discrete factual claims; self-explanation is most powerful for causal mechanisms and logical arguments; dual coding is most powerful for spatial, structural, and sequential content. The reader who can identify what kind of content they are reading, what comprehension goal they are pursuing, and which strategy is most appropriate — and who then deploys that strategy deliberately — is demonstrating conditional metacognitive knowledge in its most fully developed form. This meta-strategic capacity does not develop automatically from general reading experience; it requires explicit strategy instruction with comparative evaluation across conditions, reflection on effectiveness, and deliberate practice in selecting from a repertoire.

> [!claude-insight] **Metacognitive Instruction Must Precede Strategy Instruction** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> A systematic review of comprehension instruction research reveals an important sequencing principle that is frequently violated in instructional practice: **metacognitive awareness of comprehension processes should be developed before or simultaneously with strategy instruction, not after**. Many reading strategy programs fail or have weak effects because they teach strategies without first developing students' metacognitive awareness of the comprehension problems those strategies address. Students who do not yet recognize that they construct textbases without situation models, who do not yet notice comprehension monitoring failures, who do not yet experience the difference between surface familiarity and deep understanding — these students have no motivational and functional context for strategy use. They learn strategies as arbitrary procedures rather than as solutions to comprehension problems they have actually experienced. Effective instruction begins by developing comprehension awareness — creating conditions where students can observe their own monitoring failures, compare shallow and deep comprehension products, and notice the gap between fluency and understanding. Only on this foundation does strategy instruction achieve its full potential. This sequencing principle applies equally to self-directed adult learners developing academic reading skill: diagnosis of metacognitive awareness gaps should precede prescriptive strategy training.

> [!claude-insight] **The Metacognitive Ceiling Problem in Self-Directed Academic Reading** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> The deepest challenge for lifelong academic readers developing their practice outside formal instruction contexts is what might be called the **metacognitive ceiling problem**: our metacognitive assessments of our own comprehension are limited by our current comprehension. We cannot evaluate whether our situation model for a domain is accurate and complete using only the knowledge contained in that same situation model. Expert comprehension of a domain text is required to fully evaluate whether an amateur comprehension of the same text is adequate — which means expert evaluation is unavailable to the amateur by definition. This creates predictable patterns: self-directed readers in new domains tend to significantly overestimate comprehension quality (because monitoring using their own deficient schemas cannot detect schema-level failures), tend to feel more confident in poorly-understood domains after reading (because even shallow understanding feels like progress relative to prior ignorance), and tend to underinvest in domain knowledge building (because metacognitively they believe they have understood more than they have). The practical solution to the metacognitive ceiling problem is systematic external calibration: seeking expert feedback, using retrieval testing with authoritative answer keys (structured study materials, practice problems with solutions, or discussion with domain experts), and maintaining what might be called *calibration humility* — a principled prior belief that one's comprehension in any new domain is likely shallower than it feels, warranting more investment and testing than intuition suggests.

> [!section-summary] **Section 7 Key Takeaways** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> 1. Scaffolded instruction — providing expert support that progressively withdraws as competence develops — is the primary mechanism for developing reading comprehension skills that transfer to independent practice. The I-We-You scaffolding reduction sequence is the developmental arc.
> 2. Reciprocal Teaching is the most extensively validated reading comprehension intervention, integrating metacognitive strategy development with social modeling and distributed comprehension in a systematic format with robust transfer effects.
> 3. Self-regulated reading as the instructional end-goal involves three phases (forethought, performance, self-reflection) that together constitute a metacognitive reading cycle. Developing each phase requires explicit attention in instruction and deliberate practice in study.

> [!section-summary] **Section 8 Key Takeaways** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> 1. The transition to self-directed academic reading requires internalizing the scaffolding functions previously provided by formal education: goal-setting, comprehension monitoring, strategy selection, and learning evaluation.
> 2. PKM practices (progressive summarization, atomic note-making, spaced review, link-building) provide systematic infrastructure for sustaining cumulative academic knowledge development over a lifetime of self-directed reading.
> 3. Writing to learn — particularly explanatory and argumentative writing — is not just a pedagogical scaffold but a permanent epistemic practice that tests and deepens comprehension in ways that reading alone cannot achieve.
> 4. The metacognitive ceiling problem — the limitation of self-evaluation to what one already knows — creates systematic overconfidence in new domains and warrants principled external calibration practices.

## Protocols & Methods

> [!protocol] **Protocol 1: Pre-Reading Activation and Goal-Setting (Adapted from SRL Forethought Phase)** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **When to use:** Before beginning any challenging academic text — journal articles, textbook chapters, primary literature, academic argument essays.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Preview structure** (2 minutes): Read title, abstract/summary, headings, and conclusion first. Form an initial prediction about the text's central argument.
> 2. **Background knowledge audit** (1 minute): Ask: What do I already know about this topic? What are my existing schemas? Are any of them likely to be challenged? Write 2–3 bullet points.
> 3. **Vocabulary scan** (2 minutes): Skim text for unfamiliar vocabulary, especially Tier 2 academic words and domain-specific Tier 3 terms. Look up essential terms before reading.
> 4. **Goal-setting** (1 minute): Write one specific reading goal ("I want to understand how X explains Y" or…

> **When to use:** During dense, conceptually challenging passages in academic texts where surface reading is insufficient.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Read one paragraph or coherent section.**
> 2. **Cover or look away from text.**
> 3. **Self-explain:** State in your own words what the passage argued — what was the claim, what was the evidence, what inferences connect them. Be specific.
> 4. **Gap detection:** Identify anything you couldn't explain clearly. This is a genuine comprehension gap — mark it explicitly.
> 5. **Targeted re-reading:** Return to the text only to address identified gaps — look for the answer to your specific question, not for general re-familiarisation.
> 6. **Inference check:** Articulate the most important inference the passage requires that is NOT stated explicitly.

> [!protocol] **Protocol 1: The PREP-READ-RETRIEVE-WRITE Academic Reading Protocol** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> A four-phase protocol for academic texts optimizing generative processing, metacognitive monitoring, and long-term retention:
> 
> **Phase 1 — PREP (5-10 min before reading):**
> - Structural preview: examine title, abstract, headings, topic sentences, conclusion
> - Prior knowledge activation: write 3-5 sentences on what you already know about the topic
> - Set comprehension goals: formulate 3 specific questions you want this text to answer
> - Activate appropriate reading mode: this is discursive reading, not narrative reading
> 
> **Phase 2 — READ (calibrated to text difficulty):**
> - Deploy section-by-section reading: complete one major section before moving to next
> - Self-explain at causal mechanism points: explain "why" to yourself before reading further
> - Annotate comprehension failures explicitly: mark passages where monitoring signals failure
> - Apply elaborative interrogation to key claims: "Why is this true? What evidence supports it?"
> 
> **Phase 3 — RETRIEVE (10-15 min after reading each major section):**
> - Close the text and retrieve everything recalled about the section
> - Write your retrieval (even just notes) to check against text
> - Return to text specifically for items not recalled or recalled inaccurately
> - Answer your three comprehension goal questions
> 
> **Phase 4 — WRITE (15-30 min after completing the full text):**
> - Write a synthesis paragraph integrating the text's central argument with prior knowledge
> - Write one question the text raises that it does not answer
> - Write one connection to other texts, ideas, or experiences
> - Note any comprehension failures that remain unresolved and require follow-up
