# Script (Schank and Abelson, 1977)

> [!definition] Script (Schank and Abelson, 1977)
> A script is a stereotyped sequence of actions that defines a well-known social situation — essentially, a schema for event sequences. The restaurant script, Schank and Abelson's canonical example, includes entering, being seated, receiving a menu, ordering, eating, receiving the bill, paying, and leaving. Scripts consist of: *entry conditions* (the circumstances under which the script applies), *roles* (the characters involved: customer, waiter, chef), *props* (physical objects required: menu, food, check), *scenes* (major subdivisions of the script), and *results* (what is true after the script concludes). Scripts generate predictions, guide action, and support comprehension of narrative by allowing readers to infer unstated steps.

> [!evidence] Script (Schank and Abelson, 1977)
> A **script** is a stereotyped sequence of events characterizing a familiar situation — a cognitive structure that specifies the typical *actors*, *props*, *scenes*, *roles*, and *sequence of actions* that constitute a conventional social event. The restaurant script, for example, specifies: entering → being seated → receiving menu → ordering → eating → receiving bill → paying → leaving, along with the typical actors (customer, waiter, chef), typical props (menu, table, napkins, bill), and typical enabling conditions (having money, being hungry). Scripts are procedural event schemas: they encode knowledge about how to participate in and comprehend familiar situational sequences.
> *— [[schema-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31_report]]*
