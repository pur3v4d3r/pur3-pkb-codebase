# Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)

> [!definition] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The use of external resources — physical objects, digital tools, other people — to perform cognitive operations that would otherwise be performed internally, thereby reducing the working memory demands of a task. Cognitive offloading is adaptive when the offloaded operation would impose excessive load without improving schema formation; it is potentially maladaptive when it substitutes for the internal processing that produces durable schemas. Distinguished from extended mind (which makes a metaphysical claim about the mind's boundaries) by focusing on functional load reduction rather than cognitive extension.
>
> **See also:** [[cognitive-offloading]], [[extended-mind-theory]], [[4e-cognition]], [[active-note-making]]

> [!evidence] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The use of the environment to reduce the cognitive demands placed on internal biological cognitive systems. Offloading encompasses strategies ranging from writing things down to arranging physical objects spatially to using digital reminders. Importantly, offloading is not the same as cognitive extension in Clark and Chalmers's sense: offloading is a strategy for reducing cognitive load, while extension is a claim about the actual constitution of the cognitive system. The distinction is subtle but theoretically important: a person can offload extensively without any of the offloaded processes qualifying as part of their extended cognitive system.
> *— [[extended-mind-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11_report]]*

> [!evidence] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The deliberate or habitual use of environmental resources to reduce demands on internal cognitive systems. Examples include writing lists to reduce memory load, setting alarms to support prospective memory, and using spatial arrangement of objects to encode task state. Cognitive offloading is empirically well-documented and clearly adaptive. Distinguished from cognitive extension: offloading accepts that the relevant cognitive work is happening (or no longer happening) internally; extension claims the work may be genuinely distributed across the organism-environment system.

> [!evidence] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The use of external resources to reduce the cognitive demands of a task. Includes strategies such as writing things down (reducing memory load), using calculators (reducing computation load), and delegating monitoring to external systems (reducing metacognitive load). A double-edged phenomenon: reduces immediate cognitive demands but may prevent development of internal capacity.
>
> **Boundary:** Not all tool use constitutes cognitive offloading. The term applies when the external resource *replaces* rather than merely *augments* an internal cognitive process.
>

> The use of external tools, technologies, or environmental structures to reduce internal cognitive load by delegating memory, computation, or decision-making functions to the environment. Beneficial when accessing the external storage is less cognitively costly than internal reconstruction; potentially harmful when it degrades internal encoding without providing reliable external access.
>
> **Boundary:** Cognitive offloading is distinct from the Extended Mind Thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998), though related. Offloading describes a cognitive strategy; the extended mind is a philosophical claim about the boundaries of cognition itself.

> [!evidence] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The use of action to alter the information-processing demands on the cognitive system by externalising cognitive operations onto the environment. Offloading reduces the need to represent or manipulate information internally, instead distributing those operations across the agent-environment system.
>
> **Boundary:** Cognitive offloading is not equivalent to simply using tools. The relevant feature is that the action specifically alters what the cognitive system must do — it modifies the computational demand, not merely the physical environment. Writing a note is offloading when it reduces what must be held in working memory; writing a note as a decorative act is not.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Provides the mechanistic account of why PKM systems reduce cognitive load — they externalise the maintenance, manipulation, and retrieval of information that would otherwise compete for working memory capacity.
>
> **See also:** [[extended-mind-theory]], [[epistemic-actions]], [[distributed-cognition]], [[cognitive-load-theory]], [[active-externalism]]

> [!evidence] Cognitive Offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
> The use of action and the environment to reduce the cognitive demands of a task by transferring information processing or storage to external resources. Encompasses three major categories: motor offloading (using bodily actions to reduce internal processing, e.g., counting on fingers), perceptual offloading (using environmental structure to offload pattern recognition or spatial reasoning, e.g., maps), and computational offloading (externalising calculation or information organisation, e.g., written arithmetic). Regulated by the ease-of-offload: practitioners offload more when offloading is less costly.
>
> **Boundary:** Cognitive offloading is not synonymous with learning or with knowledge management. It describes the cognitive strategy of reducing internal processing demands through externalisation; whether this strategy is epistemically beneficial depends on whether the offloaded processing would have contributed to schema formation and [[long-term-memory]] consolidation.
>
