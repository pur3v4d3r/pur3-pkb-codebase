# Introjected Regulation

> [!definition] Introjected Regulation
> An intermediate form of extrinsic motivation in which the person has taken in (introjected) an external pressure but not truly endorsed it. [[Introjected-Regulation-(Motivation)]] operates through internal contingencies — ego-involvement, shame avoidance, pride seeking, guilt regulation, and contingent self-worth — that maintain the same pressure dynamics as external regulation but from within. Introjection is associated with defensive functioning, anxiety, and the characteristic phenomenology of ego threat.

> [!evidence] Introjected Regulation
> The regulatory style in which the learner is driven by internalized pressures: guilt, shame, ego-threat, or the need to maintain contingent self-esteem. Introjected regulation is classified as controlled even though the source of pressure is internal, because the learner is not freely choosing engagement — they are responding to internal compulsions that feel obligatory rather than chosen. Students who study primarily to avoid feeling like a failure are exhibiting introjected regulation.

> [!evidence] Introjected Regulation
> A form of extrinsic motivation in which the regulation has been taken in but not genuinely integrated — the person acts to avoid guilt, shame, or ego-threat, or to gain approval and self-esteem. The regulation is experienced as an internal compulsion or pressure rather than as a genuine expression of self. Introjection produces contingent self-esteem, ego-involvement, heightened anxiety around performance, and vulnerability to catastrophic responses to failure. It is a controlled form of extrinsic motivation, despite involving no external contingencies, because its locus of regulation is an internal pressure rather than a freely endorsed value.
> *— [[self-determination-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-11_report]]*

> [!evidence] Introjected Regulation
> The OIT regulatory style in which the regulating contingencies have been taken into the person but not endorsed by the self. The person regulates the behavior to avoid internal experiences (shame, guilt, anxiety) or pursue internal experiences (pride, self-esteem) that have come to be contingent on the behavior. Distinguished from external regulation by being internally produced; distinguished from identified regulation by lacking the conscious value-endorsement that identification requires.
