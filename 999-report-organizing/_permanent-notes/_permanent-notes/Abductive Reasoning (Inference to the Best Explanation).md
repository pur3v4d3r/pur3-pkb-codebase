# Abductive Reasoning (Inference to the Best Explanation)

> [!definition] Abductive Reasoning (Inference to the Best Explanation)
> A third form of inference, distinct from both induction and deduction, in which the reasoner infers the most plausible or explanatory hypothesis given observed evidence. Abduction asks: *given these facts, what would best explain them?* A physician who observes a cluster of symptoms and infers the most likely diagnosis is reasoning abductively. Charles Sanders Peirce distinguished abduction from induction in the nineteenth century; where induction generalizes from instances, abduction explains them. In practice, these forms often interweave — a learner may reason abductively to a hypothesis and then inductively test it.

> [!evidence] Abductive Reasoning (Inference to the Best Explanation)
> An inference form that moves from a puzzling observation to the hypothesis that would best explain it. Coined by [[charles-sanders-peirce|Charles Sanders Peirce]]. Requires generation of candidate explanations and evaluation against criteria of scope, parsimony, plausibility, and testability. The reasoning mode of scientific discovery, medical diagnosis, historical interpretation, and detective inference. Neither guaranteed (like deduction) nor merely probabilistic (like induction) — it is *evaluative* creative inference.
> *— [[types-of-reasoning-foundational-report-2026-03-05_report]]*
