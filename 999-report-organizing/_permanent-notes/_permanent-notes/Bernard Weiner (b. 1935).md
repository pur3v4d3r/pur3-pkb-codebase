> [!person] Bernard Weiner (b. 1935)
> Social and motivational psychologist at UCLA; architect of the most comprehensive attributional theory in achievement motivation, evolving from Heider's naive psychology and Rotter's locus of control into a three-dimensional framework (locus, stability, controllability) with explicit mappings to affect, expectancy, and social judgment. Key works: *Achievement Motivation and Attribution Theory* (1974); *An Attributional Theory of Motivation and Emotion* (1986); *An Attribution Theory of Achievement Motivation and Emotion* (Psychological Review, 1985).
>
> **Lineage:** Built on Fritz Heider (naive psychology of attribution), Julian Rotter (locus of control), and John Atkinson (expectancy-value theory of motivation). Influenced Carol Dweck (implicit theories of intelligence), Dale Schunk (attribution and self-efficacy in education), and the entire attribution retraining literature.
>
> **Relationship to Report:** Provides the three-dimensional framework that is the structural backbone of the attribution vocabulary architecture in Phases II, IV, and V.

> [!person] Bernard Weiner (b. 1935)
> **Core Contribution:** Developed the three-dimensional attribution model (locus, stability, controllability) and the emotion-mediation framework that constitutes the mature theory. His work transformed attribution theory from a social perception framework (focused on how people explain others' behavior) into an achievement motivation theory (focused on how self-attributions generate emotional and motivational consequences).
>
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Built directly on Heider's internal/external distinction and Kelley's covariation model; corresponds with Lazarus's independent development of appraisal theory; influenced Abramson, Seligman, and Teasdale's reformulated learned helplessness model; provided the theoretical foundation for Dweck's implicit theory of intelligence research.
>
> **Key Works:** *An Attributional Theory of Achievement Motivation and Emotion* (1985); *An Attributional Theory of Motivation and Emotion* (1986); *Judgments of Responsibility: A Foundation for a Theory of Social Conduct* (1995).
>
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** The emotion-mediation framework developed in his 1986 book is the specific theoretical contribution that this analysis excavates and applies.
> *— [[attribution-theory-focused-analysis-2026-03-24_report]]*

> [!person] Bernard Weiner (b. 1935)
> **Core Contribution:** Attribution theory's three-dimensional structure (locus × stability × controllability) applied to academic motivation. Identified that effort attributions (internal, unstable, controllable) produce more adaptive motivation outcomes than ability attributions (internal, stable, uncontrollable) following failure.
>
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Weiner extended Heider's naive psychology of attribution and Rotter's locus of control into a multi-dimensional framework; Dweck's implicit theory of intelligence work can be understood as a dispositional account of individual differences in attribution tendency that Weiner's framework predicts but does not explain; Zimmerman incorporated Weiner's framework as the mechanism governing the self-reflection phase's self-judgment sub-process.
>
> **Key Works:** *An Attributional Theory of Achievement Motivation and Emotion* (1985); *Judgments of Responsibility* (1995).
>
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** Weiner's framework provides the formal structure for the adaptive/defensive inference fork analyzed as the cycle's primary regulatory valve in Phase IV.
