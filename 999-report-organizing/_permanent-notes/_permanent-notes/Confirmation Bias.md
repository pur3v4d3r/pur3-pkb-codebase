# Confirmation Bias

> [!definition] Confirmation Bias
> **Term:** Confirmation Bias (Wason, 1960; Nickerson, 1998)
> **Definition:** The tendency to search for, interpret, favour, and recall information in ways that confirm or support one's prior beliefs or hypotheses. Operates in three forms: biased information search, biased interpretation of evidence, and biased memory retrieval.
> **Boundary conditions:** Not always irrational — in stable, high-validity environments, prior expectations are often reliable guides. Becomes problematic when applied to contested, uncertain, or identity-relevant claims.
> **Report significance:** Identified as the paradigmatic cognitive bias that CT is designed to counteract (Section 4).
> **See also:** [[cognitive-bias]], [[motivated-reasoning]], [[myside-bias]], [[belief-bias]]

> [!evidence] Confirmation Bias
> The systematic tendency to seek, notice, weight, and remember information that confirms existing beliefs, while neglecting, dismissing, or underweighting disconfirming information. Confirmation bias is the primary psychological mechanism by which fallibilism is violated in practice — not through explicit philosophical commitment to infallibilism but through motivated asymmetric processing of evidence. First formally identified in Peter Wason's selection task experiments (1960s).

> [!evidence] Confirmation Bias
> The systematic tendency to seek, notice, interpret, and remember evidence that confirms existing beliefs, while discounting or ignoring disconfirming evidence. One of the most robust and well-studied cognitive biases; documented across populations, domains, and experimental paradigms by Peter Wason, Raymond Nickerson, and many others. Represents a fundamental distortion of the Bayesian norm of symmetric updating. A major threat to productive inductive reasoning in both scientific and educational contexts.
> *— [[inductive-reasoning-foundational-report-2026-03-06_report]]*

> [!evidence] Confirmation Bias
> A family of related cognitive tendencies — operating at the stages of information exposure, interpretation, and memory encoding — that favor processing and retention of information consistent with existing beliefs. In political contexts, confirmation bias is amplified by identity-protective motivation and by high-choice information environments that enable selective exposure. Importantly, confirmation bias does not typically involve conscious deception or motivated distortion; it operates largely below the threshold of awareness.
> *— [[political-reasoning-decision-making-foundational-report-2026-03-06_report]]*

> [!evidence] Confirmation Bias
> The well-documented tendency to seek, interpret, and recall information in ways that confirm one's preexisting beliefs or hypotheses. Represents a systematic distortion of inductive reasoning, because it biases sampling toward confirming instances and away from disconfirming counterexamples. Identified by [[peter-wason|Peter Wason]] in the 2-4-6 task (1960) and subsequently replicated extensively. A key target of critical thinking instruction.
> *— [[types-of-reasoning-foundational-report-2026-03-05_report]]*
