> **Q:** Why are PAP goal effects context-moderated rather than uniformly adaptive or maladaptive?
> **A:** PAP goals combine approach-mode regulation (adaptive) with a normative competence standard (fragile). Under conditions of competitive threat, low perceived competence, or repeated relative failure, the approach orientation can convert toward avoidance dynamics (goal conversion) — producing PAV-like cognitive and behavioral signatures. The approach component drives the initial benefits; the normative standard introduces context-dependent fragility.
> **Source:** Phase III, PAP Evidence; Phase IV, Goal Conversion
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Distinction

> **Q:** What is the Stability-Expectancy Lock, and what makes it "self-sealing"?
> **A:** The Stability-Expectancy Lock is the mechanism by which stable attributions for failure generate expectancy deficits, which reduce effort investment, which produces actual performance deficits, which appear to confirm the original stable attribution. It is self-sealing because the attribution generates behavioral evidence that supports itself.
> **Source:** Phase IV, Section 4.1

> **Q:** Why does the Hattie-Timperley finding that self-regulation-level feedback outperforms task-level feedback support the MCM over the ITM?
> **A:** The ITM predicts that feedback content quality is the primary variable; it cannot explain why feedback that builds monitoring infrastructure (self-regulation level) produces larger gains than feedback that delivers better information (task level). The MCM predicts exactly this: gains come from building the monitoring system's accuracy, not from maximizing information delivered to a monitoring system that may not be accurately tracking the gap.
> **Source:** Phase III, Hattie & Timperley evidence review
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection
> **Tags:** #hattie-timperley, #feedback-levels, #monitoring-infrastructure

> **Q:** What is the scaffolding-withdrawal model of habit formation, and what does it predict about motivational engagement over the formation period?
> **A:** The scaffolding-withdrawal model holds that the goal-directed system enables initial behavior execution and must progressively cede control to the habit system as context-action encoding strengthens. It predicts that sustained motivational engagement during execution — past the early formation period — may delay automaticity by keeping the behavior in goal-directed mode. The goal-directed system should be working itself out of a job.
> **Source:** Phase VI (original synthesis)
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Application

> **Q:** What is the regulatory language spectrum and how does it map onto SDT's internalization continuum?
> **A:** Introjected regulatory language ("I have to or I'll feel bad") → Identified ("I want to because this matters for my goals") → Integrated ("this is what I do as a learner"). > **Q:** Why does mastery goal orientation predict deeper cognitive strategy use?
>
> **A:** The mechanism runs through threat interpretation: mastery-oriented learners interpret monitoring signals (comprehension failure) as information about strategy effectiveness rather than information about ability. This prevents defensive affect regulation from crowding out cognitive regulation, keeping cognitive strategy adjustment available. Performance-oriented learners interpret the same signals as threatening, activating affect regulation at the expense of strategy adaptation.
>

> **Q:** What is the Schema-Timing Paradox revealed by Bransford and Johnson's research?
>
> **A:** Schemas must be active *during* comprehension to organize incoming information. But building a schema requires comprehending material. The paradox: to comprehend material well enough to build a schema from it, you need a schema — but you're trying to build one because you don't have one. A schema provided post-hoc cannot retroactively organize already-processed material.
>
> **Source:** Phase III
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Process

> **Q**: What is Bartlett's original claim, and how was it changed in the formalization tradition?
> **A**: Bartlett's original claim was that memory is reconstructive and culturally guided — shaped by culturally specific meaning systems. In the formalization (Rumelhart & Ortony), this was transformed into the claim that memory is mediated by abstract, amodal, culturally neutral data structures. The cultural specificity — central to Bartlett's evidence — was stripped in pursuit of cognitive universalism.
> **Source**: Phase III, Critical Challenge
> **Difficulty**: Intermediate
> **Type**: Historical-Conceptual

> [!flashcard] Seed 4
> **Q:** Why is the fluency illusion a monitoring failure, and why does the received view reinforce rather than correct it?
>
> **A:** The fluency illusion is a monitoring failure because smooth, effortless processing of surface code and text base generates subjective comprehension confidence that does not accurately reflect situation model quality. Readers feel like they understand when they don't, because the processing that produces the fluency feeling (decoding, syntactic parsing, local semantic integration) succeeds even when situation model construction fails. The received view reinforces it by teaching readers that fluent sentence-level processing is the goal — which validates the fluency signal as a comprehension indicator rather than flagging it as a potentially misleading proxy.
>
> **Source:** Phase III, Practical Challenge
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Process
> **Tags:** #fluency-illusion #metacognitive-monitoring #text-base-trap

> **Q:** Why is a process goal more protective of self-efficacy during difficulty than an outcome goal?
> **A:** Because a process goal specifies behaviors under the learner's control — the learner can succeed at the process goal even when comprehension outcome is incomplete. Outcome goals create a risk of failure that is not fully controllable, while process goals preserve an achievable evaluation standard even on difficult sessions.

> [!flashcard] Seed 4
> **Q:** What is the "cognitive transparency assumption" and what is its role in the Zimmerman cycle?
>
> **A:** The cognitive transparency assumption is the tacit claim that learners have sufficiently accurate introspective access to their own cognitive states to support effective regulation. It is embedded in the cycle's architecture: Forethought Phase self-efficacy appraisal assumes accurate competence assessment; Performance Phase self-monitoring assumes accurate awareness of cognitive state; Self-Reflection Phase attribution assumes accurate causal inference. Research on the fluency illusion and illusion of knowing demonstrates that this access is often systematically inaccurate, particularly for novel material in novices.
>
> **Source:** Phase III–IV
>

>
