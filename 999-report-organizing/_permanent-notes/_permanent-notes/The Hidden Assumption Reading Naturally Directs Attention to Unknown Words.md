## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] The Hidden Assumption: Reading Naturally Directs Attention to Unknown Words
> **The assumption**: When a reader encounters an unfamiliar word in text, reading automatically triggers a processing event that results in some degree of vocabulary acquisition. The word is noticed, contextual inference is attempted, meaning is constructed, and encoding occurs.
>
> **Why it's hidden**: This assumption is so intuitive — surely we notice words we don't know — that it is rarely made explicit in the reading-for-vocabulary literature. It underlies the acquisition rate estimates (e.g., 5–15% per encounter) as their baseline.
>
> **What happens if it's wrong**: Skilled readers routinely *skip* unknown words without conscious processing. The strategies that make reading fluent — top-down inference, semantic prediction, tolerance of lexical uncertainty — also systematically prevent the effortful processing that vocabulary acquisition requires. Proficient readers trained to read for comprehension do not naturally slow down at unknown words; they fill in semantically plausible values and continue. This means the actual rate of *attended* word encounters may be much lower than the acquisition rate literature assumes.
>
> **Assessment**: This assumption partially fails. Research on reading strategies (Nassaji, 2004) confirms that readers frequently do not stop to process unknown words and that comprehension-oriented reading is systematically less conducive to vocabulary acquisition than attention-oriented reading. The assumption survives only in conditions where readers are explicitly instructed to attend to vocabulary — which transforms incidental into intentional learning.
