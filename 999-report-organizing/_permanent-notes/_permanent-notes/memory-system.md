# Memory System

> [!definition] **Memory System** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> A memory system is a functionally and neurologically distinct set of processes and representations that stores, organizes, and retrieves information by different mechanisms, supports different types of knowledge, and dissociates from other systems under conditions of amnesia, development, or neurological damage. The term was systematized by [[endel-tulving]] (1985), who argued that memory systems differ in their brain structures, acquisition procedures, representational formats, and retrieval mechanisms — not merely in content.
> 
> **Boundary:** The term does not mean simply "a type of memory content" (e.g., "memories of people" is not a memory system). A system is defined by its computational architecture and neural implementation, not its subject matter.
> 
> **See also:** [[working-memory]], [[long-term-memory]], [[declarative-memory]], [[episodic-memory]], [[semantic-memory]], [[procedural-memory]]

## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> The foundational paper proposing the multi-store model of memory: sensory register → short-term store → long-term store. While subsequently superseded by the working memory model and multiple-systems frameworks, this paper defined the research agenda of cognitive memory research for two decades. *Recommended for:* Section 1 (historical foundations), Section 3 (working memory origins).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> The landmark paper replacing the unitary short-term store with the multicomponent working memory model. Reports the dual-task experiments showing that concurrent memory load impairs reasoning less than expected, implying separate verbal and reasoning subsystems. *Recommended for:* Section 3 (working memory model).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> Classic monograph demonstrating schema-guided reconstruction in memory through serial reproduction experiments with culturally unfamiliar materials. Established the constructive, adaptive conception of memory that anticipates all subsequent work on reconstructive memory, misinformation, and schema effects. *Recommended for:* Section 6 (reconstructive memory), Section 4 (LTM organization).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> Proposed the levels-of-processing framework as replacement for multi-store models, arguing that retention is a function of depth of analysis during encoding rather than residence in a memory store. Spawned thousands of studies and transformed the understanding of encoding. *Recommended for:* Section 5 (encoding).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> The founding text of experimental memory research: demonstrates the forgetting curve, spacing effect, savings in relearning, and serial position effects through systematic self-experimentation. *Recommended for:* Section 6 (forgetting curve), Section 1 (historical foundations).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> The classic misinformation experiment showing that verb choice in a post-event question ("smashed" vs. "contacted") alters estimated speed and subsequent memory details, establishing the misinformation effect. *Recommended for:* Section 6 (misinformation).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> Landmark study demonstrating that retrieval practice (testing) produces dramatically better long-term retention (~80% recall) than repeated study (~36% recall), even when participants studying had more exposure time. *Recommended for:* Section 8 (applications, testing effect).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> Classic paper establishing the ~7-item capacity limit of short-term memory across modalities, introduced the concept of chunking as the unit of capacity limitation, and connected information theory to cognitive limits. *Recommended for:* Section 3 (capacity).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> Comprehensive quantitative review of the spacing effect literature across hundreds of studies, establishing the distributed practice advantage in verbal learning tasks and providing parameters for optimal gap-to-retention-interval ratios. *Recommended for:* Section 8 (spaced repetition).

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*

## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Hermann Ebbinghaus (1850-1909)** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> German experimental psychologist who pioneered the quantitative study of memory. His self-experimental work (*Über das Gedächtnis*, 1885) established the forgetting curve, the spacing effect, and the method of savings in relearning. Ebbinghaus introduced the concept of the *nonsense syllable* as a controlled learning unit and invented the practice of learning-to-criterion paradigms. His empirical empiricism preceded the cognitive revolution by 70 years but established the quantitative, experimental character of memory research. Related figures: [[william-james]] (contemporary theorist),…

> [!person] **Frederic Bartlett (1886-1969)** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> British psychologist whose *Remembering* (1932) demonstrated the constructive, schema-guided nature of memory through serial reproduction experiments. Bartlett challenged Ebbinghaus's decontextualized, atomistic approach and proposed that memory serves adaptive social and cognitive functions, not mere storage. His work was largely ignored for four decades, then recovered and became foundational to cognitive psychology in the schema-theoretic tradition. Related figures: [[endel-tulving]], [[Elizabeth-Loftus]].

> [!person] **Alan Baddeley (1934–)** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> British cognitive psychologist who, with Graham Hitch, proposed the multicomponent working memory model (1974) that replaced the Atkinson-Shiffrin short-term memory store. Baddeley's subsequent four decades of work elaborated each component of the model, added the episodic buffer (2000), and integrated the model with neuropsychological evidence. His contributions defined the research program of working memory for five decades. Related figure: [[graham-hitch]], [[Nelson-Cowan]] (alternative models).

> [!person] **Endel Tulving (1927–)** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> Estonian-Canadian cognitive psychologist at the University of Toronto who proposed the episodic-semantic memory distinction (1972), developed the encoding specificity principle with Donald Thomson (1973), introduced the concept of autonoetic consciousness as the phenomenological mark of episodic memory, and conducted foundational work on the neuroscience of episodic memory. Tulving's theoretical innovations — establishing the multiple memory systems framework for declarative memory — are arguably the most influential contributions to LTM theory in the twentieth century. > [!person] **Elizabeth Loftus (1944–)** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What are the four components of Baddeley's Working Memory Model (most recent version)?
**A:** (1) Phonological Loop (verbal/phonological storage and rehearsal); (2) Visuospatial Sketchpad (visual and spatial maintenance/manipulation); (3) Central Executive (attentional controller — updating, shifting, inhibiting); (4) Episodic Buffer (multimodal integrator with LTM, added 2000).
**Source:** Section 3
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** working-memory, baddeley-model, cognitive-architecture

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** Distinguish Tulving's *episodic* from *semantic* memory. What is the key phenomenological marker Tulving used to define the distinction?
**A:** *Episodic memory* stores personally experienced events in their spatiotemporal context, enabling "mental time travel" — the subjective re-experiencing of past events (autonoetic consciousness). *Semantic memory* stores general world knowledge without personal temporal context (noetic consciousness — knowing about, not remembering experiencing). Tulving used *autonoetic consciousness* (self-aware consciousness of one's personal past) as the defining phenomenological marker of episodic memory.
**Source:** Section 4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** long-term-memory, tulving, episodic-memory, semantic-memory, consciousness

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the misinformation effect (Loftus)? Give one example from the original research.
**A:** The misinformation effect is the incorporation of post-event information (leading questions, misleading suggestions, others' accounts) into the memory of the original event, subsequently recalled as genuine. Example: participants shown a car accident video and asked "How fast were the cars going when they *smashed* into each other?" reported higher speeds and more often reported seeing broken glass than those asked about "contact" — though no broken glass was present.
**Source:** Section 6
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** misinformation-effect, reconstructive-memory, elizabeth-loftus, eyewitness-testimony

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What does "encoding specificity" predict about memory retrieval?
**A:** The Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973) predicts that retrieval success is greatest when retrieval cues match the context present at encoding. The memory trace encodes both the target item and the encoding context (environmental cues, internal state, associated stimuli). Reinstating encoding context at retrieval facilitates recall; mismatching context impairs it.
**Source:** Section 5
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** encoding-specificity, retrieval, tulving, context-dependent-memory

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is reconsolidation, and why does it matter for both memory theory and clinical practice?
**A:** Reconsolidation is the process by which a retrieved (reactivated) memory returns to a brief labile state (protein-synthesis dependent) before being re-stabilized. It challenges the once-consolidated-always-stable view of LTM and provides a cellular mechanism for memory updating. Clinically: it suggests that reactivating traumatic memories in safe contexts with new, non-fear-associated information may allow the fear-associated elements to be updated — a basis for reconsolidation-based exposure therapies and pharmacological interventions (e.g., propranolol) in PTSD.
**Source:** Section 7
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** reconsolidation, memory-consolidation, PTSD, clinical-memory

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** Compare Cowan's and Miller's capacity estimates for working memory. What explains the difference?
**A:** Miller (1956) estimated short-term memory capacity at 7 ± 2 *chunks*. Cowan (2001) re-analyzed the literature and argued the true capacity of the *focus of attention* is approximately 4 ± 1 *chunks*. The difference is explained by the contribution of the phonological rehearsal loop: Miller's higher estimates partially reflected phonological rehearsal support (cycling items through the inner voice), which inflates apparent visual or attentional capacity. Cowan's estimate isolates the central attentional capacity without rehearsal's contribution.
**Source:** Section 3
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** working-memory-capacity, miller, cowan, chunks, phonological-loop

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What are "desirable difficulties" (Bjork)? Give three examples and explain why they improve learning despite feeling harder.
**A:** Desirable difficulties are learning conditions that slow or impair *acquisition performance* while substantially improving *long-term retention and transfer*. Three examples: (1) *Spaced practice:* distributing study over time rather than massing it; (2) *Interleaving:* mixing problem types rather than blocking by type; (3) *Retrieval practice:* testing recall rather than re-reading. They improve learning because: effortful retrieval strengthens the memory trace more than fluent access; spacing allows partial forgetting that makes each review a genuine retrieval challenge (triggering reconsolidation); interleaving requires discrimination learning between problem types, building more flexible schemas.
**Source:** Section 5
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** desirable-difficulties, spacing-effect, testing-effect, retrieval-practice, bjork

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the "expertise reversal effect" in Cognitive Load Theory, and what does it imply about instructional design?
**A:** The expertise reversal effect (Sweller): instructional techniques effective for novices become redundant or even detrimental for experts. Specifically, the worked example effect — novices learn better from worked examples than from unguided problem-solving — reverses for experts, who are better served by problem-solving that extends their schemas. The implication: instructional design must be adaptive to learner expertise level; a single fixed instructional format will be suboptimal for at least one expertise level. Novices need scaffolded examples to reduce [[element-interactivity|element interactivity]]; experts need problems to challenge their existing schemas.
**Source:** Section 8
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** cognitive-load-theory, expertise-reversal, worked-examples, instructional-design

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What role does the amygdala play in memory, and how does this explain the enhanced memorability of emotional events?
**A:** The amygdala modulates memory consolidation for emotionally significant events by enhancing hippocampal consolidation during high-arousal states. Stress hormones (norepinephrine, cortisol) released during emotional experiences act on the amygdala, which in turn strengthens hippocampal long-term potentiation during post-encoding consolidation. This produces the well-documented emotional memory enhancement — emotionally arousing events are remembered more vividly and durably than neutral events of equal objective importance — and also the enhanced intrusiveness and traumatic re-experiencing that characterize fear memory in PTSD.
**Source:** Section 4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** amygdala, emotional-memory, hippocampus, PTSD, memory-consolidation
