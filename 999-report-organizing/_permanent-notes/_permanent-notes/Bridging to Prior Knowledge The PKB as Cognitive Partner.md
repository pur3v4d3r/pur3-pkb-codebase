## Additional Material

> [!schema-activation] Bridging to Prior Knowledge: The PKB as Cognitive Partner
> Consider the familiar experience of returning to notes taken months earlier and finding them opaque — the words recognizable, the structure intact, yet the understanding stubbornly absent. This common frustration reveals something fundamental about the relationship between [[knowledge-representation|knowledge representation]] and [[encoding|encoding]]: the act of storing information and the act of learning information are not the same process, and no amount of organizational elegance can substitute for the cognitive work that genuine understanding demands. The research traditions explored in this report — from Sweller's [[cognitive-load-theory|cognitive load theory]] to Bjork's [[desirable-difficulties|desirable difficulties]] framework to Clark and Chalmers' [[extended-mind-theory|extended mind thesis]] — converge on a deceptively simple insight: the structures through which knowledge is organized profoundly shape whether and how that knowledge can be retrieved, connected, and applied. A PKB built on principles derived from [[cognitive-architecture|cognitive architecture]] research does not merely store what has been learned; it participates in the learning itself, creating conditions under which [[retrieval-practice|retrieval]] strengthens memory traces, [[elaboration|elaboration]] deepens understanding, and [[schema-construction|schema construction]] proceeds through the architectural affordances of the system rather than despite them.
>
> **Guiding Question:** How can the architectural design decisions of a personal knowledge base — its linking structures, note formats, review systems, and organizational patterns — be grounded in empirically validated cognitive strategies to transform the PKB from a passive repository into an active cognitive partner?
