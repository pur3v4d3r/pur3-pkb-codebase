> [!open-question] Perspective Switch
> Set aside the cognitive efficiency lens of CLT for a moment. Adopt instead the perspective of a motivational theorist — someone who studies how interest, challenge, agency, and competence need satisfaction affect learning. From this vantage point, what does "optimal cognitive challenge" look like? Flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi) suggests that engagement is highest when challenge slightly exceeds current competence — a condition that would be characterized as elevated load in CLT terms. What features of learning become prominent from this perspective that were invisible from the CLT perspective? What questions arise that CLT cannot ask?

Not abstract slot-filling, but: which motor programs are partially activated? Which perceptual simulations are run? Whose body, whose sensory history, whose situational affordances are recruited? > [!open-question] Perspective Switch
> Set aside SDT's three-need framework entirely. From the perspective of [[achievement-goal-theory]], what does "autonomous motivation" look like? Achievement goal theory would analyze the same behavioral data in terms of mastery vs. performance goal orientations — whether learners are focused on developing competence or on demonstrating it relative to others. The two frameworks make overlapping but not identical predictions. What would a classroom environment need to look like to be simultaneously autonomy-supportive (SDT) and mastery-goal-promoting (AGT)? Where do the prescriptions converge, and where do they pull in different directions? Practicing this theoretical perspective-switch is not about choosing a winner — it is about noticing what each framework makes visible that the other obscures.
> *— [[self-determination-theory-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-19_report]]*

> [!open-question] Perspective Switch
> Set aside the Zimmerman cycle entirely for a moment. Adopt the perspective of Philip Winne and Allyson Hadwin's [[Winne-&-Hadwin's-Metacognitive-Processing-Model|COPES model]], which treats SRL not as a sequence of phases but as a set of *conditional, event-driven* cognitive operations that respond to task conditions (C), cognitive operations (O), products (P), evaluations (E), and standards (S). From this vantage point, learning regulation is not cyclical — it is responsive. Regulation occurs not on a schedule but when specific conditions are met: when a discrepancy is detected between current performance and a standard, regulation is triggered. What does the Zimmerman cycle look like from inside the COPES framework? What features of SRL become prominent that were invisible before? What questions arise that the Zimmerman cycle cannot ask?
