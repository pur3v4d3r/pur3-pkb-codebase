> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** Explain how goal framing vocabulary affects comprehension monitoring criteria — specifically how mastery-approach vs. performance-avoidance goal language changes what "counts" as successful comprehension.
> **A:** Goal vocabulary establishes the criterion against which monitoring is calibrated. Mastery-approach goal language ("I want to understand the mechanism") sets a demanding criterion (genuine mechanistic understanding); performance-avoidance language ("I need to get through this") sets a surface criterion (completing the reading). When the criterion is surface, genuine comprehension failures go undetected and corrective re-reading is never triggered — producing the appearance of having read without the substance of comprehension.
> **Source:** Phase IV
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Tags:** #comprehension-monitoring #achievement-goals #metacognition #reading

> **Q:** How does the concept of cognitive disequilibrium connect to the desirable difficulties literature?
> **A:** Both predict that productive cognitive challenge — assimilation failure that requires schema revision — produces deeper and more durable learning than smooth, effortless processing. Desirable difficulties (spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice) create the conditions for effortful engagement and schema revisions that constructivism proposes are the mechanism of genuine learning.

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** Why is EI described as complementary rather than competitive with retrieval practice? At what points in the learning cycle does each strategy operate?
> **A:** EI primarily operates during *initial encoding* — it deepens and enriches the quality of the initial memory trace by integrating new propositions into the prior knowledge network. Retrieval practice primarily operates during *consolidation* — strengthening already-encoded information through active reconstruction against resistance. Combining EI (for deep initial encoding) with subsequent spaced retrieval practice (for consolidation) should outperform either strategy alone.
> **Source:** Phase V
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Tags:** #retrieval-practice, #elaborative-interrogation, #learning-sequence

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** How does introjection function as a motivational hazard in self-directed PKM practice?
> **A:** Introjection is partially internalised regulation driven by guilt, anxiety, or self-worth contingency rather than genuine valuing. In PKM, it manifests when self-imposed assessment triggers self-punishment ("I *should* have finished this") rather than informational processing ("What does this difficulty tell me about my strategy?"). Assessment structures must be designed to function informationally (supporting competence) rather than controllingly (triggering introjection).
> **Source:** Section 2, 7
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Tags:** #introjection #sdt #self-assessment #pkm-hazards

> **Q:** How does the Dunning-Kruger effect relate to Nelson-Narens' monitoring-control architecture?
> **A:** The Dunning-Kruger effect — low-competence individuals' greater over-confidence — is a direct instantiation of monitoring failure in the Nelson-Narens framework. Low domain knowledge impairs both object-level performance and meta-level monitoring accuracy (because the knowledge required to detect performance failures is the same domain knowledge lacking in poor performers). The meta-level's model of object-level performance is thus doubly impaired.

> **Q:** Why is conditional metacognitive knowledge the most domain-specific dimension of the taxonomy, and what are the implications for transfer?
> **A:** Conditional knowledge (knowing when and why a strategy is effective) requires knowledge of the specific task structures, epistemic conventions, and performance standards of a domain to be genuinely functional. Generic conditions (e.g., "use elaborative interrogation for expository text") are only marginally functional — truly conditional knowledge specifies conditions at the level of text genre, comprehension goal, prior knowledge threshold, and task demand. Transfer of conditional metacognitive knowledge across domains is poor because these conditions are domain-indexed.
> **Source:** Phase III.6
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Tags:** #conditional-metacognitive-knowledge #domain-specificity #transfer #metacognitive-knowledge

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** How does Efklides' MASRL model extend the Nelson-Narens framework?
> **A:** The Nelson-Narens model treats monitoring and control in largely cognitive-computational terms. Efklides' MASRL model foregrounds the phenomenological and affective dimension: metacognitive experiences (feeling of difficulty, feeling of knowing, confidence) are constitutive elements of the monitoring process that carry motivational valence. The same feeling of difficulty can motivate increased effort or trigger avoidance depending on goal orientation and self-efficacy, meaning that metacognitive regulation is motivationally conditioned — not merely cognitively determined.
> **Source:** Phase III
> **Difficulty:** Advanced

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** How does the Coverage-Efficiency Threshold in vocabulary acquisition connect to Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development? What does each concept add to the other?
>
> **A:** Vygotsky's ZPD specifies that optimal learning occurs at the edge of current competence — in the zone between independent performance and performance requiring support. The Coverage Threshold is a precise quantification of the vocabulary-learning ZPD: at 98% coverage, the reader is operating at exactly the edge where support (available cognitive slack + context inference) can convert challenge (unfamiliar words) into learning. Below 98%, the learner is beyond the ZPD — challenge without the cognitive support to process it. Above 99%, the learner is below the ZPD — no meaningful challenge. The Coverage Threshold adds quantitative precision to Vygotsky's concept for this domain; the ZPD adds developmental context to the threshold concept, suggesting that the threshold itself shifts as reading skill and vocabulary develop.
>
> **Source:** Phase III, analytical insight on optimal challenge zone; Phase IV
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection
> **Tags:** #zone-of-proximal-development, #vygotsky, #coverage-threshold, #learning-theory

> **Q:** How does the Frame-Before-Fill principle operate in software architecture design, and what does this analogy illuminate about reading comprehension?
>
> **A:** In software architecture, developers who begin implementing components without first establishing a system-level architecture face the software equivalent of the textbase trap: working components that don't cohere into a working system. The architectural frame (system diagram, dependency map) gives component implementations a structural position — just as a pre-reading structural frame gives text propositions a structural position. The analogy illuminates that Frame-Before-Fill is a domain-independent cognitive construction principle, not a reading-specific technique. It applies wherever complex mental models must be built from detailed inputs.
>

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** How does the Nelson-Narens monitoring-control model illuminate the relationship between the Forethought and Self-Reflection phases?
> **A:** Both phases are meta-level operations: Forethought is prospective metacognitive control (the meta level issuing goals and strategic instructions to the object level before performance) and Self-Reflection is retrospective monitoring followed by prospective control adjustment (the meta level assessing what happened at the object level and recalibrating future instructions). The phases are the two active regulatory moments of the monitoring-control cycle.
> **Source:** Phase V
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Tags:** #metacognition, #nelson-narens, #monitoring-control, #srl

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** How does the Threshold-Exposure Principle transfer to concept learning in science education, and what would it predict about passive lecture attendance?
>
> **A:** Transfer: in both vocabulary learning and science concept learning, passive exposure to the target item (hearing a term in a lecture, encountering a word in text) produces encoding at just sufficient depth for the immediate comprehension goal. This shallow processing does not cross the encoding threshold for durable representation. For lecture attendance specifically, the principle predicts that hearing a concept explained — even with attention and good notes — will produce poor retention compared to contexts that force elaborative processing (explaining the concept in one's own words, applying it to a novel example, generating a prediction). Exposure frequency is not the bottleneck; processing depth is.
>
> **Source:** Phase VII
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection

> [!flashcard] Seed 10 — Connection
> **Q:** What is morphological awareness and why is it a "force multiplier" on vocabulary breadth?
> **A:** Morphological awareness is knowledge of word-part structure (roots, prefixes, suffixes) and the ability to use it for meaning inference. It's a force multiplier because each morpheme learned enables probabilistic inference across all words sharing that morpheme — learning the Latin root *-rupt* (break) helps with rupture, disrupt, erupt, irrupt, corrupt, interrupt, bankrupt, and others simultaneously.

> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
