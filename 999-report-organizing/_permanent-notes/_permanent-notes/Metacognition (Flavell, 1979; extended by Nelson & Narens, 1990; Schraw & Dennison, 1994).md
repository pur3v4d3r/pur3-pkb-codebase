# Metacognition (Flavell, 1979; extended by Nelson & Narens, 1990; Schraw & Dennison, 1994)

> [!definition] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979; extended by Nelson & Narens, 1990; Schraw & Dennison, 1994)
> Metacognition designates the class of cognitive processes in which cognition itself serves as the object of cognitive activity. It encompasses both the knowledge an individual possesses about cognition — including declarative knowledge about one's own cognitive characteristics and the demands of cognitive tasks, procedural knowledge about effective cognitive strategies, and conditional knowledge about when and why to deploy those strategies — and the regulatory processes through which that knowledge is applied to monitor, evaluate, and adjust ongoing cognitive engagement. Metacognition is distinguished from first-order cognition by its reflexive structure: it takes cognitive states, processes, and products as its domain, rather than the external world or domain content.
>
> **Boundary:** Metacognition does not encompass all cognitive monitoring or all self-awareness. Automatic monitoring processes that operate without deliberate engagement and are inaccessible to introspection are typically excluded from the metacognitive domain. Similarly, domain content knowledge — even highly elaborated expertise — is not metacognitive unless explicitly deployed in service of monitoring or regulating cognitive processes. The reflexive, deliberately accessible quality of metacognitive processes distinguishes them from other forms of cognitive self-reference.
>
> **Etymology:** From Greek *meta* (beyond, after, about) + Latin *cognitio* (the act of knowing). The *meta* prefix signals higher-order status: metacognition is cognition about cognition, as metaphysics is the philosophical study of physics' foundational assumptions.
>
> **Historical Development:** The term was coined by Flavell in the early 1970s as a technical label for what he was observing in memory development research — children's knowledge about memory. By 1979 he had extended it to encompass cognitive monitoring generally. Nelson and Narens (1990) then re-grounded it in formal information-theoretic architecture. Schraw and Dennison (1994) further operationalized it through the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory, enabling quantitative empirical study.
>
> **Common Misconception:** Metacognition is not simply self-reflection or general thoughtfulness. The construct has specific technical content: it refers to cognition that targets cognitive processes, not cognition that targets the self generally. Thinking about one's emotional state, for instance, is not metacognition unless the focus is specifically on the cognitive dimensions of that emotional processing. Similarly, metacognition is not always slow, explicit, or effortful — experienced practitioners often deploy metacognitive strategies with considerable fluency and automaticity.
>
