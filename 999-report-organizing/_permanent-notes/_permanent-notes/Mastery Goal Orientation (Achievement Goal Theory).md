# Mastery Goal Orientation (Achievement Goal Theory)

> [!definition] Mastery Goal Orientation (Achievement Goal Theory)
> An achievement goal orientation characterized by the reader's primary concern with developing genuine understanding, expanding competence, and mastering the task domain, with success defined as personal improvement and comprehension depth rather than normative performance. Mastery orientation predicts deep processing, persistence under difficulty, use of elaborative and organizational strategies, and positive attribution of failures to strategic rather than ability causes.
>
> **Boundary:** Mastery goal orientation describes the *type* of goal pursued, not the intensity of pursuit. A reader can pursue mastery goals half-heartedly (mastery orientation, low motivation quantity) or pursue performance goals intensively (performance orientation, high motivation quantity). Both dimensions matter independently.
>
> **See also:** [[achievement-goal-theory]], [[mastery-goal-orientation]], [[growth-mindset]], [[intrinsic-motivation]]

> [!evidence] Mastery Goal Orientation (Achievement Goal Theory)
> **Definition:** A motivational orientation toward learning tasks in which success is defined by competence development, understanding, and personal improvement — contrasted with performance goal orientation (success defined by outperforming others or avoiding appearing incompetent). Within Pintrich's framework, mastery orientation characterizes the motivational area of the Forethought Phase and predicts deeper cognitive strategy use through the threat-reappraisal mechanism.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Mastery orientation is the motivational variable with the most consistent relationship to deep cognitive processing in the SRL literature, operating through the mechanism described in Phase IV: mastery-oriented learners interpret monitoring signals as informational rather than threatening.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Task choice that prioritizes challenge over ease; persistence through difficulty; interpretations of failure as strategy failure rather than ability failure.
