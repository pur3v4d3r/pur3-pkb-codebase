## Additional Material

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Working memory's capacity of approximately four independent items constitutes the fundamental bottleneck of human cognition — not a flaw but a structural feature.
> - Chunking is the mind's primary adaptive response to this constraint, compressing multiple elements into single representational units.
> - Expertise arises not from expanded working memory but from the accumulation of structured chunks that allow vastly more information to be processed within the same narrow bandwidth.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Chunks are structured representational units bound by meaningful relations, not mere groupings; they possess internal coherence, hierarchical organization, and associative richness.
> - Template theory refined early chunk models by introducing variable slots, bridging chunking with [[schema-theory|schema theory]] and accounting for expert flexibility in novel situations.
> - Chunks nest hierarchically, and the depth of this nesting — not merely the number of chunks — distinguishes experts from novices.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Expert-novice differences are not differences in working memory capacity but differences in knowledge organization — specifically, in the number, size, depth, and interconnection of domain-specific chunks.
> - Chunk development progresses through qualitatively distinct stages, from element-by-element processing (novice) to holistic pattern recognition with automatic response selection (expert).
> - As chunks become more deeply integrated, they become more difficult to articulate — a paradox that has significant implications for teaching and knowledge transfer.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Schemas are higher-order organizational frameworks constructed from individual chunks; they go beyond pattern recognition to provide interpretive and decision-making structures for entire categories of situations.
> - [[schema-automation|Schema automation]] frees working memory resources, creating a self-reinforcing cycle in which each increment of automation opens cognitive space for further learning.
> - [[cognitive-load-theory|Cognitive load theory]] provides the instructional framework: effective teaching manages intrinsic load, minimizes extraneous load, and maximizes germane load in ways that must be calibrated to the learner's current level of chunk and schema development.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Expert pattern recognition, as described by the [[Recognition-Primed-Decision-Model|RPD model]], depends on the automatic activation of richly annotated chunks that encode situations, expectations, goals, and appropriate responses as integrated packages.
> - Expert intuition is the subjective experience of this automated process — not mystical but also not fully articulable, due to the same automation that makes it rapid and effortful.
> - The accuracy of expert intuition is bounded by the representativeness of the chunk library, creating vulnerabilities when novel situations superficially resemble familiar ones.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - [[deliberate-practice|Deliberate practice]] is the developmental mechanism through which high-quality chunks are constructed; it is distinguished from routine experience by its targeted goals, focused effort, informative feedback, and operation at the boundary of current ability.
> - Chunking can be understood as the unit currency of deliberate practice — each cycle producing new chunks, enriched existing chunks, or hierarchically integrated chunk structures.
> - Mental representations (internalized standards of excellence) serve as the schemas against which performance is evaluated, and feedback is the mechanism by which these representations are calibrated and chunks are refined.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - [[cognitive-load-theory|Cognitive load theory]] translates chunking and schema theory into actionable instructional design principles, centered on managing the demands placed on working memory during learning.
> - The [[worked-example-effect|worked example effect]] and [[expertise-reversal-effect|expertise reversal effect]] demonstrate that optimal instruction must be continuously calibrated to the learner's developing expertise — what helps novices can hinder experts.
> - The [[Four-Component-Instructional-Design|4C/ID model]] provides a comprehensive framework that manages the full novice-to-expert transition through scaffolded learning tasks, appropriately timed information, and targeted automation practice.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - The domain-specificity of chunks constitutes the most fundamental boundary of expertise: expert knowledge does not transfer readily across domains, and general cognitive training does not substitute for domain-specific chunk accumulation.
> - Individual differences in the rate and ceiling of chunk formation remain an open question, with deliberate practice accounting for substantial but not all variance in expert performance.
> - Neuroscience increasingly validates the chunking account, showing expertise-related changes in neural efficiency, brain structure, and functional connectivity that align with the progressive automation of domain-specific schemas.
> *— [[chunking-and-expertise-development-foundational-report-2026-04-15_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The intention-action gap is a real, empirically documented, and architecturally explicable phenomenon. It arises from failures at multiple points: the predecisional failure to cross the Rubicon into commitment; the absence of implementation plans that bind intentions to specific cues; competition from other intentions that crowd out effortful goal pursuit; motivational decay between intention formation and action opportunity; and insufficient [[self-efficacy]] to overcome initiation thresholds. Understanding this gap is the prerequisite for understanding why specific features of how goals are set and structured matter enormously.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Goal-Setting Theory establishes that goal *properties* — not simply the existence of goals — determine whether goals improve performance. Specifically, challenging and specific goals, when pursued with high commitment, produce higher performance than vague or easy goals, mediated by directing attention, mobilizing effort, sustaining persistence, and activating strategy. Feedback is a necessary co-condition, functioning through a discrepancy-monitoring loop that drives regulatory adjustment. These mechanisms map directly onto the cognitive architecture of [[self-regulated-learning]]: to understand how goals function within SRL, we need to trace how these mechanisms operate across Zimmerman's three regulatory phases.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Zimmerman's Cyclical SRL Model provides the structural framework within which Goal-Setting Theory mechanisms operate. In the Forethought phase, goal-setting and strategic planning establish the directional, effort-mobilizing, persistence-sustaining intent. In the Performance phase, self-control and self-observation operationalize those intents through real-time regulation. In the Self-Reflection phase, evaluation and attribution determine whether the cycle generates adaptive growth or maladaptive entrenchment. The recursive nature of the model explains why early SRL investments pay compound returns: each high-quality cycle builds the self-knowledge, strategic repertoire, and motivational beliefs that improve subsequent cycles.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Volitional control occupies the critical space between goal formation and goal attainment, protecting goal pursuit against the psychological forces — competing motivations, affective interference, decision fatigue, environmental distraction — that convert sincere intentions into failed follow-throughs. The key mechanisms are: Kuhl's action-vs-state orientation (managing attentional focus under difficulty), [[implementation-intention|implementation intentions]] (pre-specifying situational triggers to automatize goal-directed behavior), emotion regulation strategies (reappraisal to maintain access to goal-directed cognition under stress), and progressive habit formation (transferring volitional demands to automatized cue-response structures). These mechanisms are not alternatives; they function as complementary layers of a volitional architecture.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Metacognition constitutes the epistemic infrastructure of SRL: without accurate monitoring of one's own cognitive processes, the feedback loops that drive regulatory adaptation cannot close. The critical variable is not metacognitive sophistication in the abstract but monitoring *accuracy* — calibration between self-assessment and actual performance. The Dunning-Kruger problem reveals that novice learners face a metacognitive deficit that makes externally-sourced calibration (testing, expert feedback) a necessary complement to endogenous self-monitoring. Above monitoring, metacognitive regulation — planning, monitoring, evaluation — orchestrates the SRL cycle; in Pintrich's extended framework, this regulation extends beyond cognition to motivation, behavior, and context. Metacognitive capacity is trainable, and its development represents one of the highest-leverage investments in long-term learning.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Goal quality — the motivational architecture of what is being pursued and why — fundamentally moderates the effectiveness of goal-setting procedures. Achievement goal orientation (mastery vs. performance vs. avoidance) determines the quality of regulatory engagement with task demands and responses to difficulty. [[self-determination-theory]] identifies autonomous vs. controlled regulation as an independent moderator of engagement quality and wellbeing. Self-efficacy determines approach vs. avoidance behavior at the point of challenge. Optimal SRL requires goals that are simultaneously well-structured procedurally (specific, challenging, committed, with feedback) and well-constructed motivationally (mastery-oriented, autonomously regulated, efficacy-supportive). The absence of motivational quality degrades SRL outcomes even when procedural quality is high.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The integrated model of goal-setting and SRL identifies four interacting systems — goal architecture, SRL cycle quality, volitional protection, and motivational orientation — each necessary and none sufficient alone. Goal properties operate directly within each SRL phase: specificity determines monitoring precision in the Performance phase, challenge calibration drives the effort mechanism, and commitment determines Rubicon crossing in Forethought. Self-concordance addresses the long-horizon motivational problem: goals that are genuinely aligned with values sustain the motivational investment that procedural excellence alone cannot guarantee. The recursive self-referentiality of SRL development means that the process of learning to self-regulate is itself the best context for developing self-regulatory skill.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The translation of integrated SRL theory into practice requires five interacting design principles: goal architecture that is specifically structured, challenge-calibrated, and self-concordant; systematic implementation intentions that convert intentions to cue-triggered behaviors; externally sourced calibration that bypasses endogenous monitoring illusions; structured self-reflection practice with attributionally accurate prompts; and explicit motivational architecture that supports mastery orientation, autonomous regulation, and basic psychological need satisfaction. These principles function as a complete system: selective adoption without addressing the weakest component of the system will produce partial, unstable improvements rather than the compounding competence development that the full four-system architecture enables.
> *— [[goal-setting-and-self-regulated-learning-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Human memory comprises multiple, functionally distinct systems; long-term memory is the most expansive and consequential component.
> - The Atkinson-Shiffrin multi-store model established the foundational framework of sensory, short-term, and long-term stores, though its emphasis on rehearsal as the primary encoding mechanism has been superseded by deeper accounts of processing quality.
> - The asymmetry between working memory's limited capacity and long-term memory's unlimited capacity is the central architectural constraint of human cognition, forcing selectivity in encoding and underwriting the importance of schema-based expertise.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Long-term memory is not a single system but a family of functionally and neurologically distinct subsystems.
> - The most fundamental division is between declarative (explicit) memory — further divided into episodic and semantic — and non-declarative (implicit) memory, which includes procedural skills, priming, and conditioning.
> - Neuropsychological evidence, particularly from amnesic patients, provided the critical dissociations that established the multiple memory systems framework.
> - Episodic and semantic memory interact dynamically: episodic experiences serve as the raw material from which decontextualized semantic knowledge is gradually extracted.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Encoding is the constructive process that transforms experience into durable memory traces; its quality is determined by the depth, elaboration, and distinctiveness of the cognitive processing applied.
> - The levels-of-processing framework established that deep semantic processing produces superior retention to shallow perceptual processing, though transfer-appropriate processing refined this by showing that encoding-retrieval match matters as much as absolute depth.
> - The generation effect and desirable difficulties framework demonstrate that effortful, generative encoding enhances long-term retention even when it reduces immediate performance.
> - Dual coding theory shows that encoding in both verbal and imaginal formats creates redundant retrieval pathways.
> - The encoding specificity principle establishes that retrieval success depends on the match between encoding context and retrieval cues.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Consolidation transforms labile encoded traces into stable long-term representations through two levels of process: synaptic consolidation (minutes to hours, involving LTP and protein synthesis) and systems consolidation (weeks to years, involving hippocampal-neocortical dialogue).
> - The standard model of consolidation holds that memories gradually become hippocampally independent; the multiple trace theory challenges this by arguing that detailed episodic memories require permanent hippocampal support.
> - Reconsolidation demonstrates that retrieved memories re-enter a labile state, enabling update but also introducing vulnerability.
> - Sleep plays an active role in consolidation, with slow-wave sleep supporting hippocampal replay and systems consolidation, and REM sleep potentially supporting integration with existing knowledge.
> - Consolidation is not passive preservation but active transformation — a process that schematises, generalises, and integrates at the cost of episodic fidelity.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Memories are stored as distributed patterns of activation across neural networks, not as discrete records in fixed locations.
> - The organization of semantic knowledge has been modelled as hierarchical networks and, more successfully, as spreading activation networks with associative links of varying strength.
> - Schemas — organized knowledge structures representing typical features of experience categories — serve as the fundamental units of complex knowledge organization, guiding encoding, storage, and retrieval.
> - Schemas are both productive (enabling inference, comprehension, and efficient storage) and problematic (introducing systematic distortions through schema-consistent gap-filling).
> - Memory storage is better understood as an ongoing process of reconstruction than as a static state of preservation.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Retrieval is an active, cue-dependent, reconstructive process, not a passive readout of stored information.
> - The availability/accessibility distinction explains why memories that exist in storage may be temporarily unretrievable: retrieval failure is often a failure of cue-trace matching, not a failure of storage.
> - Retrieval operates through free recall, cued recall, and recognition, each involving different cognitive demands and producing different performance profiles.
> - Retrieval modifies memory: the testing effect demonstrates that retrieval strengthens the retrieved trace, while retrieval-induced forgetting shows that selective retrieval inhibits competing traces.
> - Retrieval is best understood not as the end-point of the memory cycle but as a re-encoding event that reshapes the accessibility landscape of long-term knowledge.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Forgetting is not a unitary process but reflects multiple mechanisms: trace decay, interference (proactive and retroactive), cue-dependent inaccessibility, and motivated suppression.
> - The forgetting curve, first described by Ebbinghaus, shows rapid initial decline followed by deceleration — one of the most robust regularities in psychology.
> - Memory is fundamentally constructive and reconstructive, as demonstrated by Bartlett and elaborated by Loftus: remembering involves the assembly of a plausible account from stored fragments, schematic expectations, and post-event information.
> - The misinformation effect demonstrates that memory can be genuinely altered by post-event suggestion, with significant implications for legal testimony.
> - Forgetting can be reframed as adaptive editing — a mechanism for maintaining the currency and relevance of the memory system's contents.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - The desirable difficulties framework provides the unifying theory of evidence-based memory enhancement: conditions that impose productive effort during encoding and retrieval produce stronger and more flexible long-term traces.
> - The key evidence-based strategies are spacing (distributed practice), retrieval practice (testing as learning), interleaving (mixed practice), generation (active production), and elaborative interrogation (explanatory questioning).
> - The metacognitive paradox means that learners' intuitive study preferences are systematically miscalibrated: the strategies that feel most effective are typically the least effective for long-term retention.
> - The effectiveness of desirable difficulties is moderated by expertise level: strategies that benefit intermediate learners may overwhelm novices or bore experts.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> - The encoding specificity principle establishes that retrieval success depends on the match between encoding context and retrieval cues.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Consolidation transforms labile encoded traces into stable long-term representations through two levels of process: synaptic consolidation (minutes to hours, involving LTP and protein synthesis) and systems consolidation (weeks to years, involving hippocampal-neocortical dialogue).
> - The standard model of consolidation holds that memories gradually become hippocampally independent; the multiple trace theory challenges this by arguing that detailed episodic memories require permanent hippocampal support.
> - Reconsolidation demonstrates that retrieved memories re-enter a labile state, enabling update but also introducing vulnerability.
> - Sleep plays an active role in consolidation, with slow-wave sleep supporting hippocampal replay and systems consolidation, and REM sleep potentially supporting integration with existing knowledge.
> - Consolidation is not passive preservation but active transformation — a process that schematises, generalises, and integrates at the cost of episodic fidelity.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Memories are stored as distributed patterns of activation across neural networks, not as discrete records in fixed locations.
> - The organization of semantic knowledge has been modelled as hierarchical networks and, more successfully, as spreading activation networks with associative links of varying strength.
> - Schemas — organized knowledge structures representing typical features of experience categories — serve as the fundamental units of complex knowledge organization, guiding encoding, storage, and retrieval.
> - Schemas are both productive (enabling inference, comprehension, and efficient storage) and problematic (introducing systematic distortions through schema-consistent gap-filling).
> - Memory storage is better understood as an ongoing process of reconstruction than as a static state of preservation.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Retrieval is an active, cue-dependent, reconstructive process, not a passive readout of stored information.
> - The availability/accessibility distinction explains why memories that exist in storage may be temporarily unretrievable: retrieval failure is often a failure of cue-trace matching, not a failure of storage.
> - Retrieval operates through free recall, cued recall, and recognition, each involving different cognitive demands and producing different performance profiles.
> - Retrieval modifies memory: the testing effect demonstrates that retrieval strengthens the retrieved trace, while retrieval-induced forgetting shows that selective retrieval inhibits competing traces.
> - Retrieval is best understood not as the end-point of the memory cycle but as a re-encoding event that reshapes the accessibility landscape of long-term knowledge.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - Forgetting is not a unitary process but reflects multiple mechanisms: trace decay, interference (proactive and retroactive), cue-dependent inaccessibility, and motivated suppression.
> - The forgetting curve, first described by Ebbinghaus, shows rapid initial decline followed by deceleration — one of the most robust regularities in psychology.
> - Memory is fundamentally constructive and reconstructive, as demonstrated by Bartlett and elaborated by Loftus: remembering involves the assembly of a plausible account from stored fragments, schematic expectations, and post-event information.
> - The misinformation effect demonstrates that memory can be genuinely altered by post-event suggestion, with significant implications for legal testimony.
> - Forgetting can be reframed as adaptive editing — a mechanism for maintaining the currency and relevance of the memory system's contents.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> - The desirable difficulties framework provides the unifying theory of evidence-based memory enhancement: conditions that impose productive effort during encoding and retrieval produce stronger and more flexible long-term traces.
> - The key evidence-based strategies are spacing (distributed practice), retrieval practice (testing as learning), interleaving (mixed practice), generation (active production), and elaborative interrogation (explanatory questioning).
> - The metacognitive paradox means that learners' intuitive study preferences are systematically miscalibrated: the strategies that feel most effective are typically the least effective for long-term retention.
> - The effectiveness of desirable difficulties is moderated by expertise level: strategies that benefit intermediate learners may overwhelm novices or bore experts.
> *— [[long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-16]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The Nelson–Narrens model reframes metacognition from a taxonomic inventory of self-knowledge types into a formal informational architecture. Two levels — meta and object — exchange information through asymmetric channels: monitoring flows upward (inferential, cue-based, fallible) and control flows downward (directive, action-initiating). This architecture unifies diverse empirical phenomena under a single grammar and commits to a representational, model-theoretic view of self-knowledge that explains both metacognitive competence and metacognitive error.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The Nelson–Narrens model synthesises three intellectual lineages: Flavell's metacognitive taxonomy (conceptual vocabulary), information-processing theory (architectural precision), and cybernetic feedback theory (self-regulatory dynamics). Nelson contributed empirical rigour; Narrens contributed formal specification. The resulting framework is a testable architectural claim, but it inherits a cold-cognition bias that later extensions have worked to correct.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Monitoring is the ascending informational channel through which the meta-level constructs its model of the object-level. It operates through cue utilisation (intrinsic, extrinsic, and mnemonic cues), not through direct inspection. The diagnostic validity of monitoring depends on which cues are available and utilised, as demonstrated by the delayed JOL effect. Monitoring operates across multiple timescales and levels of specificity, and it is susceptible to systematic biases — overconfidence, fluency illusions — that are structural features of the cue-mediated architecture rather than mere imperfections.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Control is the descending channel through which the meta-level modifies object-level processing. Major control processes include study-time allocation (refined by Metcalfe's region of proximal learning hypothesis), strategy selection and switching, retrieval termination, and effort regulation. Monitoring and control are ideally tightly coupled, producing effective self-regulation, but can decouple due to motivational, strategic, or contextual factors. The control channel operates at multiple timescales and is under-specified by the Nelson–Narrens model in ways that SRL frameworks seek to elaborate.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The Nelson–Narrens taxonomy of metacognitive judgments — EOL, JOL, FOK, and retrospective confidence — provides a structured vocabulary for the empirical study of monitoring. Each judgment type occurs at a specific phase of the learning–retrieval cycle and draws on a distinct cue ecology. The delayed JOL effect and the monitoring dual memories hypothesis confirm the cue-mediated nature of monitoring. FOK judgments demonstrate that monitoring can operate even when retrieval fails, but commission errors reveal the fundamental limitation of accessibility-based monitoring. Retrospective confidence is susceptible to the overconfidence bias, reflecting reliance on non-diagnostic cues.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Calibration (absolute accuracy) and resolution (relative accuracy) are dissociable dimensions of monitoring quality with different implications for metacognitive control. The Calibration–Regulation Coupling Thesis proposes that calibration supports threshold decisions while resolution supports allocation decisions. Monitoring failures can be organised into a Three-Gradient Taxonomy: cue-validity failures, cue-availability failures, and model-integration failures, each requiring different interventions. Calibration training works by modifying the cue ecology, confirming the Nelson–Narrens model's cue-mediated account of monitoring.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> The Nelson–Narrens model functions as the cognitive core of the major SRL frameworks. Zimmerman's cyclical model maps its three phases onto different types of monitoring and control. Pintrich extends the monitoring–control architecture beyond cognition into motivational, behavioural, and contextual domains. Winne elaborates the information-processing details of the monitoring–evaluation–control loop. Schraw operationalises the framework for educational practice. The productive tension between the model's precision and its limited scope drives ongoing efforts toward integrative SRL theories.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section Summary
> Contemporary extensions of the Nelson–Narrens model include neuroscientific research identifying the anterior prefrontal cortex as a metacognitive substrate, cross-domain applications of the monitoring–control architecture, and connections to predictive processing. Criticisms target the binary-level assumption, the neglect of affect and motivation, and the model's information-processing commitments. The model remains the foundational framework for metacognition research while continuing to evolve in response to these challenges.
> *— [[nelson-narrens-metacognitive-model-foundational-report-2026-04-16_report]]*
