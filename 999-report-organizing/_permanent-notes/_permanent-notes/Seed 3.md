> **Q:** What is the "self-protection paradox" in PAV goal dynamics, and what mechanism explains it?
> **A:** PAV goals are adopted to protect the appearance of competence, but their cognitive and behavioral consequences (surface processing, help-seeking suppression, defensive attribution) systematically reduce genuine competence development. The mechanism: avoidance regulation redirects attentional and cognitive resources from task-relevant processing toward self-presentation monitoring, suppressing the deep engagement that generates real competence.
> **Source:** Phase III, PAV Evidence; Phase IV, Mechanisms

> **Q:** Why are effort attributions motivationally superior to ability attributions, in terms of all three dimensions of Weiner's model?
> **A:** Effort is unstable (generates hope, not hopelessness), controllable (generates guilt as motivation, not shame as paralysis), and internal (preserves the self-evaluation that makes future success meaningful). The motivational superiority is overdetermined — operating through three independent mechanisms simultaneously.

> **Q:** What is a calibration event, and what distinguishes it from ordinary information delivery?
> **A:** A calibration event is an assessment activity that produces an internally-generated encounter with the discrepancy between felt-knowing and actual-knowing. It is distinguished by being internally generated (cannot be attributed to external factors) and by producing a prediction error the monitoring system cannot rationalize away. Retrieval failure is a paradigm case: the student's monitoring system predicted "I know this" and the retrieval attempt disconfirmed it unambiguously.
> **Source:** Phase IV, calibration event taxonomy; Appendix Lexicon
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Distinction
> **Tags:** #calibration-event, #testing-effect, #retrieval-practice

> **Q:** What is "chunking" in the context of basal ganglia habit formation, and what is its behavioral signature?
> **A:** Chunking is the encoding of a behavioral sequence as a unified unit with a triggering start signal and a terminal stop signal in the dorsolateral striatum. Behavioral signature: sequences execute without attentional guidance of intermediate elements; disruption of any element impairs the whole sequence; the beginning and end are neurally tagged.

> [!flashcard] Seed 3
> **Q:** What is the Attributional Vocabulary Loop, and in which direction does it most commonly self-amplify?
> **A:** A self-amplifying cycle: reflection vocabulary encodes attributional dimensions → those dimensions shape goal vocabulary in forethought → goal vocabulary determines monitoring sensitivity → monitoring quality determines outcome experiences → outcomes feed back into reflection vocabulary. It most commonly amplifies destructively because poor outcomes are more likely to produce ability attribution vocabulary (which forecloses the loop) than strategy attribution vocabulary (which keeps it open).
> **Source:** Phase IV, Mechanism 1

> **Type:** Process + Application

> **Q:** Why does the attribution retraining literature constitute evidence for the constitutive (rather than expressive) account of vocabulary?
> **A:** Because in attribution retraining, vocabulary change (practicing "I haven't mastered this yet") precedes and produces the underlying belief change — not the reverse. If vocabulary were purely expressive, you would need the underlying belief to change first. The timing evidence favors constitutive causality.

> [!flashcard] Seed 3
> **Q:** What is the "self-efficacy monitoring loop" in Pintrich's framework?
>
> **A:** Three stages: (a) Prior self-efficacy → strategy selection at forethought; (b) Monitoring signals → real-time self-efficacy updating during engagement; (c) Updated self-efficacy → regulatory response (persist, switch strategies, seek help, or disengage). The loop enables adaptive regulation when self-efficacy is monitored and repair strategies are applied; it becomes self-reinforcing in failure when monitoring is bypassed.
>
> **Source:** Phase IV, Mechanism 1
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Process
> **Tags:** #self-efficacy #metacognitive-monitoring #pintrich

> **Q:** What evidence from the chess expertise studies is most directly relevant to the schema construction problem?
>
> **A:** The 50,000 chunks / 10-year evidence: chess masters have ~50,000 chunk schemas and acquiring them requires ~10 years of deliberate practice. This tells us the *duration* and *scale* of schema construction but not the *mechanism*. The evidence shows that schema construction occurs (experts have schemas novices lack) but does not reveal how the transition from no-schema to schema happens.

> **Q**: What is the expert blind spot, and why is it a problem for schema theory?
> **A**: The expert blind spot is the documented tendency for subject-matter experts to be systematically worse instructors of novices because their schema automation places intermediate processing steps below conscious access. It is a problem for schema theory because the theory predicts that richer schemas should improve instruction — the expert blind spot is a direct prediction failure.

> [!flashcard] Seed 3
> **Q:** What is the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy, and what evidence falsifies it in reading comprehension?
>
> **A:** The Automatic-Assembly Fallacy is the mistaken belief that adequate processing at a lower level automatically produces coherent integration at a higher level. In reading, it is the belief that understanding sentences automatically produces a coherent situation model. It is falsified by the specific comprehender dissociation: readers with intact word-level decoding and sentence-level processing who nevertheless construct severely impaired situation models. If the automatic-assembly claim were correct, this population could not exist.
>

> **Type:** Distinction

> [!flashcard] Seed 3
> **Q:** What makes an attribution "adaptive" in the SRL cycle? Give the three necessary criteria.
> **A:** An adaptive attribution is (1) SPECIFIC — names a particular mechanism or behavior rather than a global trait; (2) UNSTABLE — implies the outcome could be different with different strategy/preparation; (3) CONTROLLABLE — within the learner's influence to change. All three are necessary: a specific but uncontrollable attribution (e.g., "this is just a particularly hard domain") does not produce adaptive inference.
> **Source:** Phase IV, Causal Attribution mechanism
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Distinction

> [!flashcard] Seed 3
> **Q:** What is a "degenerate cycle" in the context of Zimmerman's SRL model, and why does it matter for SRL generalization?
>
> **A:** A degenerate cycle is one in which the functional phase-transition connections are absent: the learner enters the performance phase without meaningful forethought and exits with global evaluative reflection rather than causal, strategy-specific self-reflection. It matters because learners who run degenerate cycles in every domain cannot compound self-regulatory capability across contexts, explaining why SRL training often fails to generalize.
>

> [!flashcard] Seed 3
> **Q:** What is "monitoring-regulation decoupling" and why does the Zimmerman model struggle to account for it?
>
> **A:** Monitoring-regulation decoupling is the phenomenon in which monitoring behaviors (noticing comprehension failures, expressing uncertainty) occur without triggering regulatory action (changing strategy, seeking additional resources). The Zimmerman model struggles to account for it because the cycle's circular architecture implies tight coupling — the model does not specify the conditions under which the monitoring-to-regulation link succeeds or fails. The Nelson-Narens two-level model provides a finer-grained account: monitoring (object-to-meta information flow) and control (meta-to-object information flow) are separable channels, each susceptible to independent failure.
>
