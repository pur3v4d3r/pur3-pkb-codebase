# Schema

> [!definition] Schema
> A generalized, organized mental structure representing knowledge about categories of objects, events, relationships, or procedures, stored in long-term memory. Schemas are active during comprehension — generating expectations, directing attention, and enabling inference — rather than passive storage containers. Schemas are modified through *accommodation* when incoming information cannot be assimilated.

> [!evidence] Schema
> A **schema** (plural: schemata or schemas) is a mental framework or organized knowledge structure that encodes prototypical knowledge about objects, events, situations, and relationships in a domain. Schemas function simultaneously as perceptual templates (enabling recognition of what is encountered), inferential engines (generating expectations and filling gaps), interpretive frameworks (determining what incoming information means), and action scripts (guiding goal-directed behavior in familiar situations). In the context of constructivism, schemas are the existing cognitive structures into which new information is assimilated and which are modified through accommodation in response to perturbation.
> *— [[constructivist-learning-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!evidence] Schema
> An organized cognitive structure representing generalized knowledge about a domain, derived from experience and used to structure the interpretation of new information. In communication, schemas function as the listener's interpretive framework: new information is assimilated into existing schemas (extending them) or requires schema revision (conceptual change). Mismatched schemas between speaker and listener are a primary source of communication failure. See [[schema-theory]], [[mental-models]], [[constructivist-learning-theory]].

> [!evidence] Schema
> A [[schema|schema]] is an organized knowledge structure in [[long-term-memory|long-term memory]] that represents a category of concepts, events, or procedures, specifying the relationships among its component elements and providing default expectations that guide perception, comprehension, and action. Schemas function as cognitive templates that enable the processing of complex, multi-element information as a single unit within [[working-memory|working memory]], dramatically reducing the cognitive load associated with expert performance. The construction of schemas through learning and the automation of schemas through practice constitute the primary mechanisms of [[expertise-development|expertise development]].
> *— [[pkb-pkm-cognitive-strategies-for-learning-and-pkb-architecture-foundational-report-2026-04-18]]*

> [!evidence] Schema
> A structured mental framework that organizes generalized knowledge about a class of entities, events, situations, or relationships. Schemas contain variables (slots) with default values, are embedded in hierarchies of related schemas, and function as active knowledge structures that guide perception, inference, and reconstructive recall. The term was given its contemporary technical meaning by [[frederic-bartlett|Frederic Bartlett]] (1932) and elaborated by [[David-Rumelhart|David Rumelhart]] (1975, 1980). Boundary condition: a schema is not an isolated fact, a simple category label, or a verbatim memory record.
> *— [[schema-theory-and-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-06_report]]*

> [!evidence] Schema
> A structured, generative mental representation organizing related knowledge about a class of entities, events, or situations using fixed structural features and variable slots instantiated by specific incoming data or default inferences. The foundational construct of schema theory.
> *Historical Note*: The term derives from Bartlett's (1932) reformulation of Kantian schemata in empirical psychology. *Common Misconception*: A schema is not a simple concept or category label — it is a generative predictive framework. *See also*: [[schema-theory]], [[schema-construction]], [[schema-automation]], [[schema-attractor]], [[schema-progressive-architecture]]

> [!evidence] Schema
> A mental representation that organizes knowledge about a concept, domain, situation, role, event, or procedure. Schemas have structure (slots with constraints on filler values), generativity (they generate predictions and inferences beyond what is explicitly given), and hierarchy (schemas are embedded within more abstract schemas). First formalized in cognitive science by [[rumelhart]] and Norman (1975); foundational to modern learning science, cognitive psychology, and instructional design. See also: [[schemas]], [[schema-theory]].
