## Open Threads

> [!tension-identified] The Vocabulary-Causality Problem: Does Word Choice Drive Motivation or Merely Label It?
> **The Tension:** The central practical claim of this report — that deliberately changing inner speech vocabulary produces genuine motivational change — rests on an assumption that vocabulary is causally upstream of motivational states, not merely downstream of them. This is contested.
>
> **Position A — Language Constitutes Motivation (Wittgenstein, Vygotsky, Constructionist):** Language is not a passive reporter of pre-linguistic states; it organizes and partly constitutes cognitive and motivational experience. Changing the vocabulary of inner speech does not merely rename existing states — it genuinely reorganizes the cognitive content that language both expresses and structures. Evidence: attribution retraining interventions, which change both causal language and subsequent motivation and performance; theory of constructed emotion (Barrett), which suggests emotional categories are constructed through language-mediated conceptualization.
>
> **Position B — Motivation is Primary, Language Derivative (Traditional Representationalism):** Motivational states are non-linguistic mental events that are subsequently labeled by language. Changing vocabulary may produce momentary relabeling without changing the underlying state; the observable effects of attribution retraining may work through mechanisms other than language change.
>
> **Current Evidence:** Attribution retraining intervention studies (Perry et al., 1993; Haynes et al., 2006) do support causal effects of reframing on performance, but the mechanism is not definitively established as linguistic. The most honest position is that language and motivation are mutually constitutive, with neither unambiguously primary.
>
> **Why It Matters:** If language is only derivative, the vocabulary protocols in this report are misdirected — they address the symptom rather than the cause. If language is constitutive, they address the mechanism directly.
>
