# Rubicon

> [!definition] **Rubicon** *(from [[implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> The metaphorical boundary between the predecisional phase (in which the actor weighs alternative goals) and the preactional phase (in which the chosen goal is planned and enacted), named after Caesar's irrevocable river crossing. Crossing the rubicon represents a qualitative shift from open-minded deliberation to commitment-protective implementation.
> 
> **Boundary:** The rubicon metaphor is occasionally criticized as overstating the irreversibility of the transition; in practice, actors do reconsider commitments after the rubicon has been crossed.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The rubicon image clarifies why implementation intentions are most effective when commitment is firm and least effective when commitment is still being weighed.
> 
> **See also:** [[action-phase-model]], [[volition]]

## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Peter M. Gollwitzer (b. 1950, NYU / University of Konstanz)** *(from [[implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> The principal architect of implementation-intention theory, whose 1993 chapter in the *European Review of Social Psychology* and 1999 *American Psychologist* article established the construct in its mature form. Gollwitzer's collaboration with Heinz Heckhausen on the action phase model provided the theoretical home from which implementation intentions were derived. Gollwitzer's subsequent collaborations with Paschal Sheeran (meta-analytic synthesis), Gabriele Oettingen (MCII development), and others extended the construct across health, academic, and clinical domains.

> [!person] **Heinz Heckhausen (1926-1988, University of Bochum / Max Planck Institute Munich)** *(from [[implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> The German motivation psychologist whose action phase model provided the theoretical scaffolding from which implementation intentions were derived. Heckhausen's distinction between the motivational and volitional phases of action restored the volitional concept to mainstream psychology after decades of behaviorist suppression and prepared the conceptual ground that Gollwitzer's later work built upon.

> [!person] **Paschal Sheeran (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill)** *(from [[implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> The British-American social psychologist whose 2006 meta-analysis with Gollwitzer (94 studies, d=0.65) provided the most influential single empirical synthesis of implementation-intention effects, establishing the construct as an evidence-based intervention rather than a promising hypothesis. Sheeran's subsequent work has extended the meta-analytic record and explored boundary conditions across health-behavior domains.

> [!person] **Gabriele Oettingen (NYU / University of Hamburg)** *(from [[implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> The developer of mental contrasting and the principal collaborator on the MCII compound intervention. Oettingen's research program on positive future thinking, expectancy-dependent commitment, and the failure modes of unstructured fantasizing complements Gollwitzer's action-control program and produces the most evidence-supported applied form of implementation-intention practice.

> [!person] **Michael Bratman (Stanford University)** *(from [[implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-foundational-report-2026-04-20]])*
> The American philosopher whose *Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason* (1987) developed the philosophical theory of intention as a planning state with stability, settledness, and downstream commitment-organizing functions. Bratman's planning theory provides the philosophical foundation against which implementation intentions can be understood as a specific psychological mechanism that exploits the structural features Bratman identified at the philosophical level.

**A:** Goal intentions specify *what* the actor wants to achieve ("I intend to achieve Z"); implementation intentions specify *when, where, and how* the corresponding behavior will be enacted ("when X, I will Y"). > **Q:** Distinction: Strategic vs. Habitual Automaticity
> **A:** **Q:** How does strategic automaticity differ from the automaticity of consolidated habits?
