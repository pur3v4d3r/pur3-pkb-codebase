# Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973)

> [!definition] Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973)
> The Encoding Specificity Principle states that a retrieval cue will facilitate recall of a target item if and only if information about the cue and its relationship to the target was encoded together at the time of initial learning. More precisely: *what is stored is what is encoded, and what is retrieved depends on what was available at encoding.* Successful remembering requires that the information present at the time of testing overlap — structurally, semantically, or contextually — with the information that was encoded. Memory retrieval is therefore not a function of trace strength alone but of the fit between encoded trace and retrieval cue.
>
> **Boundary:** The ESP does not claim that identical cues are required for retrieval. It specifies that the *information about the cue* must have been encoded at learning. Partial matches enable partial retrieval; the relationship must be encoded, not merely the cue itself.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This definition grounds every subsequent section. Understanding that retrieval depends on an encoding-retrieval relationship — not trace strength alone — reorients how we think about forgetting, practice, learning design, and knowledge systems.
>
> **See also:** [[encoding-specificity]], [[endel-tulving]], [[donald-thomson]], [[memory-systems]], [[retrieval-practice]]

> [!evidence] Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973)
> The principle that a retrieval cue is effective only to the extent that it matches the information encoded alongside the target at the time of original learning. Memory performance thus depends not only on the quality of encoding or the strength of the stored trace but on the degree of overlap between encoding and retrieval contexts.
>
> **Boundary:** Encoding specificity does not mean that only identical cues can trigger retrieval. It means that the effectiveness of any cue is determined by the degree to which it reinstates the processing context present at encoding.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This principle reframes retrieval success as a function of cue-trace compatibility rather than trace strength alone, with implications for study strategy and assessment design.
>
> **See also:** [[encoding-specificity-principle]], [[transfer-appropriate-processing]], [[Cue-Dependent-Forgetting]]

> [!evidence] Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973)
> The [[encoding-specificity-principle]] holds that a retrieval cue is effective in proportion to its overlap with the encoding context of the target memory. What is encoded is not merely the target item but a complex including the contextual cues present at encoding; retrieval success depends on how well the retrieval environment reinstates the encoding context. This explains context-dependent memory (recall is better when test and study environments match) and state-dependent memory (recall is better when internal state at test matches state at encoding).
>
> **Boundary:** Does not imply that retrieval is impossible without contextual reinstatement; it merely predicts that contextual match facilitates retrieval. High-strength traces can be retrieved despite contextual mismatch, and the benefit of reinstatement decreases as trace strength increases.
>
> **See also:** [[encoding-specificity-principle]], [[transfer-of-learning]], [[retrieval-practice]], [[context-dependent-memory]]

> [!evidence] Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973)
> The principle that retrieval success depends on the overlap between conditions at encoding and conditions at retrieval. Information encoded in a particular context (emotional state, physical environment, semantic context) is best retrieved in a context that provides cues overlapping with those encoded alongside the original trace. This explains why learning material in exactly the conditions under which it will be tested (or constructing those conditions at study) can improve recall — and why material learned in a radically different context may fail to be retrieved even though it is demonstrably in long-term memory.

> [!evidence] Encoding Specificity Principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973)
> The [[encoding-specificity-principle]] states that memory retrieval is most effective when the cues present at retrieval match or overlap with the information that was encoded along with the target at study time. Information is not stored as an isolated unit but as a context-rich encoding that includes the schematic framing, emotional tone, environmental cues, and conceptual associations active at encoding time. Retrieval succeeds when the cue can activate this encoding context.
>
> **Boundary:** This principle applies to episodic memory most strongly but has implications for semantic memory as well, where the "context" is conceptual rather than environmental — the cluster of associated concepts active when an idea was first encountered shapes how it can subsequently be retrieved.
>
