# Functional Significance  Informational, Controlling, Amotivating

> [!definition] **Functional Significance  Informational, Controlling, Amotivating** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> See full definition in Phase II. Functional significance is the perceived motivational role of an external event — the function the agent interprets the event as serving. Three aspects are identified: informational (competence-affirming, autonomy-preserving), controlling (pressure toward specific outcomes), and amotivating (competence-disconfirming). The dominant aspect in an individual's interpretation determines the motivational consequence of the event.
> 
> **See also:** [[autonomy-support]], [[controlling-environment]], [[autonomy-supportive-environment]], [[reward-contingency-types]]

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **The Central Claim of CET**: The motivational impact of an external event is not determined by its objective properties (tangible vs. verbal, large vs. small, expected vs. unexpected) but by the functional significance the individual assigns to it — specifically, whether the event is experienced as informational and autonomy-supportive or as controlling and autonomy-undermining. The same event can have opposite effects depending on the interpretive frame the individual applies.

This prediction distinguishes CET sharply from behaviorist accounts and generates a specific methodological requirement: tests of CET should include mediation analyses…

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between the perceived locus of causality (PLOC) and locus of control?
**A:** The PLOC concerns the perceived cause of one's *behavior* — whether one's actions are experienced as self-determined (internal PLOC) or externally caused (external PLOC). Locus of control (Rotter) concerns beliefs about the causes of *outcomes* — whether one's actions reliably produce intended results. The PLOC is about agency over action; locus of control is about the predictability of outcomes from action.
**Source:** Phase II — PLOC Definition
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** PLOC, locus-of-control, CET, SDT

**A:** It measures the time a participant spontaneously spends with a target activity during an ostensibly unmonitored period after the experimental manipulation. The primary criticism is that it may conflate intrinsic motivation with satiation, scheduling constraints, or contextual availability of competing activities — rather than purely indexing motivational state.

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the overjustification effect, and what attribution-theoretic principle generates it?
**A:** The overjustification effect is the reduction in intrinsic motivation that follows the introduction of an expected extrinsic reward for an already-interesting activity. The attribution-theoretic principle is Kelley's discounting principle: when multiple plausible causes for an action are present, the perceived role of any single cause is reduced. The highly salient external reward "crowds out" the internal cause (intrinsic interest) in the individual's causal attribution.
**Source:** Phase III — Historical Origins; Lexicon
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Connection
**Tags:** overjustification, attribution-theory, CET, intrinsic-motivation

> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** How does CET treat ego involvement? What makes it analogous to an external controlling condition?
**A:** Ego involvement is a state in which performance is driven by the contingency between outcome and self-worth. CET treats it as an *intrapersonal* controlling condition: like external controlling events, ego involvement shifts the PLOC from the intrinsic features of the activity toward a self-evaluative agenda, converting exploratory engagement into self-protective performance management. The mechanism is the same as external control — a shift in functional significance from informational to controlling — but the pressure source is internal.

> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** How does the interpersonal climate moderate the functional significance of external events according to CET?
**A:** An autonomy-supportive climate reduces the likelihood that any given external event (including potentially controlling ones like rewards or evaluations) will be parsed as controlling in its functional significance. Conversely, a controlling climate increases the likelihood that even informationally rich events will be experienced as pressure. The climate does not determine consequences directly — it modulates how events are interpreted through the functional significance framework.

> **A:** **Q:** Why do unexpected rewards not undermine intrinsic motivation according to CET?

> [!flashcard] **Card 11** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** How does CET relate to Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT) within SDT?
**A:** BPNT specifies that autonomy and competence (and relatedness) are universal psychological needs whose satisfaction is necessary for well-being and effective functioning. CET provides the micro-level specification of how environmental events either satisfy or frustrate the needs for autonomy (via PLOC) and competence (via perceived competence modulation). CET is the mechanism through which BPNT operates at the level of specific external events.
**Source:** Phase III — CET within SDT
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Connection
**Tags:** CET, BPNT, SDT, basic-psychological-needs

> [!flashcard] **Card 12** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is CET's prediction about the effect of verbal positive feedback on intrinsic motivation, and what is the key conditioning factor?
**A:** Verbal positive feedback reliably enhances intrinsic motivation because it satisfies the competence need without imposing controlling pressure. The key conditioning factor is the interpersonal climate: if the context in which the feedback is delivered is autonomy-supportive, the informational aspect dominates and intrinsic motivation is enhanced. If the context is controlling — even when the content of the feedback is positive — the controlling aspect may compete with or override the informational aspect.
**Source:** Phase II — Propositions; Phase IV — Mechanisms
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Type:** Application
**Tags:** verbal-feedback, intrinsic-motivation, autonomy-support, CET

> [!flashcard] **Card 13** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the "motivational tax" metaphor as used in this report, and what does it describe?
**A:** The "motivational tax" refers to the depletion of intrinsic motivational capital that controlling environments impose on participants. Even when controlling systems achieve behavioral compliance (outputs are produced), they simultaneously consume the autonomous motivational resources that drive creative, self-sustaining, deep performance. The metaphor captures CET's core warning: systems that maximize short-term behavioral output through control may systematically undermine the intrinsic motivational substrate on which long-term, high-quality performance depends.
**Source:** Phase VI — Synthesis
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Connection
**Tags:** CET, motivational-tax, autonomous-motivation, intrinsic-motivation

> [!flashcard] **Card 14** *(from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** How does CET distinguish its account of the overjustification effect from Lepper et al.'s original attribution-theoretic framing?
**A:** Lepper et al. grounded the overjustification effect in Kelley's discounting principle — the external reward crowds out the internal cause in attributional inference. Deci and Ryan retheorized the mechanism through need-satisfaction language: the controlling event shifts the perceived locus of causality (frustrating autonomy need) and modulates perceived competence (either supporting or frustrating the competence need). The need-satisfaction account generates additional predictions about well-being and affect that the purely attributional account cannot produce — specifically, that the quality of subjective experience (not just behavior) should be degraded by controlling conditions.
**Source:** Phase III — CET in Relation to Other Frameworks
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Type:** Distinction
**Tags:** CET, overjustification, attribution-theory, SDT, psychological-needs

## References

- **Deci, E. L. (1971). Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 18*(1), 105–115.**: The founding empirical study of the CET research program. Deci's experiments with SOMA cube puzzles and monetary reward provided the first controlled evidence of the undermining effect. Essential primary source for understanding the paradigm.

**Recommended Sections:** The method section establishes the free-choice behavioral paradigm; the Discussion presents Deci's original theoretical interpretation.
- **Lepper, M. R., Greene, D., & Nisbett, R. E. (1973). Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the "overjustification" hypothesis. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 28*(1), 129–137.**: The independent parallel discovery of the undermining effect from an attribution-theoretic perspective. The "overjustification" terminology and preschool-drawing paradigm originated here. Essential companion piece to Deci (1971).
- **Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). *Intrinsic motivation and self-determination in human behavior*. Plenum Press.**: The primary theoretical text formalizing CET's seven propositions within the emerging SDT framework. Chapters 3–5 present the full CET architecture. Graduate-level reference for the theoretical foundations.
- **Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (2000). The "what" and "why" of goal pursuits: Human needs and the self-determination of behavior. *Psychological Inquiry, 11*(4), 227–268.**: The most comprehensive single-article overview of SDT, including CET's relationship to the other mini-theories. Recommended as the primary entry point into the full SDT framework post-CET.
- **Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Psychological Bulletin, 125*(6), 627–668.**: The definitive quantitative synthesis of 128 reward-intrinsic motivation experiments. Rebutted Cameron and Pierce (1994) and established the contingency-differentiated treatment of reward effects. Essential reading for understanding the empirical status of CET.

**Recommended Sections:** Tables 1–4 present the effect sizes by contingency type; Discussion addresses the Cameron-Pierce controversy.
- **Cameron, J., & Pierce, W. D. (1994). Reinforcement, reward, and intrinsic motivation: A meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 64*(3), 363–423.**: The primary empirical challenge to CET and the text that triggered the meta-analytic controversy. Read alongside Deci et al. (1999) to understand the methodological debate.
- **Grolnick, W. S., & Ryan, R. M. (1987). Autonomy in children's learning: An experimental and individual difference investigation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 52*(5), 890–898.**: Established empirically that autonomy-supportive instructional conditions produce superior conceptual understanding and retention compared to controlling conditions, with documented motivational mediation. Core educational application study.
- **Deci, E. L., Connell, J. P., & Ryan, R. M. (1989). Self-determination in a work organization. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 74*(4), 580–590.**: Extended CET predictions to organizational contexts; manager training in autonomy-supportive behavior produced significant effects on employee intrinsic motivation, trust, and well-being. Foundational organizational psychology application.
- **Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78.**: Widely cited integrative overview of SDT's empirical base and practical applications. Accessible entry point; cited over 60,000 times. Key sections on autonomy-supportive contexts and learning outcomes.
- **Amabile, T. M., DeJong, W., & Lepper, M. R. (1976). Effects of externally imposed deadlines on subsequent intrinsic motivation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 34*(1), 92–98.**: Demonstrated that deadlines, as controlling contextual events, undermine intrinsic motivation — extending CET's predictions beyond rewards to time pressure and scheduling constraints. Important for understanding the full scope of controlling events.
- **Koestner, R., Ryan, R. M., Bernieri, F., & Holt, K. (1984). Setting limits on children's behavior: The differential effects of controlling vs. informational styles on intrinsic motivation and creativity. *Journal of Personality, 52*(3), 233–248.**: Demonstrated that the verbal style of instructions — controlling vs. informational — modulates intrinsic motivation and creativity independently of the content of the instructions. Foundational for understanding interpersonal climate moderators.
- **Chirkov, V., Ryan, R. M., Kim, Y., & Kaplan, U. (2003). Differentiating autonomy from individualism and independence: A self-determination theory perspective on internalization of cultural orientations and well-being. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 84*(1), 97–110.**: Cross-cultural investigation of SDT's autonomy construct across four cultures; found that autonomous choice and relatedness-consistent internalization predicted well-being across cultures, supporting the universality claim while acknowledging cultural expression differences.
- **Reeve, J. (2009). Why teachers adopt a controlling motivating style toward students and how they can become more autonomy supportive. *Educational Psychologist, 44*(3), 159–175.**: Analysis of the structural and psychological factors that lead teachers to adopt controlling styles despite the motivational costs; provides practical guidance for autonomy-supportive teaching. Combines CET with organizational constraints analysis.
- **Vansteenkiste, M., Niemiec, C. P., & Soenens, B. (2010). The development of the five mini-theories of self-determination theory: An historical overview, emerging trends, and future directions. In T. C. Urdan & S. A. Karabenick (Eds.), *The Decade Ahead: Theoretical Perspectives on Motivation and Achievement* (pp. 105–165). Emerald Group.**: Comprehensive historical overview of the development of all five SDT mini-theories, placing CET in the context of the full theoretical development. Essential contextualizing reference.
