## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] Technical Detail: The Multiple Sources of Processing Fluency
> Processing fluency is not a unitary construct. Research has identified multiple distinct sources that produce the same phenomenal experience of "easiness," each with a different relationship to actual learning:
>
> 1. <span style='color: #9E6CD3;'>**Perceptual fluency:**</span> Ease of visual or auditory processing. Driven by font clarity, contrast, presentation duration, sensory quality. Correlation with learning: **near zero.** A word in clear 18pt fonts feels more "known" than the same word in blurry 10pt fonts, but this has no bearing on whether the word has been encoded.
>
> 2. <span style='color: #9E6CD3;'>**Conceptual fluency:**</span> Ease of semantic processing. Driven by prior knowledge, associative strength, context congruence. Correlation with learning: **moderate to high.** When conceptual fluency reflects genuine semantic integration, it does predict learning.
>
> 3. <span style='color: #9E6CD3;'>**Retrieval fluency:**</span> Speed and ease of retrieving information from memory. Driven by cue-target associative strength, encoding quality, practice history. Correlation with learning: **highest** among fluency sources, especially when measured during actual retrieval attempts rather than during study.
>
> 4. <span style='color: #9E6CD3;'>**Encoding fluency:**</span> Ease experienced during initial study. Driven by material difficulty, prior knowledge, encoding strategy. Correlation with learning: **low to moderate** and often misleading — easy encoding can reflect shallow processing rather than deep learning.
>
> **Precision:** These fluency types are conceptually distinct but phenomenologically overlapping. The monitoring system does not appear to distinguish between them — all contribute to a single "feeling of ease" that the meta-level uses as its primary input.
> **Dependencies:** Requires understanding of the cue-utilization framework from Level 2.
