# Accretion

> [!definition] Accretion
> The most common and least disruptive mode of schema change. New information is added as *instances* or facts within the existing schema framework, without modifying the schema's structure. The schema's variables, slots, constraints, and default values remain unchanged; only the content instantiated in those slots is updated.
>
> **Example:** Encountering a new restaurant (Thai food, $15 average price, 30-minute wait) adds an instance to the RESTAURANT schema without changing what a restaurant IS. The person now has more data points for what "a restaurant" involves, but the abstract schema is unchanged.
>
> **When accretion is the response:** Accretion occurs when the incoming information is *assimilable* — it fits within the existing schema framework, even if it surprises some expectations on specific slot values. The schema's "coverage predicate" (the criteria for this schema being applicable) is not violated.
>
> **Educational implication:** Much of what passes for "learning" in instruction is accretion — students add facts to existing schemas. Accretion is important but insufficient for conceptual change. It doesn't change how students *understand* — only what they know about specific cases.

> **Accretion** is the incremental addition of new information to an already-existing schema by filling empty or under-specified slots with newly learned values. It is the cognitively cheapest form of schema modification — the new information slots smoothly into an existing structure without creating schematic disturbance.

> [!evidence] Accretion
> The cognitively least demanding form of schema modification: the addition of new factual information to an existing schema by filling available slots with new values. Accretion preserves the schema's underlying organizational structure and is the primary form of everyday learning.
