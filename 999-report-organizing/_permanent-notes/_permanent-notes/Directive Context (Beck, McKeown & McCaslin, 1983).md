# Directive Context (Beck, McKeown & McCaslin, 1983)

> [!definition] Directive Context (Beck, McKeown & McCaslin, 1983)
> A naturally occurring textual context that provides sufficient semantic, syntactic, and pragmatic information for a reader to infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word with reasonable precision. Contrasted with non-directive contexts (which neither help nor hinder inference) and misdirective contexts (which actively support incorrect inferences). Research suggests that directive contexts constitute a minority of naturally occurring contexts in which unfamiliar words appear.
>
> **Boundary:** Context directiveness is not binary — it exists on a continuum from maximally misdirective to maximally directive. A context is "directive" relative to a reader's background knowledge: what is directive for a reader with relevant domain knowledge may be non-directive for a reader without it.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** "The *querulous* child whined constantly, complained about every minor inconvenience, and refused to accept any answer that wasn't in his favor" — this is a directive context for "querulous" because multiple independent cues converge on the meaning.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Context directiveness is one of the key variables in the acquisition rate equation — natural text provides directive contexts for a minority of unfamiliar word encounters, which explains why raw frequency of reading cannot fully substitute for intentional study.
>
