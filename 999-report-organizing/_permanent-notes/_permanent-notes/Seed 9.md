> **Q:** How does the vocabulary specificity of an intermediate reading goal affect comprehension?
> **A:** Generic intermediate goal vocabulary ("I want to understand this section") fails to generate monitoring checkpoints because there is no moment when you know whether you "understand." Specific generative vocabulary ("I want to be able to explain the main argument's structure" or "I want to be able to critique the central assumption") generates precise checkpoints: you know exactly when you have or have not achieved them. Specificity in goal vocabulary therefore directly determines monitoring sensitivity and error-detection frequency.
> **Source:** Phase V, Tool 4 / Phase IV Mechanism 3
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Application + Process

> **Q:** Why do the forethought and self-reflection phases of Zimmerman's SRL model qualify as "primarily vocabulary events" according to the reconstruction?
> **A:** Because all the key processes in these phases — goal setting, self-motivation beliefs, strategic planning (forethought) and self-evaluation, attributional reasoning, adaptive inferences (self-reflection) — occur in inner speech. And because inner speech vocabulary is constitutive of (not merely expressive of) the motivational outcomes of these processes, the specific vocabulary deployed in each phase directly constitutes the motivational architecture of the subsequent phase. Vocabulary development is therefore SRL practice, not merely support for SRL practice.
> **Source:** Phase V Reconstructive Synthesis
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection

> **Q:** What are the three abstract transfer principles extracted from Pintrich's framework?
>
> **A:** (1) Multi-domain simultaneous regulation: effective regulation of any complex system requires monitoring across all relevant subsystems, not just the most salient. (2) Making implicit processes explicit enables improvement: processes that can be explicitly represented can be deliberately modified. (3) Environment is a first-class regulatory domain: when agent-level interventions have diminishing returns, environmental interventions are often underexplored.

> [!flashcard] Seed 9
> **Q:** What is the Construction-Activation Asymmetry, and why does it matter for instructional design?
>
> **A:** The asymmetry is the categorical difference between schema construction (slow, effortful, analogy-dependent, bottom-up) and schema activation (fast, automatic, cue-triggered, top-down). It matters for instructional design because the strategies that support schema activation (retrieval practice, interleaving, spacing) are different from — and may actively underserve — schema construction contexts. Applying activation strategies to construction contexts is a systematic mismatch.
>
> **Source:** Phase II, V
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Application
> *— [[schema-construction-problem-focused-analysis-2026-03-22_report]]*

> **Q:** How does the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy transfer to software architecture?
>
> **A:** In software engineering, individually well-written functions do not automatically produce well-designed system architecture. Architecture requires dedicated architectural attention — a global-level perspective that cannot be derived from reviewing each function in isolation. An engineer who checks each function for correctness and concludes the system is well-designed is committing the Automatic-Assembly Fallacy. The transfer encoding: satisfaction with local quality (clean functions, correct syntax) should always be accompanied by a dedicated global-coherence check (separation of concerns, dependency structure, maintainability).
>

> [!flashcard] Seed 9
> **Q:** How does the Self-Reflection Phase *construct* self-efficacy for the next Forethought Phase?
> **A:** Self-efficacy in a solo PKB learning system is built primarily through mastery experiences — direct experience of successfully achieving comprehension criteria. These experiences are *generated* in the Performance Phase but *interpreted* in the Self-Reflection Phase through the attribution mechanism. If Self-Reflection produces accurate mastery-referenced self-evaluation and controllable attributions, self-efficacy updates accurately. If it produces ability-based attributions, self-efficacy either deflates (failure → I'm not capable) or becomes inflated through fluency illusion (apparent success without genuine comprehension). Self-efficacy is therefore a *dynamic output* of Self-Reflection, not a stable input to Forethought.
> **Source:** Phase IV, Return-and-Deepen: Self-Efficacy Revisited
> **Difficulty:** Advanced
> **Type:** Connection

> [!flashcard] Seed 9
> **Q:** What are the three types of metacognitive knowledge according to Flavell, and what role does each play in the Forethought/Self-Reflection cycle?
> **A:** Declarative (knowing about yourself, tasks, strategies — used in Forethought to select appropriate strategies and in Self-Reflection to recognize what went wrong), Procedural (knowing how to implement strategies — used in the Performance Phase), and Conditional (knowing when and why to use strategies — used in both Forethought to select and in Self-Reflection to identify which strategy should have been used instead).
> **Source:** Part VII — Metacognitive Awareness

> [!flashcard] Seed 9
> **Q:** What is "defensive inference" in the Self-Reflection Phase, and how does it break the cycle?
>
> **A:** Defensive inference is the Self-Reflection Phase sub-process in which learners respond to negative self-evaluation by attributing failure to unmodifiable external factors (task difficulty, bad luck) or by reducing effort to protect ability self-attributions. It is contrasted with *adaptive inference* (modifying strategy and increasing appropriate effort). Defensive inference breaks the cycle by severing the Self-Reflection → Forethought link: instead of the failure experience modifying the next cycle's goals and strategies, it modifies only affect and self-protective behavior. Mastery-orientation environments and attributional feedback are the primary interventions.
>
