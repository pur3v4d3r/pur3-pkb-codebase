# Event Indexing Model

> [!definition] **Event Indexing Model** *(from [[situation-model-construction-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-29]])*
> **Definition:** A model of situation model structure proposing that readers continuously monitor and update five dimensions of the described situation: temporal, spatial, causal, intentional, and entity. Reading times increase when any dimension shifts, indicating active model maintenance. Boundary condition: the model describes competent readers; poor comprehenders show less sensitivity to dimension shifts, indicating less active model monitoring.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Provides the structural architecture for the reconstructed situation-model monitoring strategy; the five dimensions serve as the monitoring checklist for deliberate situation-model construction.
> 
> **Cross-References:** [[situation-model]], [[situation-models-in-text-comprehension]], [[mental-simulation]], [[working-memory-in-reading]]

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Kintsch and van Dijk (1978) and subsequent researchers showed that readers who were asked "inference questions" (questions requiring integration across text segments or combination with prior knowledge) performed dramatically worse than those asked "textbase questions" (questions answerable from explicit text statements) — but only when readers had read without strategic purpose. Readers given explicit instructions to construct an understanding of "how the described system works" showed dramatically improved inference question performance without losing textbase performance. This finding was replicated by Graesser, Singer, and Trabasso (1994) using longer, more complex texts.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The Kintsch/Graesser line of research consistently shows that the single greatest predictor of situation model quality is not reading ability, domain knowledge, or text difficulty — it is whether the reader is actively engaged in trying to understand *how* and *why*, rather than *what*. This is an orientation effect, not an ability effect. The implication is that most adults who "get lost in details" are not failing due to cognitive limitations; they are failing due to a suboptimal reading orientation that tracks textbase when it should be tracking situational coherence.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Karpicke and Roediger (2008) showed that students who re-read passages consistently rated their comprehension higher than students who attempted free recall of the same passages — yet performed significantly worse on subsequent tests. The re-reading students experienced *fluency* (the text felt easy to process on the second reading) and mistook this processing ease for understanding. The free recall students experienced *difficulty* (they could not retrieve much), which accurately reflected their incomplete situation models.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Maki and colleagues (1994–2000) conducted a series of studies on metacognitive accuracy in reading comprehension — the correlation between students' predicted comprehension and actual test performance. Across studies, the correlation was positive but surprisingly weak (typically around r = 0.30–0.40), particularly for inference questions. Crucially, the lowest metacognitive accuracy was found for students who had read most fluently — they were confident precisely because the reading had felt easy, not because they had built a situation model.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann, and Glaser (1994) studied students learning from worked examples in physics. Students who spontaneously explained what they were reading — generating causal accounts, bridging gaps, relating examples to principles — showed dramatically superior performance on transfer tests compared to students who read without self-explaining, even when the latter group spent more time reading. The effect was not about time on task but about the qualitative nature of the processing. Self-explanation forces situation model construction because it requires the reader to produce causal inferences that passive reading does not demand.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Slamecka and Graf's original generation effect experiments showed that words generated by participants (from partial cues) were recalled significantly better than words read passively — even when the generated words were identical to the read words. Subsequent research by Jacoby (1978) and Roediger and colleagues established that the generation advantage extends to conceptual content: generating an explanation, a paraphrase, or an inference produces substantially stronger memory for the underlying concept than reading it passively.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Taken together, the self-explanation, generation effect, and retrieval practice literatures converge on a finding that is simple to state but radical in its implications: situation models cannot be *received*; they can only be *constructed*. The cognitive work required to build a situation model — generating causal inferences, bridging implicit gaps, integrating prior knowledge — is specifically the work that passive reading allows the reader to avoid. This is the deep reason why reading efficiency strategies that reduce cognitive effort during reading (faster reading, skimming, re-reading instead of recalling) consistently produce poor knowledge retention: they are optimizing against the very cognitive load that situation model construction requires.

> [!evidence] Supporting Evidence *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Recht and Leslie (1988) studied students with high and low baseball knowledge reading a passage about a baseball game. They also divided students by reading ability. The result was stark: high-knowledge readers outperformed low-knowledge readers on comprehension regardless of reading ability. High-knowledge poor readers outperformed low-knowledge good readers. Prior knowledge provided the situational context — the partial situation model — into which text propositions could be placed, allowing high-knowledge readers to construct detailed, coherent models even when their linguistic processing was less efficient.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Situation models are indexed across five dimensions — **C**ausality, **A**gency, **T**ime, **S**pace, and **M**otivation. Comprehension proceeds smoothly when all five remain coherent across successive text segments. Comprehension breaks down — and readers "lose the thread" — when one or more dimensions becomes discontinuous without adequate bridging.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Of the five CATSM dimensions, causality is uniquely vulnerable to disruption in academic expository text, and its disruption cascades into failures of the other four. Here is why: unlike agency, time, and space, which are often explicitly marked in text (subject noun phrases, tense, spatial prepositions), causal relations are frequently *implicit*. The text says "X occurs. Y follows." The causal connection between X and Y must be inferred by the reader using prior knowledge. When prior knowledge is insufficient to support the inference, causality collapses — and with it, the coherent structure that would give the other dimensions their meaning. This is precisely why "building background knowledge first" is not just a general good practice but a *prerequisite for situation model construction* in domains where the reader lacks sufficient prior knowledge to bridge implicit causal gaps.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> [[advance-organizer|Advance Organizers]] (Ausubel, 1960) function precisely by providing a schematic frame before content is encountered. Their mechanism of action is now understood through the situation model framework: they initiate construction of a partial situation model — a frame with empty slots — that gives incoming textual propositions a structure to attach to. Without this frame, each proposition must be processed in isolation, and the coherence that would link them must be inferred entirely from within the text. Pre-reading strategies (surveying structure, writing a prediction sentence) are applied advance organizers.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The evidence on fluency and comprehension monitoring reveals a genuine tension at the heart of self-regulated reading: the phenomenological signal that readers use to judge their own comprehension — ease of processing — is systematically dissociated from the quality of the situation model they have built. A reader can process every word fluently, feel confident they understood, and have constructed virtually no situation model. This creates a structural monitoring problem: the natural feedback loop that should regulate reading effort is unreliable. This is not a peripheral finding; it is the central reason why conventional re-reading and "try harder to understand" instructions consistently fail. The reader's internal feedback system is calibrated to the wrong variable.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The Recht and Leslie finding reveals a deep structural problem: situation model construction requires prior knowledge to bridge implicit causal gaps, but readers typically encounter new content precisely *because* they lack prior knowledge of it. This creates a bootstrapping paradox — you need a partial situation model to build a situation model. The practical resolution is not to avoid reading in domains where you lack knowledge, but to *use pre-reading strategies to build the minimum prior knowledge frame* (through survey, prediction-writing, and schema activation) before content reading. This frame does not need to be detailed; it only needs to provide enough causal and structural anchors to prevent the propositions from floating in isolation.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The Textbase Trap is the specific failure mode in which a reader's fluency at textbase construction actively *prevents* situation model construction by producing a false comprehension signal. The mechanism is: automatic textbase building → ease of processing → feeling of understanding → termination of comprehension effort → no situation model constructed. The trap is not that the reader cannot build a situation model; it is that the reader receives a false signal that building one is unnecessary. This is qualitatively different from cognitive overload failure (Mechanism 1), where the reader experiences difficulty and knows they are struggling. The Textbase Trap produces confident incomprehension — readers who are certain they understood and were wrong. The Fluency Illusion is the mislabeling; the Textbase Trap is the specific mechanism by which it prevents situation model construction.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> This report proposes the Causal Spine Bottleneck as a specific mechanism within situation model construction failure: the situation model has a hierarchical structure in which the causal dimension functions as the primary organizing spine, and the other four CATSM dimensions (agency, time, space, motivation) can only achieve coherence if causal tracking is maintained. When causal tracking fails — specifically when the reader fails to generate the bridging inferences required to connect causally implicit text segments — the entire CATSM structure becomes incoherent, and detail accumulation without integration becomes inevitable. The phenomenological experience of "getting lost in details" is the reader's awareness of CATSM incoherence — not a loss of any particular detail, but a loss of the causal spine that would give the details their structural positions.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> The situation model is the cognitive gold standard for reading comprehension. It is not what a text says — it is what the text is *about*. Constructing one requires generating information beyond what the text explicitly states, integrating new content with prior knowledge, and building a coherent mental simulation of the described domain. All effective reading strategies work, ultimately, by facilitating this construction.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> The C-I model proposes that text comprehension proceeds through two distinct phases: a fast, constraint-unconstrained **construction phase** that generates an initial, partially incoherent representation drawing on text propositions and weakly associated prior knowledge; and an **integration phase** that applies a constraint-satisfaction process to the constructed representation, suppressing elements that fail to cohere with the emerging interpretation and strengthening elements that do. These two phases operate cyclically across reading — at roughly each sentence or clause boundary — rather than as two sequential passes over the entire text.

> [!analytical-insight] Key Insight *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> Constructionist Theory's specification of *which* inferences are generated automatically is the theoretical foundation for the prescriptive claim that reading strategies should explicitly target elaborative inference generation. Causal antecedent inferences (explaining why events occurred) and goal-motive inferences (explaining what agents are trying to achieve) are generated automatically because they directly serve the coherence and explanation assumptions. But elaborative depth — the richness of the situation model beyond minimal causal coherence — is *not* automatic. It requires deliberate prompting. This is why passive reading by skilled readers produces situation models that are causally coherent but mechanistically thin.











































## Practical Implications

> [!example] **Application**
> *Describe how this concept applies in practice.*

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Before reading any complex text, execute a 3–5 minute pre-reading sequence: (1) Read only the headings, subheadings, and the first and last paragraphs. (2) Write a one-sentence statement of what you predict the text argues or explains. (3) Write a specific purpose question: "I am reading this to understand \_\_\_\_\_." This frame initiates partial situation model construction before content reading begins, giving incoming propositions structural anchors.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> During reading, actively track the causal chain. In the margin or in your notes, maintain a running causal map: "X → Y → Z." Whenever a new causal claim appears, add it to the chain. Whenever the text moves to a new topic without providing a causal connector, pause and ask: *What is the causal connection between what I just read and what I am about to read?* If you cannot answer, this is a gap requiring active inference generation before proceeding.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> After every paragraph, briefly cover the text and restate the paragraph's *causal contribution* in your own words — not what it said, but what it *did* to the overall argument or explanation. "This paragraph established the mechanism by which X causes Y" rather than "This paragraph was about X and Y." The active reformulation forces situation model construction.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Within five minutes of completing a reading session, without looking at the text, write down everything you can remember — in any order, without evaluation. Then specifically attempt to reconstruct the causal spine: "What caused what, in what sequence?" This retrieval attempt both reveals gaps and strengthens the memory traces for what was successfully constructed. [[retrieval-practice|Retrieval practice]] research consistently shows this produces better long-term retention than re-reading.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **Causal Chain Annotations (C-dimension):** Use directional markers (→) to make explicit the causal relationships between described events, mechanisms, or claims. When a text implies causation without stating it, writing the causal annotation IS the bridging inference made explicit and externalized: "[Event/state A] → [Event/state B] because [your generated causal explanation]." Causal annotations directly target the dimension of the situation model that most strongly predicts comprehension quality and transfer.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **Dimensional Gap Annotations (T, S, I, E-dimensions):** When you notice that one of the Event Indexing dimensions is unclear — you have lost track of when, where, who, or why — annotate the gap explicitly rather than reading over it: "T?: unclear when this follows from [prior event]" or "I?: unclear why this agent is pursuing this goal at this point." Gap annotations make comprehension failures explicit and located, converting vague confusion into specific, addressable deficits.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **Integration Annotations:** When you recognize a connection between current content and earlier text, other texts, or prior knowledge, annotate the connection immediately with a linking label: "CONNECTS TO: [[relevant-prior-concept]]" or "CONTRADICTS: [prior claim]." These annotations externalize the lateral structure of the situation model — the connections between sub-models and between text content and world knowledge — which are the most fragile elements of an emerging situation model and the first to be lost under reading time pressure.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **Question Annotations:** The most powerful single annotation type for situation model construction. Rather than noting that something is interesting, note the question it raises: "WHY does this follow from the previous argument?" or "WHAT WOULD FALSIFY this claim?" or "HOW does this mechanism account for [counterexample you are generating]?" Question annotations activate elaborative inference generation in a way that assertive annotations do not — they create an obligation to generate the answer, which is the act of situation model construction itself.

> [!example] **Application** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **Compression Annotations:** After annotating a section, attempt to compress your marginal notes into a single sentence capturing the causal structure of the section. If the compression cannot be produced, your annotations captured detail, not structure. The compression annotation at the section end is the highest-level summary of your situation model for that section.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The interventions described above can reliably improve situation model construction quality for readers who have *some* relevant prior knowledge — enough to support basic causal inference generation. For readers with near-zero prior knowledge in a domain, the situation model construction process will be severely limited regardless of strategy, because the bridging inferences simply cannot be generated from nothing. In these cases, the appropriate first move is not better reading strategy but prior knowledge building — reading an introductory text, watching an explanatory video, or having a brief conversation with a domain expert before tackling complex primary sources.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The quality of situation model construction is constrained by text quality as well as reader strategy. Texts that are causally explicit (that mark causal relations with connectives like "because," "therefore," "which caused," "as a result") support better situation model construction than equally informative texts that present information declaratively without causal marking. Reader strategies can partially compensate for poor text design, but they cannot fully overcome it. When a text is highly dense and poorly connected, seeking a better-written source is often more efficient than attempting to reconstruct causal connections the author has not provided.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> There is a genuine tension between the cognitive demands of the situation model construction strategies described here and the cognitive capacity available for applying them. Beginning readers, readers in cognitively demanding domains, or readers who are learning multiple new strategies simultaneously may find that the strategy application itself consumes working memory resources, paradoxically *reducing* situation model construction quality. The resolution is that situation model construction strategies, like any skill, require practice to become automatized. Initial strategy application should be done with relatively accessible texts where the cognitive load from content processing is manageable.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **The Over-Annotation Trap**: The most common annotation pathology is not under-annotation but over-annotation — marking so much text that annotations cease to carry signal. Over-annotation typically co-occurs with fluency illusions: the reader feels that highlighting and marking demonstrate engagement, while this activity actually substitutes for the effortful inferential work that situation model construction requires. If annotation feels comfortable and automatic, it is almost certainly capturing textbase content rather than situation model structure. A useful heuristic: if you are annotating more than 10-15% of text content, you are annotating propositions, not architecture.

> [!warning] **Key Distinction** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **Common Annotation Mistakes:** (1) Highlighting without commentary — highlighted text is a flag that content seemed important, not a record of why it mattered or how it connects to anything. (2) Annotating as a substitute for comprehension monitoring — marking a confusing passage without articulating what is confusing does not repair the situation model gap. (3) Single-pass annotation — the most effective annotation systems involve at minimum a structural first pass (seed the situation model) and a detailed second pass (annotate dimensions and connections); single-pass annotation typically results in textbase-level notes with minimal structural capture.

> [!tension] **The Fluency-Comprehension Decoupling Problem** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> The evidence on fluency and comprehension monitoring reveals a genuine tension at the heart of self-regulated reading: the phenomenological signal that readers use to judge their own comprehension — ease of processing — is systematically dissociated from the quality of the situation model they have built. A reader can process every word fluently, feel confident they understood, and have constructed virtually no situation model. This creates a structural monitoring problem: the natural feedback loop that should regulate reading effort is unreliable. This is not a peripheral finding; it is the central reason why conventional re-reading and "try harder to understand" instructions consistently fail. The reader's internal feedback system is calibrated to the wrong variable.

> [!tension] **Comprehension Depth vs. Reading Efficiency** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Position A:** Deep comprehension — situation model construction — requires slow, effortful, generation-intensive reading. This position, supported by the generation effect and working memory capacity research, implies that reading efficiency (words per minute) and comprehension depth are in direct tension.
> 
> **Position B:** Skilled readers achieve both high reading rate and deep comprehension through automatized situation model construction, not through slower processing. Expertise in a domain allows readers to generate causal inferences rapidly and automatically, making the efficiency-comprehension tradeoff a feature of novice reading that disappears with skill.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** The evidence clearly supports Position A for novice readers in new domains and for cognitively demanding text. The evidence for Position B is real but applies primarily to readers with extensive domain expertise reading within their domain. For most adult learners in most reading contexts, the tradeoff is genuine.
> 
> **Why This Matters:** The tension determines what "better reading" means. If Position A dominates, the goal is to develop strategies that make effortful processing more efficient (not to speed up). If Position B dominates, the goal is to develop automatized situation model construction through extensive domain practice.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** Both positions are correct, but their domains of applicability are different. The practical implication for the reader of this report is Position A: develop effortful situation model construction strategies now; Position B will emerge naturally as domain expertise increases.

> [!tension] **Strategy Instruction vs. Background Knowledge Building** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Position A:** Situation model construction failure is primarily a strategy problem. Readers fail to build situation models because they lack the metacognitive awareness and strategic habits to orient toward causal tracking, active generation, and frame-before-fill construction. Strategy instruction can reliably improve situation model quality across domains and difficulty levels.
> 
> **Position B:** Situation model construction failure is primarily a prior knowledge problem. Without sufficient domain knowledge to bridge implicit causal gaps, no reading strategy can produce a coherent situation model. Strategy instruction is valuable but secondary to domain knowledge building.
> 
> **Current Evidence State:** Both positions have substantial empirical support. The evidence suggests the relationship is interactive: strategies help most when prior knowledge is moderate; when prior knowledge is very low, strategies cannot compensate; when prior knowledge is very high, strategies are less necessary.
> 
> **This Report's Stance:** Strategy and knowledge are complementary, but this report emphasizes strategies because they are more directly actionable for a reader who is actively developing reading skills. The practical recommendation is to pursue both simultaneously.

> [!tension] **Propositional vs. Analogical/Embodied Representation** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **The Tension:** Whether situation models are propositional structures (enriched networks of propositions with situational specifications) or genuinely analogical/simulation-based representations implemented in perceptual and motor systems.
> 
> **Position A — Propositional (Kintsch, 1998):** The C-I model treats situation models as formally propositional. "Simulation" is a useful metaphor, not a literal description of the representational format.
> 
> **Position B — Analogical/Embodied (Zwaan, 2004; Barsalou, 1999):** The Event Indexing Model and embodied simulation accounts propose that situation models are genuinely simulation-based, activating perceptual and motor systems. Neuroimaging evidence (somatosensory cortex activation during comprehension of action sentences) supports the analogical position.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** The embodied simulation account has gained substantially more support over the past 20 years. The scientific debate has shifted from "propositional vs. analogical" to "how much is simulation, and across which domains?"
> 
> **Why It Matters:** The representational format has direct implications for optimal reading strategies. Analogical accounts suggest that active visualization and motor imagery may specifically enhance situation model quality.
> 
> **See also:** [[mental-models]], [[mental-simulation]], [[propositional-representation]]

> [!tension] **Automatic vs. Strategic Situation Model Construction** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **The Tension:** Whether situation model construction is primarily automatic (driven by default processes in skilled reading) or primarily strategic (requiring deliberate metacognitive intervention).
> 
> **Position A — Primarily Automatic (Constructionist Theory):** Skilled readers automatically generate causal coherence-preserving inferences as a default; situation model construction is a by-product of skilled fluent reading.
> 
> **Position B — Primarily Strategic (Self-explanation literature):** Chi et al. (1994) and related research demonstrate that even skilled readers construct substantially better situation models when using deliberate self-explanation strategies, indicating that automatic processes are not sufficient for deep comprehension.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** A middle position is best supported: automatic processes produce a minimally coherent situation model; strategic processes are necessary to extend it to the depth required for inferential transfer.
> 
> **This Report's Position:** Strategic processes are necessary for situation model depth that supports transfer; automatic processes establish minimum causal coherence but not explanation-level depth.
> 
> **See also:** [[self-explanation-effect]], [[metacognitive-scaffolding]], [[automaticity]]

> [!tension] **Working Memory Capacity as Fixed vs. Functionally Expandable** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **The Tension:** Whether WMC constraints on situation model construction are stable individual difference parameters or are effectively expandable through strategy development and domain knowledge acquisition.
> 
> **Position A — Fixed:** The WMC literature has historically treated reading span as stable. Low-WMC readers are structurally disadvantaged.
> 
> **Position B — Functionally Expandable:** Cognitive load theory's schema automation account and [[verbal-efficiency-theory|Verbal Efficiency Theory]] suggest that increasing fluency and domain knowledge expands working memory *available* for situation model construction by reducing lower-level processing demands.
> 
> **Current Evidence:** WMC itself shows limited trainability, but functional WMC available for situation model construction can be expanded through strategy instruction and domain knowledge development.
> 
> **See also:** [[working-memory-capacity]], [[cognitive-load-theory]], [[reading-fluency]], [[automaticity]]

> [!open-question] **The Optimal Granularity of Situation Model Construction Pauses** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> This report recommends pausing at "causal segment" boundaries for active reconstruction. But what is the optimal granularity for such pauses? The answer likely depends on text density, prior knowledge, and the reader's working memory capacity — but no systematic research has specified the interaction.

> [!open-question] **Which Annotation Features Best Predict Situation Model Quality?** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> **The Question:** Is there empirical evidence establishing which specific annotation behaviors — causal marking, dimensional tracking, question generation, compression — most reliably predict situation model quality on inference testing and transfer tasks?
> 
> **What We Know:** Self-explanation (a form of elaborative annotation) robustly improves situation model quality. Elaborative interrogation improves retention relative to re-reading.
> 
> **What We Don't Know:** No study has directly compared annotation types (causal chains vs. dimensional flags vs. question annotations vs. compression annotations) for their specific contributions to situation model quality, controlling for time on task.
> 
> **Why It Matters:** The DIME Annotation Protocol in this report is theory-derived and well-motivated but awaits direct empirical validation.
> 
> **Research Direction:** A within-subjects reading study comparing annotation type conditions on inference posttests and transfer tasks would directly test the DIME protocol.
>

## Debates

> [!debate] **The Generalizability of the Specific Comprehender Research** *(from [[situation-model-construction-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-29]])*
> The specific comprehension difficulty literature is primarily based on children and adolescents. It is less clear whether the same clean dissociation between decoding and situation model construction is as prevalent in adult readers, or whether adult-typical reading failures show a different profile. Some researchers argue that adult poor comprehenders typically show both decoding limitations and inference generation failures, making the dissociation less clean. This dispute matters for whether the specific comprehender findings can be straightforwardly generalized to adult self-directed learners.

It is stable because it produces no negative feedback signal — the reader feels fine, the text is processing smoothly, and there is no phenomenological marker for the absence of a situation model. It persists until an external comprehension test specifically targeting the situation model reveals the gap.

> **Q:** Seed 8
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Construction-Integration model's account of why poor situation models form?

**A:** In the CI model, comprehension proceeds through cycling construction phases (building a network of propositions from text plus activated prior knowledge) and integration phases (refining the network through spreading activation to produce coherence). Poor situation models form when the integration phase fails — when the spreading-activation convergence that should produce a coherent situation model does not reach a coherent outcome. This failure is most commonly caused by inadequate prior knowledge (insufficient integration constraints) or failure to generate the elaborative inferences that bridge propositions to a world-representation.

> **Q:** Seed 10
> **A:** **Q:** What specific monitoring practice does the reconstructed understanding recommend that the received view never recommends?

> **Q:** Seed 5 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Generation Constraint on situation model construction?

> **A:** The Generation Constraint states that situation models cannot be received through passive reading — they can only be produced through active construction. The cognitive work of generating causal inferences, bridging implicit gaps, and integrating prior knowledge is specifically the work that passive reading allows readers to avoid. This is why re-reading fails to repair comprehension and why post-reading free recall significantly outperforms re-reading for retention: retrieval practice forces the generative work that passive reading bypasses.

> **Q:** Seed 6 — Application
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry, and what practical reading intervention does it most directly support?
> 
> **A:** The Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry states that situation model construction is directionally one-way: a structural frame can accept and organize details, but an accumulation of unframed details cannot generate a structural frame. This directly supports pre-reading scaffolding as a prerequisite (not merely a helpful addition) to content reading. Scanning structure, writing a prediction sentence, and formulating a purpose question before reading build the partial structural frame into which content-level propositions can attach. Without this frame, proposition accumulation produces a textbase but not a situation model.

> **Q:** Seed 7 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does the generation effect in memory research (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) relate to the self-explanation effect in reading comprehension (Chi et al., 1989)?
> 
> **A:** Both establish the same structural principle — the Generation Constraint — from different methodological directions. Slamecka & Graf showed that words actively generated by participants were remembered better than identical words passively read, demonstrating the memory advantage of active production over passive reception. Chi et al. showed that students who explained worked examples during study — generating causal accounts rather than reading passively — built substantially better knowledge representations. Both converge on the claim that generation (producing new content from partial cues) is the mechanism that forces active encoding and model construction.
>

## Protocols & Methods

> [!protocol] **The Situation-Model Construction Protocol (Per Reading Session)** *(from [[situation-model-construction-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-29]])*
> **Context:** Use when reading any complex, non-trivial text where genuine understanding (not merely recognition) is the goal. Particularly important when reading material that will need to be applied, discussed, or integrated with other knowledge.
> 
> **Steps:**
> 
> **Before Reading (2-5 minutes):**
> 1. Preview structure (headings, intro paragraph, concluding paragraph) to build skeletal situation model
> 2. Generate 3-5 questions from headings: convert "The Mechanism of X" to "What is the mechanism of X and why does it work that way?"
> 3. State current belief in 1-2 sentences: "I currently believe that X because Y." This is the anchor against which updates will be measured.
> 
> **During Reading (applied continuously):**
> 4. After every section: state in one sentence what the section contributed to the…

> [!protocol] **The Full Pre-Reading Frame Protocol** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Context:** Use before reading any complex expository text (academic articles, technical chapters, analytical essays). Takes 3–7 minutes.
> 
> **Derived From:** Phase II (Advance Organizer function), Phase V (Implication 1), Frame-Before-Fill Asymmetry (Phase VI).
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Survey Structure (2 min):** Read only headings, subheadings, the opening paragraph, and the closing paragraph of each major section. You are reading for *architecture*, not content.
> 2. **Write a Prediction Sentence (1 min):** Complete this sentence without looking at the text: "This text is going to argue/explain/demonstrate \_\_\_\_." This activates prior knowledge and initiates the structural frame.
> 3. **Set a Purpose Question (1 min):** Write: "I am reading this to understand \_\_\_\_." The more specific, the better. "I want to understand the causal mechanism by which X produces Y" is better than "I want to understand X."
> 4. **Activate Prior Knowledge (1–2 min):** Write 3–5 things you already know or believe about the topic. These are the schema anchors onto which incoming propositions will attach.
> 
> **Success Criteria:** You should be able to sketch the text's approximate structure (its major divisions and their logical relationship) before beginning content reading.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** Rushing this step. The 3–7 minutes here prevent 30 minutes of confused rereading later. The prediction sentence feels uncertain — that uncertainty is precisely what active reading will resolve.

> [!protocol] **The Causal Spine Tracking Protocol** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> **Context:** Use during reading of any text with an argument or mechanistic explanation. Requires a notebook or annotation margin.
> 
> **Derived From:** Phase IV (Mechanism 3: Causal Spine Bottleneck), Phase V (Implication 4).
> 
> **Steps:**
> 1. **Maintain a running causal chain** in your notes: "X → Y → Z." Each time you encounter an explicit or implicit causal claim, add it to the chain.
> 2. **At every paragraph break**, ask: "What is the causal relationship between this paragraph and the previous one?" If you cannot answer, this is a gap requiring active inference generation.
> 3. **Mark causal gap points** in the margin with a "?" — these are locations where the text does not explicitly connect, and you must bridge them yourself.
> 4. **Generate bridging inferences** at gap points: "Given what I know about the mechanism, the connection must be \_\_\_\_." Write this in the margin. A tentative inference is always better than a gap.
> 
> **Success Criteria:** At the end of the reading, you can write the complete causal chain from memory — a 3–7-step sequence of causally connected claims that captures the text's core mechanism or argument.
> 
> **Common Pitfalls:** Tracking only explicit causal language ("because," "therefore") and missing implicit causal relationships. The most important gaps are often where the text assumes background knowledge the reader doesn't have.

> [!protocol] **Pre-Reading Situation Model Seeding Protocol (derived from C-I Model + Advance Organizer theory)** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> *Purpose: construct a low-resolution situation model before full-text reading, reducing working memory demands during the reading itself.*
> 
> **Step 1 — Scenario Priming (2-3 min):**
> Write one sentence: *What situation, mechanism, event sequence, or domain is this text going to help me understand?* This is a scenario question, not a topic question. It should name the referent world, not the text's subject heading.
> 
> **Step 2 — Structural Survey (5-10 min):**
> Read, in order: abstract or introduction, all section headings and subheadings, conclusion. Do not read body paragraphs. Goal: extract the causal skeleton — the main claims and the logical connectives between them.
> 
> **Step 3 — Prior Knowledge Activation (2-3 min):**
> Write 3-5 sentences: what you already know about the described situation or domain, including what causes what, what the main mechanisms are, and what the main controversies are. This is your initial situation model.
> 
> **Step 4 — Prediction Generation:**
> Write two specific predictions about what the text will argue, demonstrate, or claim. Predictions commit you to an existing situation model that the text will confirm, disconfirm, or complicate.

> [!protocol] **The DIME Annotation Protocol (derived from Event Indexing Model — original synthesis)** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> *Purpose: externalize the situation model across all five situational dimensions through targeted annotation of structure rather than content.*
> 
> **The Five Annotation Types:**
> 
> **C — Causal annotations (highest priority):** Use → to mark causal relationships. When the text implies causation without stating it, write the causal annotation as the generated inference: "[A] → [B] because [your causal explanation]." This is the most critical annotation type.
> 
> **T — Temporal annotations:** Mark when the temporal sequence is unclear: "T?: unclear when this follows [prior event]." Or confirm sequence: "T✓: this follows [timeframe] after [prior event]."
> 
> **S — Spatial annotations:** For texts describing physical mechanisms, spatial configurations, or structural relationships: "S: [brief spatial description or diagram]."
> 
> **I — Intentional annotations:** Mark the goals and motivations of agents (narrative) or the argumentative purposes of authors/theorists (expository): "I: [Agent/author] is trying to [goal] because [your explanation]."
> 
> **E — Entity annotations:** Track key actors' state changes: "E[Name]: [current state, changed from prior state]."
> 
> **Quality check:** After each section, verify all five dimensions are represented (or deliberately absent for good reason). Unmonitored dimensions are the typical location of situation model gaps.

> [!protocol] **The Annotation Threshold Decision Framework** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> *Purpose: determine when annotation is worth interrupting reading flow.*
> 
> **Stop and annotate when ANY of the following:**
> 1. A causal mechanism has just been explained — mark with C-annotation
> 2. A foundational distinction has been drawn that will bear weight throughout the text
> 3. A coherence failure has been detected — flag the dimensional gap explicitly
> 4. An integration opportunity has been recognized — annotate the connection immediately
> 5. A claim has been made that requires justification the text has not provided — mark "WHY?"
> 
> **Do not annotate for:** Interesting but structurally peripheral content; examples and illustrations unless the example IS the argument; material already integrated from prior knowledge.
> 
> **Target density:** 10-15% of text content annotated. If exceeding this, you are annotating propositions, not architecture.

> [!protocol] **The Section-Ending Compression Protocol** *(from [[situation-models-text-comprehension-foundational-report-2026-03-29]])*
> *Purpose: convert annotations into situation model elements; diagnose model quality at section boundaries.*
> 
> **After each major section:**
> 
> **Step 1:** Close the text.
> 
> **Step 2 — Produce the three-part narrative:** "This section is about [X], because [Y], which means [Z]." X = topic; Y = causal grounding (why X is so, or why the author claims what they claim); Z = implication (what follows for the broader argument or for your understanding).
> 
> **Step 3 — Diagnose:**
> - All three parts: situation model functioning for this section.
> - X but not Y/Z: textbase, not situation model. Reread targeting causal structure.
> - Cannot produce X clearly: coherence failure. Return to text; targeted rereading at section opening and closing.
> 
> **Step 4:** Write the compression as a margin annotation at the section end. This is the highest-level situation model summary for this section.

## Concrete Examples

> [!example] **The Cascade in Concrete Terms** *(from [[situation-model-construction-focused-analysis-2026-03-29]])*
> Imagine reading a cognitive psychology chapter on the relationship between working memory capacity and reading comprehension. The text presents: (1) working memory capacity research, (2) reading comprehension research, (3) a correlation finding, (4) a proposal about the mechanism. A reader in the failure cascade will successfully build a textbase: "Working memory capacity correlates with reading comprehension. The mechanism might involve..." But the situation model requires the reader to actively generate: *Why* would working memory capacity matter for reading comprehension specifically? *What is the actual process by which WM supports comprehension?* *What would this mean for how to help low-WM readers?* These are inference questions. The cascade reader never asks them. They finish the chapter able to recall the correlation finding and unable to explain anything about the underlying process.
