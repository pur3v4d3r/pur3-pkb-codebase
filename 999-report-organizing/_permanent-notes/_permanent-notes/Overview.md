## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] Overview
> This report provides a comprehensive, graduate-level analysis of [[constructivist-learning-theory]] — one of the most influential and widely debated frameworks in [[educational-psychology]] and [[cognitive-psychology-foundations]]. Constructivism holds a deceptively simple core claim: learners do not passively receive knowledge from the environment but actively construct it through their engagement with experience, prior knowledge, and social interaction. This claim has been elaborated by thinkers as different as [[jean-piaget]], who located construction in the individual organism's encounter with physical reality, and [[lev-vygotsky]], who located it in the social and cultural fabric of human interaction. The report traces constructivism's philosophical origins through its multiple theoretical traditions — cognitive, social, radical, and situated — examines the cognitive mechanisms through which construction occurs, analyzes the instructional models constructivism has generated, engages seriously with the guidance controversy and empirical critiques, and synthesizes constructivism's relationship with adjacent cognitive science frameworks including [[schema-theory]], [[cognitive-load-theory]], [[self-regulated-learning]], and [[metacognition]]. The report concludes with an assessment of constructivism's enduring contributions, its genuine limitations, and the conditions under which constructivist and direct instructional approaches are best deployed. Over 10,000 words.

> [!evidence] Overview
> This report examines the relationship between working memory — the brain's severely capacity-limited cognitive workspace — and Personal Knowledge Management (PKM) systems, arguing that the design of effective PKM infrastructure should be grounded in the science of [[cognitive-offloading]]. Working memory's architectural constraints, established across seven decades of cognitive science research from [[george-miller]]'s foundational capacity studies through [[alan-baddeley]]'s multicomponent model and [[Nelson-Cowan]]'s embedded-processes framework, create irreducible bottlenecks in cognition that no amount of training fully eliminates. [[cognitive-offloading]] — the deliberate externalisation of cognitive operations onto physical or digital environments — offers a scientifically grounded response to these constraints. When understood through the lens of [[extended-mind-theory]], the boundaries of the cognitive system dissolve: a well-designed [[personal-knowledge-base]] is not merely a storage supplement but an extension of the mind's working architecture. This report develops seven core design principles for offloading-optimised PKM, grounded in [[cognitive-load-theory]], the extended mind thesis, and empirical offloading research, while also interrogating counter-perspectives including cognitive dependency risks, the replacement-vs.-augmentation debate, and the boundary conditions under which offloading enhances rather than diminishes cognitive capability. The report concludes that treating [[personal-knowledge-management]] systems as cognitive prosthetics — rather than as mere archives — is both epistemically justified by the evidence and practically transformative for the practitioner.
> *— [[pkm-working-memory-cognitive-offloading-foundational-report-2026-04-05_report]]*

> [!evidence] Overview
> Relationship Motivation Theory (RMT) is the sixth and most recent mini-theory within [[self-determination-theory]] (SDT), formally articulated by [[edward-deci]] and [[richard-ryan]] in 2014. RMT addresses a gap that earlier SDT frameworks left partially unresolved: the question of what motivates people to pursue, maintain, and invest in close interpersonal relationships, and how the quality of that motivation shapes both relationship functioning and psychological well-being. While prior SDT mini-theories addressed [[relatedness-need]] primarily as a context for autonomous functioning — the supportive social conditions under which other needsare met — RMT elevates relatedness as an object of motivation in its own right. People do not merely need to feel connected; they are motivated toward connection in qualitatively different ways, and those motivational qualities produce systematically different relational and personal outcomes.
>
> This report provides a comprehensive analytical treatment of RMT across nine integrated sections. It begins by situating RMT within the historical and intellectual landscape of motivational theory, then presents the full theoretical architecture of RMT and its integration within the broader SDT framework. Subsequent sections investigate the autonomous-controlled motivational continuum as applied to relationship pursuit and maintenance, followed by a deep analysis of perceived partner responsiveness as the central mechanistic pathway through which high-quality relational motivation operates. The report then interrogates the apparent paradox of [[autonomy-relatedness-integration]] — the tension between self-determination and interpersonal connection that classical theories treated as inherently opposed — before tracing the developmental origins of relational motivation through [[attachment-theory]] and conditional regard histories. Two sections examine the need satisfaction ecology of close relationships and the evidence base for RMT's core propositions, including methodological considerations and boundary conditions. The report concludes with a treatment of applications across clinical, educational, and organizational contexts, followed by far transfer analysis and a comprehensive appendix supporting ongoing PKB development.
> *— [[relationship-motivation-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-01_report]]*
