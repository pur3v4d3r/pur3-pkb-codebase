> [!schema-activation] Prior Knowledge Bridge: What You Already Know About Memory
> Before engaging this report, calibrate your existing schema. You likely hold intuitions about memory that this theory directly addresses — and may complicate. Consider: when you remember something well, what made it stick? You may already suspect that "thinking harder" about something helps, that rote repetition feels less effective than genuine understanding, or that studying while distracted produces shallow results. These are informal expressions of the levels of processing thesis. The theory formalizes exactly these intuitions, but also reveals where they break down. You may also hold the folk model of memory as a kind of recording device or storage system — the [[atkinson-shiffrin-multi-store-model]] gave this intuition scientific form. The levels framework challenges it. If you have studied [[cognitive-load-theory]] or [[schema-theory]], you will find productive intersections here. If you know [[desirable-difficulties]] or [[retrieval-practice]], this report provides much of their theoretical backstory. The guiding question for this report: **What is it about the act of encoding — not the time spent, not the intention to remember, not the mere repetition — that determines how much we retain?**
> *— [[levels-of-processing-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-07_report]]*

> [!schema-activation] Prior Knowledge Bridge: What You Already Know About Memory
> Before engaging with this report, consider what you already know about memory from experience:
>
> You have noticed that some information seems to disappear almost immediately (what did you just read at the top of this page before looking away?), while other memories persist for decades (your childhood home, the melody of a song learned in school). You've experienced the frustration of knowing you know something but being unable to retrieve it — the tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon. You've been surprised to discover that a vivid memory was wrong in some detail.
>
> These everyday observations map directly onto the theoretical territory this report covers. Your experience of momentary mental holding corresponds to [[working-memory]]. Your episodic memories of personal events live in [[episodic-memory]], while your knowledge of factual information (that the capital of France is Paris, that water is H₂O) resides in [[semantic-memory]]. The fallibility you've experienced reflects [[reconstructive-memory]] — the discovery that remembering is not playback but re-creation.
>
> If you have encountered [[cognitive-load-theory]], [[schema-theory]], or [[spaced-repetition]], you have already been working with downstream applications of memory systems research. This report will provide the mechanistic foundations that explain why those applications work.
>
> **Guiding Question for this Report:** How do the architectural properties of working memory and long-term memory — their capacities, formats, encoding mechanisms, and retrieval dynamics — constrain and enable human learning, and how can understanding these properties be used to design more effective intellectual practice?
> *— [[memory-systems-working-memory-and-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-07_report]]*
