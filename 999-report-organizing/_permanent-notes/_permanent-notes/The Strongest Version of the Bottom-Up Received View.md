## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] The Strongest Version of the Bottom-Up Received View
> The most compelling version of the bottom-up account begins with an undeniable empirical finding: in early reading development, the single strongest predictor of comprehension is **[[decoding|decoding skill]]**. Readers who cannot efficiently convert print to phonological representation cannot comprehend, because their [[working-memory|working memory]] is fully consumed by the mechanics of word recognition. When those mechanics become automatic — when [[lexical-automaticity|lexical automaticity]] is achieved — working memory capacity is freed for higher-level processing, and comprehension improves almost as a consequence. The [[simple-view-of-reading|Simple View of Reading]] (Gough & Tunmer, 1986) formalizes this: Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Linguistic Comprehension, where both components are necessary and neither alone is sufficient.
>
> Furthermore, most linguistic comprehension draws on processes that operate automatically and implicitly: syntactic parsing, semantic integration, anaphora resolution. A fluent reader integrating "The dog bit the man. He went to the hospital." does not consciously reason about who "He" refers to — this referential resolution happens automatically, within milliseconds, below the threshold of conscious awareness. If this much happens automatically, the view goes, why should we expect the larger-scale assembly of meaning to be fundamentally different?
>
> **Why this version matters**: If this view were correct, the primary leverage point for improving comprehension would always be at the decoding and vocabulary level. Improving someone's word recognition would reliably improve their comprehension, and strategic training would be secondary. For early readers and those with genuine decoding deficits, this prediction is accurate and highly useful.
> *— [[situation-model-construction-dialectical-re-examination-2026-03-29_report]]*
