> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the difference between self-efficacy and self-esteem, and why does this distinction matter for inner speech?
> **A:** Self-efficacy is a domain-specific, task-specific, future-oriented *capability judgment* ("can I do this?"). Self-esteem is a global evaluation of self-worth. The distinction matters because efficacy-undermining inner speech targets specific tasks and domains, making it more amenable to reframing, while self-esteem threats are more global and harder to address through domain-specific vocabulary.
> **Source:** Phase II
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate

> **Q:** How does elaborative interrogation differ from self-explanation in content target and mechanism?
> **A:** EI targets factual-relational propositions ("X has property Y") and produces causal accounts of why facts hold; self-explanation targets procedural or causal content and produces accounts of how processes work. The mechanisms partially overlap (both involve generative processing) but EI more directly depends on prior semantic knowledge for answer generation.

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the difference between a textbase and a situation model? Give a concrete reading example.
>
> **A:** A textbase is the propositional representation of what the text asserts, stripped of exact wording — it supports verbatim recall and close-paraphrase recognition. A situation model is the integrated representation of the subject matter itself, constructed by fusing textbase propositions with prior knowledge schemas — it supports inference, transfer, and novel application. Example: a reader who can recall that "photosynthesis converts light into glucose" has a textbase; one who can explain why plants use glucose as an energy carrier rather than storing ATP directly has a situation model.
>
> **Source:** Phase II
>

> **Q:** What is the critical difference between autonomous motivation and controlled motivation in SDT?
> **A:** Autonomous motivation involves volitional engagement — acting from self-endorsed interest, values, or identity. Controlled motivation involves compliance — acting from external pressure (external regulation) or internalised guilt/self-worth contingencies (introjection). Both can produce behaviour, but autonomous motivation produces deeper processing, greater persistence, and enhanced well-being.

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the devaluation test, and what does it distinguish?
> **A:** An experimental procedure in which the reward associated with a behavior is made unpalatable *after* the behavior is established. If the behavior persists after devaluation, it is habitual (stimulus-bound); if it ceases, it is goal-directed (outcome-sensitive). It is the gold-standard method for distinguishing habits from goal-directed actions.
> **Source:** Phase II / Phase IV
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Tags:** #habit-formation, #goal-directed-action, #neuroscience, #methodology

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** How does the Nelson-Narens framework distinguish monitoring from control?
> **A:** Monitoring is the causal relationship flowing from the object-level to the meta-level (information about ongoing cognitive processing flows upward). Control is the reverse: the meta-level issues commands that modify object-level processing. They are analytically distinct but empirically interleaved.
> **Source:** Phase II; Phase IV
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the difference between declarative, procedural, and conditional metacognitive knowledge?
> **A:** Declarative = knowing *that* (a strategy exists and what it does); Procedural = knowing *how* (the operational steps for executing a strategy); Conditional = knowing *when and why* (the contextual conditions under which a strategy is effective or ineffective). Conditional knowledge is the most sophisticated, most domain-specific, and most frequently neglected in instruction.
> **Source:** Phase II.2
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Tags:** #metacognitive-knowledge #declarative #procedural #conditional

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the difference between pure incidental acquisition, enhanced incidental acquisition, and intentional acquisition? Why does the distinction matter practically?
>
> **A:** *Pure incidental acquisition*: learning a word as an unintentional by-product of reading, with minimal special attention to unfamiliar words. *Enhanced incidental acquisition*: noticing unfamiliar words consciously, pausing to infer meaning from context and morphology, with reading comprehension as the primary goal. *Intentional acquisition*: deliberately studying vocabulary as a primary goal. The distinction matters practically because the three modes produce different acquisition rates (pure < enhanced << intentional for individual words), different disruptions to reading flow (pure = none; intentional = significant), and suit different vocabulary domains (pure for high-frequency words; intentional for low-frequency/technical vocabulary).
>
> **Source:** Phase II, Incidental vs. Intentional Acquisition section
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Type:** Distinction
> **Tags:** #vocabulary-acquisition, #incidental-learning, #deliberate-practice, #reading

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the key difference between a process goal and an outcome goal in SRL, and why does it matter for self-reflection?
> **A:** A process goal specifies a learning strategy or quality of engagement; an outcome goal specifies an end product or metric of completion. Process goals create evaluable criteria for self-reflection — "did I execute this strategy, and how effectively?" — whereas outcome goals yield only binary evaluation — "done / not done" — which is informationally poor for learning improvement.
> **Source:** Phase II
> **Difficulty:** Intermediate
> **Tags:** #srl, #goal-setting, #process-goals, #self-reflection

> [!flashcard] Seed 2 — Distinction
> **Q:** What is the difference between vocabulary breadth and vocabulary depth?
> **A:** Breadth = how many words one knows at some level. Depth = how well one knows those words across all dimensions (form, meaning, use, collocational range). Breadth can be large with many shallowly-known words; depth refers to the richness of knowledge for individual words.
> **Source:** Phase II, Conceptual Foundations
