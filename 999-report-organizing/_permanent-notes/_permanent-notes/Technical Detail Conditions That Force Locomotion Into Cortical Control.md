## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] Technical Detail: Conditions That Force Locomotion Into Cortical Control
> The following conditions shift walking from predominantly subcortical (automatic, cognitively free) to predominantly cortical (controlled, CE-consuming):
>
> 1. **Novel terrain:** Walking on surfaces the walker has not habituated to — stairs, uneven ground, slippery surfaces, narrow passages
> 2. **Obstacle encounters:** Navigating around pets, furniture rearrangement, other people in the walking path
> 3. **Speed changes:** Especially deceleration and acceleration, which require basal-ganglia-mediated motor plan updating
> 4. **Turning:** Direction reversals at the end of a pacing path require brief executive engagement — more so for tight turns than wide loops (this is why walking in circles is more automatic than walking back-and-forth for many people)
> 5. **Postural challenges:** Carrying objects, fatigue-induced balance instability
> 6. **Dual-task complexity escalation:** When the reading task suddenly becomes very demanding (e.g., encountering a conceptually difficult paragraph), the executive monitoring system may briefly reassert control over walking as part of a general "emergency executive mobilization" — this can manifest as the walker **stopping walking** during a particularly difficult passage (a well-documented phenomenon in dual-task research known as "prioritization stopping")
> 7. **Environmental monitoring demands:** Hearing a noise, seeing movement in peripheral vision, awareness of another person in the space
>
> Each of these conditions **transiently increases the CE cost of walking**, which **transiently increases the dual-task cost on reading.** For a habituated indoor walker in a controlled environment, most of these conditions occur rarely. **Turning points** (condition 4) are the most frequent of these disruptions and represent the most consistent (though brief) dual-task cost peaks.
> *— [[walking-academic-reading-working-memory-deep-dive-2026-04-13_report]]*
