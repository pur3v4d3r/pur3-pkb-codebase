> Attribution theory begins with the recognition that causal explanation is a selective, event-triggered cognitive process — activated most reliably by unexpected outcomes, negative results, and personally significant events. The process is purposive: it serves the forward-looking function of guiding future action, not merely the backward-looking function of explaining the past. The asymmetry of activation — more causal analysis following failure than success — creates conditions of inherent vulnerability to distortion, a structural feature that shapes much of the subsequent theory.
> *— [[attribution-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-19]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 1 Summary
> Working memory is a active cognitive workspace — not a passive buffer — where information is temporarily maintained and manipulated to support ongoing cognition. The construct emerged to resolve theoretical anomalies in the Atkinson-Shiffrin multi-store model, particularly the neuropsychological evidence that short-term memory damage does not prevent long-term learning. Working memory matters because (1) it is the immediate site of cognitive work during learning and reasoning, (2) individual differences in working memory capacity predict a wide range of complex cognitive outcomes, and (3) understanding its architecture provides actionable insight for instructional design, metacognitive strategy selection, and cognitive development.
> *— [[baddeley-model-of-working-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> The visible asymmetry between expert and novice performance is not primarily a matter of speed or analytic power but of perceptual ontology — what counts, for each observer, as a unit of analysis. Experts encode domain-typical material into larger units that allow working memory to operate efficiently on configurations that would overwhelm a novice. > [!section-summary] Section 1 Summary
> The diagnostic phenomenon for metacognitive reading research is silent comprehension failure: text proceeds smoothly, the reader feels they have understood, but no coherent representation has been built. The architecture that produces this phenomenon involves a separation between object-level reading processes (decoding, parsing, proposition assembly) and meta-level monitoring (tracking whether the assembly hangs together), with the monitoring signal depending heavily on processing fluency — a cue that is correlated with comprehension imperfectly and that can be produced by surface factors with no comprehension content. Silent failures cluster at topic transitions and inferential junctures, the very moments when monitoring is most needed.

> [!section-summary] Section 1 Summary
> Pintrich's framework emerged from a disciplinary fracture between cognitive and motivational research traditions that he was uniquely positioned to repair, and its distinctive contribution lies in extending the monitoring-control logic of metacognition across four areas — cognition, motivation, behavior, and context — rather than treating motivation as merely a precondition for cognitive regulation. > [!section-summary] Section 1 Summary
> Three interlocking problems establish the case for external metacognitive scaffolding. First, metacognitive monitoring is systematically unreliable, particularly among novice learners, creating a bootstrapping problem in which those who most need self-regulation are least able to provide it. Second, the fluency illusion causes learners to systematically overestimate comprehension, leading to maladaptive study strategies and cascading attribution errors. Third, cognitive load constraints mean that the conditions requiring the most metacognitive regulation are the conditions under which regulation is most likely to degrade. Together, these problems constitute not merely an inconvenience but a structural limitation of unaided cognition — one that external scaffolding is uniquely positioned to address.
