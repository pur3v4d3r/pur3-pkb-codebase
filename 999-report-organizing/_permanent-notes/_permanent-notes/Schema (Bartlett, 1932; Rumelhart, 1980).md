# Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980)

> [!definition] Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980)
> A cognitive structure in long-term memory that organizes knowledge about a domain, pattern, event type, or procedure in a form that supports rapid recognition, inference, and application. Schemas function by pattern-completion: when activated, they fill in unspecified details from stored regularities, enabling the rapid contextual interpretation of novel instances. In CLT, schema acquisition is the primary goal of learning; schemas effectively reduce working memory load by allowing familiar configurational patterns to be treated as single chunks rather than multiple individual elements.
>
> **Boundary:** A schema is not a copy of experience but a structured abstraction that captures regularities across experiences. Schemas guide perception and inference in ways that can cause systematic errors (e.g., filling in expected details that were absent) — this is not a bug but an inherent feature of the compression that enables cognitive efficiency.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Schema formation is the ultimate goal that PKM practices should serve; the CLT analysis evaluates PKM practices by whether they promote or impede schema formation in biological long-term memory.
>
> **See also:** [[schema-theory]], [[chunking]], [[long-term-memory]], [[transfer-of-learning]]

> [!evidence] Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980)
> A schema is a cognitive structure — an organised, generic representation of knowledge about a type of object, situation, event, or procedure — stored in long-term memory. Schemas function as interpretive frames that guide the encoding and retrieval of new information by providing default assumptions, expected slots to be filled, and inferences about unstated relationships. During reading, schemas are activated by textual cues and serve as the background against which textual information is interpreted and integrated.
>
> **Boundary:** Schemas are NOT fixed templates or rigid scripts. Rumelhart (1980) characterised schemas as "data structures for representing the generic concepts stored in memory" — they are probabilistic, prototype-like structures that generate expectations while remaining flexible. A schema can be violated, partially instantiated, or restructured through new experience.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Schema activation is the primary mechanism through which prior knowledge is recruited during reading. The quality and specificity of the schemas a reader brings to an academic text largely determines how much inference generation, situation model integration, and deep comprehension will be achievable.
>

> [!evidence] Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980)
> An organized knowledge structure representing the typical features, relationships, and sequences associated with a category of experience. Schemas guide encoding, organize storage, support inference, and enable rapid comprehension, but also introduce systematic distortion when schematic expectations are mistaken for genuine memories.
>
> **Boundary:** A schema is not an individual memory but a higher-order organizational framework that shapes the encoding, storage, and retrieval of individual memories. Not all knowledge is schema-organized; isolated facts and novel experiences may initially lack schematic support.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Schema theory is central to understanding both the constructive strengths and the distortion vulnerabilities of LTM, and is the mechanism through which cognitive load theory explains expertise development.

> [!evidence] Schema (Bartlett, 1932; Rumelhart, 1980)
> A [[schema-theory|schema]] is an organized knowledge structure in [[long-term-memory|long-term memory]] that represents a category, concept, event type, or procedure, and that guides the interpretation, encoding, and retrieval of new information. Schemas are not passive storage containers but active processing structures: they direct attention toward schema-relevant features, fill in missing information through default values, and assimilate new experiences by modifying existing representations or constructing new ones. [[schema-construction|Schema construction]] — the building of new schemas — and [[schema-automation|schema automation]] — the process by which well-practiced schemas operate with minimal conscious effort — are the two fundamental learning mechanisms in [[cognitive-load-theory|cognitive load theory]].
>
> **Boundary:** Schemas are not identical to concepts or categories; they are richer, incorporating relational structure, default values, and procedural knowledge. A schema for "academic conference" includes not only the concept but expectations about sequence, roles, spatial layout, and social norms.
>
> **See also:** [[schema-construction]], [[schema-automation]], [[schema-theory-and-knowledge-organization]], [[Schema-Theory-Bartlett,-Rumelhart]]
