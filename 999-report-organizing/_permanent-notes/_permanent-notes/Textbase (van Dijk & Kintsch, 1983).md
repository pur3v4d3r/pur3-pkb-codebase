# Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)

> [!definition] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> The propositional representation of the semantic content of the text — the web of ideas and assertions that the text makes, stripped of exact wording but preserving the relationships between propositions. The textbase is what allows readers to recognize paraphrase, answer direct comprehension questions, and recall "what the text said." Coherent textbase construction requires connecting propositions through referential and causal connectives already explicit in the text.
>
> **Boundary:** Crucially, the textbase does not incorporate the reader's own world knowledge or prior conceptual structures. It represents what the *text* asserts, not what the reader *understands*. A reader can construct a textbase of a passage about quantum mechanics without understanding quantum mechanics.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** A reader operating primarily at the textbase level can answer literal comprehension questions (questions answerable from the text) but struggles with inference questions (questions requiring integration with world knowledge).
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the level that re-reading, note-taking, and highlighting predominantly operate at. The standard passive reading strategies that EI outperforms are mostly textbase-level strategies.
>
> **See also:** [[textbase]], [[propositional-representation]], [[shallow-processing]]

> [!evidence] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> A propositional representation of the *ideas expressed by the text* — the logical content of the sentences, stripped of their exact wording. The textbase preserves meaning but not form: you remember that "the experiment showed that reward reduced intrinsic motivation," not the precise sentence used to express this.
>
> **Boundary:** The textbase represents what the *text said*, not what the *situation* described by the text is like. A reader with a rich textbase can answer questions about the text's explicit content but may fail questions that require inference or integration across the text.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** You are primarily building a textbase when you can accurately summarize a passage using different words, but find it difficult to answer "why did this happen?" or "what would follow from this?"
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Most adult readers reading at full speed build primarily textbase representations. The textbase feels like understanding, which is precisely what makes the failure to build a situation model so insidious.
>

> [!evidence] Textbase (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983)
> The textbase is a propositional representation of the semantic content of the text — the network of propositions that can be derived from the text through semantic analysis, stripped of surface linguistic variation. A proposition, in the technical sense employed here, is the smallest unit of meaningful content that can receive a truth value. It is represented as a predicate-argument structure: e.g., FAIL(TREATY) or CAUSE(MUTUAL-DISTRUST, FAIL(TREATY)). The textbase is a network of such propositions connected by coherence relations — reference (shared arguments), causation, contrast, and elaboration — that spans the semantic content of the entire text.
>
> **Boundary:** The textbase represents what the text *asserted*, not what the described situation *is like*. A complete textbase supports recall of the text's claims and can sustain answers to text-explicit questions. It does not, however, necessarily support the generation of text-implicit inferences, the application of knowledge to novel problems, or the explanation of mechanisms underlying described events. A reader with a rich textbase but no situation model can answer "what did the text say?" but struggles consistently with "what does this mean in practice?" or "why is this the case?"
>
> **Common Misconception:** The most consequential misconception in applied reading practice is conflating textbase-level comprehension with genuine understanding. The ability to summarize a text's propositional content — even in one's own words — does not demonstrate situation model construction if the summary merely recombines the text's explicit propositions without generating inferentially derived content. Paraphrase tests are textbase tests, not situation model tests.
>
