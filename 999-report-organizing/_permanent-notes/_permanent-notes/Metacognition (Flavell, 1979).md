# Metacognition (Flavell, 1979)

> [!definition] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979)
> The set of capacities comprising (1) knowledge about one's own cognitive processes, abilities, and limitations; (2) awareness of the cognitive demands posed by different tasks; (3) knowledge of strategies for managing cognitive effort; and (4) the active regulation of one's thinking through planning, monitoring, and evaluating. Critically, metacognition is NOT simply "thinking carefully" — it is specifically thinking *about* one's thinking, standing in a second-order relationship to one's own cognitive processes. The mere experience of difficulty does not constitute metacognition; recognizing that you are experiencing difficulty, diagnosing its source, and adjusting your strategy does.

> [!evidence] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979)
> Metacognition refers to one's knowledge and regulation of one's cognitive processes and products. It encompasses: (a) *metacognitive knowledge* — what one knows about one's own cognitive tendencies, the nature of cognitive tasks, and the effectiveness of cognitive strategies; and (b) *metacognitive regulation* — the executive processes by which one monitors ongoing cognitive activity and exercises control interventions based on that monitoring. Metacognition is thus not a single skill but a family of related capacities unified by their object: cognition itself as the target of cognitive processing.
>
> **Boundary:** Metacognition is not general intelligence, conscientiousness, or academic self-concept, though it correlates with each. A learner with above-average IQ but poor metacognitive monitoring may learn less efficiently than a learner with average IQ and excellent monitoring skills. Metacognition is also not equivalent to mindfulness, which is primarily attentional and affective rather than regulatory and strategic.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This definition establishes the two-component architecture — knowledge and regulation — that organizes this report's entire analytical structure.

> Cognition about cognition — encompassing both *knowledge of cognition* (what one knows about one's own cognitive processes, strategies, and task demands) and *regulation of cognition* (the active monitoring and control of cognitive processes during task execution).

> **Boundary:** Metacognition is not equivalent to intelligence, consciousness, or reflection in the everyday sense. It specifically refers to cognition that takes other cognition as its object. A person can be highly intelligent but metacognitively poor (failing to monitor comprehension during reading), or relatively modest in domain knowledge but metacognitively sophisticated (accurately detecting when they do not understand).

> [!evidence] Metacognition (Flavell, 1979)
> [[metacognition|Metacognition]] refers to the knowledge and regulatory processes that govern cognition itself. John Flavell's foundational framework distinguishes two major components: [[metacognitive-knowledge|metacognitive knowledge]] — what the learner knows about cognition in general and about their own cognitive processes in particular — and [[metacognitive-control|metacognitive regulation]] — the executive processes of planning, monitoring, and evaluating that direct cognitive activity. A third component, [[metacognitive-monitoring|metacognitive monitoring]], functions as the bridge between knowledge and control: it is the ongoing process of assessing one's current comprehension, strategy effectiveness, and proximity to learning goals.
>
> **Boundary:** Metacognition is not synonymous with consciousness or self-awareness; metacognitive processes can operate with varying degrees of explicit awareness, and skilled learners often deploy metacognitive strategies with automaticity that belies their complexity.
>
