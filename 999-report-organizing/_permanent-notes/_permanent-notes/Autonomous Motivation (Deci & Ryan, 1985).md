# Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)

> [!definition] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> A motivational quality characterised by the experience of volition, choice, and self-endorsement in action. Autonomous motivation encompasses both [[intrinsic-motivation|intrinsic motivation]] (acting for the inherent satisfaction of the activity) and well-internalised forms of [[extrinsic-motivation|extrinsic motivation]] (acting for valued outcomes that have been integrated into one's sense of self). In PKM contexts, autonomously motivated learning is associated with deeper cognitive processing, greater persistence in the face of difficulty, more creative problem-solving, and enhanced psychological well-being.
>
> **Boundary:** Autonomous motivation should not be confused with independence or isolation. A learner can be autonomously motivated while following a structured curriculum, provided they endorse the reasons for doing so. The critical variable is not the absence of external influence but the presence of volitional endorsement.
>
> **See also:** [[self-determination-theory]], [[intrinsic-motivation]], [[autonomy-need]], [[organismic-integration-theory]], [[internalization-continuum]]

> [!evidence] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> Autonomous motivation refers to regulatory styles in which the learner experiences their actions as volitional, self-endorsed, and personally meaningful. It encompasses identified regulation (doing something because it aligns with personally valued goals), integrated regulation (doing something because it is fully congruent with one's identity and values), and intrinsic motivation (doing something for the inherent interest and enjoyment of the activity). Autonomous motivation is distinguished from controlled motivation (external regulation, introjected regulation) by the presence of genuine psychological ownership: the learner is the perceived origin of their action, not its instrument.
>
> **Boundary:** Autonomous motivation is not equivalent to low effort or effortless engagement. Autonomously motivated learners regularly engage in difficult, challenging activities. What autonomy predicts is not ease but meaning: the learner chooses the challenge because it serves something they genuinely care about, which sustains engagement through difficulty in ways that external pressure cannot maintain.
>

> [!evidence] Autonomous Motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> Motivation that originates from the person's own values, interests, or genuine endorsement of an activity's importance. Encompasses both intrinsic motivation (acting because an activity is inherently enjoyable or interesting) and identified/integrated regulation (acting because one genuinely values the activity even if it is not intrinsically enjoyable). Distinguished from controlled motivation by the *perceived locus of causality*: autonomous motivation is associated with an internal perceived locus of causality, meaning the person experiences themselves as the origin of their action. In the reconstructed framework, "autonomous" is better understood as *volitional congruence* — acting in ways that align with one's own values — rather than as *independence*.
