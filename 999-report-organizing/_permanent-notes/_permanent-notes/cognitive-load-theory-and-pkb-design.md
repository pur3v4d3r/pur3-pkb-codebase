# Cognitive Load Theory and PKB Design

> [!definition] **Cognitive Load Theory and PKB Design** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> [[cognitive-load-theory-and-pkb-design|Cognitive load theory applied to PKB design]] treats the relationship between working memory limitations and knowledge management system architecture as a design constraint rather than an incidental consideration. The three-load framework — [[intrinsic-cognitive-load|intrinsic load]] (inherent complexity of the material being processed), [[extraneous-cognitive-load-in-pkm|extraneous load]] (processing demands imposed by the system design itself rather than by the content), and [[germane-cognitive-load|germane load]] (processing invested in schema construction and automation) — provides a principled basis for evaluating PKM design decisions. The design objective is to minimize extraneous load, manage intrinsic load through appropriate sequencing and scaffolding, and maximize the remaining working memory capacity available for germane processing.
> 
> **Boundary:** CLT applies most directly to situations where learners process new or complex material. For highly familiar content (automated schemas), cognitive load considerations are less constraining. A PKM system should calibrate its demands to the learner's expertise level — what is extraneous load for a novice may be negligible for an expert.
> 
> **See also:** [[cognitive-load-theory]], [[germane-cognitive-load]], [[extraneous-cognitive-load-in-pkm]], [[intrinsic-cognitive-load]], [[Cognitive-Overload]]

## Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Fergus I. M. Craik (b. 1935)** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> University of Toronto. Co-originator (with Robert Lockhart) of the [[levels-of-processing-theory|levels-of-processing]] framework (1972), establishing that encoding depth — not duration or repetition — determines memory durability. Extended by subsequent work on distinctiveness, elaboration, and transfer-appropriate processing. Foundational for Section 2's encoding analysis.
> 
> **Key work in this report:** Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research.

> [!person] **Endel Tulving (1927–2023)** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> University of Toronto. Proposed the distinction between [[episodic-memory|episodic]] and [[semantic-memory|semantic memory]] (1972), the concept of [[encoding-specificity-principle|encoding specificity]] (1973), and the synergistic ecphory model of retrieval. Tulving's work establishes that retrieval is a constructive interaction between the stored trace and the retrieval cue, not a simple readout.
> 
> **Key work in this report:** Tulving, E. (1983). *Elements of Episodic Memory*.

> [!person] **John Sweller (b. 1946)** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> University of New South Wales. Developer of [[cognitive-load-theory|cognitive load theory]], which integrates working memory limitations with instructional design. His three-load taxonomy (intrinsic, extraneous, germane) provides the design constraint framework used throughout this report, particularly in Section 7.
> 
> **Key work in this report:** Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning; Sweller, J., Ayres, P., & Kalyuga, S. (2011). *Cognitive Load Theory*.

> [!person] **Barry Zimmerman (1942–2020)** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> City University of New York. Pioneer of [[Self-Regulated-Learning-—-SRL|self-regulated learning]] theory. His cyclical model (forethought → performance → self-reflection) provides the regulatory framework applied to PKM practice in Section 5.
> 
> **Key work in this report:** Zimmerman, B. J. (2000). Attaining self-regulation: A social cognitive perspective.

> [!person] **John H. Flavell (b. 1928)** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> Stanford University. Originator of the concept of [[metacognition|metacognition]] (1976, 1979). His framework distinguishing metacognitive knowledge, monitoring, and experience provides the foundational taxonomy for Section 5's analysis of the regulation problem.
> 
> **Key work in this report:** Flavell, J. H. (1979). Metacognition and cognitive monitoring.

> [!person] **Edward Deci (1942–2017) & Richard Ryan (b. 1953)** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> University of Rochester. Co-developers of [[self-determination-theory|Self-Determination Theory]]. Their framework identifying autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs provides the motivational analysis in Section 6.
> 
> **Key work in this report:** Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (2000). The "what" and "why" of goal pursuits.

> [!person] **Robert A. Bjork (b. 1939) & Elizabeth L. Bjork** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> UCLA. Developers of the [[desirable-difficulties|desirable difficulties]] framework and the new theory of disuse (storage strength vs. retrieval strength). Their work grounds the retrieval analysis in Section 4 and the productive friction concept in PKM design.

> **Q:** SR Seed 2 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between storage strength and retrieval strength in the Bjork framework?
**A:** Storage strength reflects how well information is encoded in long-term memory (increases with encoding, does not decay). Retrieval strength reflects current accessibility (decays over time even when storage strength is high). Knowledge can be stored but inaccessible.

> **A:** **Q:** How does spreading activation theory justify link-based over folder-based PKM organization?

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> **Q:** SR Seed 10 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does transfer-appropriate processing connect encoding quality to retrieval success?
**A:** Transfer-appropriate processing (Tulving) holds that retrieval is most successful when the cognitive operations at retrieval match those at encoding. If a note was created through elaborative processing (asking why, generating connections), then retrieval cues invoking those same elaborative processes will be maximally effective. Shallow encoding produces traces that are difficult to retrieve even with good cues.

> [!protocol] **Protocol: The Encoding Depth Checklist** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> Before completing any new permanent note, verify:
> 
> 1. **Paraphrase test:** Is the note written in original language, or does it contain copied passages? Original formulation required.
> 2. **Connection test:** Does the note link to ≥2 existing permanent notes with explicit relationship statements? Bare links insufficient.
> 3. **Elaboration test:** Does the note explain *why* the concept matters, not just *what* it is? Definition-only notes are seeds, not permanent notes.
> 4. **Critical response test:** Does the note include at least one evaluative statement — an implication, limitation, question, or connection the practitioner has generated? Passive summary is shallow encoding.

> [!protocol] **Protocol: The Weekly PKB Health Check** *(from [[pkb-pkm-planning-and-cognitive-science-foundational-report-2026-04-18]])*
> A 15-minute weekly routine for externalized metacognitive monitoring:
> 
> 1. **Orphan scan** (3 min): Run diagnostic to identify notes with <2 incoming links. Target: <5% orphan rate.
> 2. **Link density check** (3 min): Review graph visualization for sparsely connected clusters. Target: no cluster with mean link density <3.
> 3. **Retrieval test** (5 min): Select 3 random permanent notes. Before opening each, attempt to recall its core claim from memory. Score: recalled / partially recalled / not recalled.
> 4. **Review queue status** (2 min): Check spaced repetition completion rate. Target: >80% of due items completed.
> 5. **Motivation pulse** (2 min): Subjective assessment — is engagement driven by interest, identified value, or obligation? Note any motivational shifts.
