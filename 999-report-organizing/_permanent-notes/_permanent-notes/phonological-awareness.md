# Phonological Awareness

> [!definition] **Phonological Awareness** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> Phonological awareness is the explicit, conscious sensitivity to the sound structure of spoken language, including the ability to identify and manipulate phonemes, syllables, and onset-rime units. It is recognised as the strongest early predictor of reading acquisition and is the primary mechanism targeted in effective early literacy interventions.
> 
> **Boundary:** Phonological awareness is NOT the same as phonics (letter-sound correspondence instruction) or phonemic awareness (the most fine-grained subset, awareness of individual phonemes). It is an umbrella category.
> 
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Phonological awareness is the developmental precursor to accurate [[decoding]], which is the precursor to fluency, which liberates [[working-memory-in-reading|working memory capacity]] for comprehension. The entire upper-level comprehension architecture rests on this foundation.
> 
> **See also:** [[decoding]], [[reading-fluency]], [[phonological-loop]], [[fluency.md]]

> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Verbal Efficiency Theory prediction about word-level processing and comprehension?
**A:** Readers whose word recognition is effortful (not automatic) consume working memory resources on decoding, leaving fewer cognitive resources for higher-order processes: inference generation, coherence monitoring, and situation model construction. Individual differences in reading comprehension are substantially explained by this cognitive bottleneck.

> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the vocabulary coverage threshold for functional comprehension of academic texts, and what proportion of texts' academic vocabulary must a reader know?
**A:** Nation (2001): approximately 98% of running words in a text must be known for adequate comprehension. Below 95% coverage, comprehension breaks down due to accumulated inferential gaps. For academic texts, this requires 5,000–8,000 general word families PLUS ~2,000–5,000 specialised academic word families.
**Source:** § Section 3 / Vocabulary Knowledge

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** Distinguish schema assimilation from schema accommodation in the context of academic reading.
**A:** *Assimilation*: new textual information is incorporated into an existing schema without modifying the schema — efficient when the schema is accurate. *Accommodation*: the schema is modified or a new schema is created in response to information that cannot be correctly assimilated. Misconception-based interference occurs when faulty schemas assimilate information that should require accommodation, producing apparent but distorted understanding.
**Source:** § Section 4 / Schema and Prior Knowledge
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** schema-theory, assimilation, accommodation, conceptual-change

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What distinction does Graesser et al.'s constructionist theory make between coherence inferences and elaborative inferences?
**A:** *Coherence (bridging) inferences* are generated automatically to maintain local and global coherence between text segments — they fill the logical gaps between adjacent propositions. *Elaborative inferences* are generated selectively to enrich the situation model with relevant world knowledge beyond coherence requirements. Academic texts are dense with coherence inferences requiring disciplinary knowledge; comprehension failure often involves missing coherence inferences.
**Source:** § Section 5 / Inference Generation
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** inference, CI-model, graesser, constructionist-theory

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the "illusion of knowing" (fluency illusion) in academic reading, and why does it obstruct comprehension development?
**A:** The fluency illusion (Bjork, 1994) is the systematic overestimation of one's comprehension quality when text is processed fluently and confirms existing beliefs. It decouples subjective certainty from actual situation model accuracy. It obstructs comprehension development because it prevents metacognitive monitoring from triggering fix-up strategies — if you believe you understand, you don't deploy repair, and comprehension gaps persist unaddressed.
**Source:** § Section 6 / Metacognitive Monitoring
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** metacognition, fluency-illusion, bjork, comprehension-monitoring

> [!flashcard] **Card 7** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Matthew Effect in reading comprehension, and what mechanisms drive it?
**A:** Stanovich (1986): vocabulary and schema advantages in early reading produce greater comprehension success, which produces greater reading motivation and volume, which produces greater incidental vocabulary acquisition and schema development, which compounds the advantage. Simultaneously, early difficulties reduce reading motivation and volume, compounding the disadvantage. The result is systematic, cumulative divergence between high and low readers over schooling. The name references Matthew 25:29 ("to him who has, more will be given").
**Source:** § Section 2 (Matthew Effect), Section 7 (interventions)
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** matthew-effect, stanovich, reading-development, vocabulary

> [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What are the four strategies in Reciprocal Teaching, and what comprehension function does each serve?
**A:** 1. *Prediction* — activates prior knowledge schemas and generates textual expectations (schema activation). 2. *Questioning* — requires integrating text propositions into comprehension-checkpoint questions (textbase and situation model). 3. *Clarifying* — identifies and addresses vocabulary, coherence, and structural failures (comprehension monitoring + fix-up). 4. *Summarising* — requires integrating the passage into a coherent whole at the situation model level (integration and coherence checking).
**Source:** § Section 7 / Instructional Principles
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** reciprocal-teaching, palincsar, brown, reading-strategy

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** What does the VSI Triad synthesis claim about individual-component reading instruction?
**A:** The Vocabulary-Schema-Inference Triad claims that the three primary components of academic reading comprehension interact *multiplicatively* rather than additively: deficiency in any one cascades into failure across the others. This implies that isolated vocabulary instruction, isolated schema-building, or isolated inference strategy instruction each produces narrow, non-transferring gains — effective academic reading instruction must address all three components concurrently and with metacognitive monitoring as the regulatory overlay.
**Source:** § Synthesis / VSI Triad
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** VSI-triad, synthesis, integrated-instruction, reading-comprehension

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[developing-reading-comprehension-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08]])*
> **Q:** Untitled
> **A:** **Q:** Name and describe the five dimensions of Zwaan's Event-Indexing Model.
**A:** Readers continuously track: (1) *Time* — temporal ordering and sequence of events; (2) *Space* — spatial location and relationship of entities; (3) *Causality* — cause-effect and reason-consequence chains; (4) *Intentionality* — goals, motivations, and actions of agents; (5) *Entity identity* — stable tracking of who/what across mentions and pronoun chains. Academic texts make differential demands on these dimensions depending on genre and discipline.
