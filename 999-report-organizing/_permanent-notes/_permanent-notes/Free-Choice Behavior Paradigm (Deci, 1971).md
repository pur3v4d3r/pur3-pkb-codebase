# Free-Choice Behavior Paradigm (Deci, 1971)

> [!definition] Free-Choice Behavior Paradigm (Deci, 1971)
> The free-choice behavioral paradigm is the primary experimental operationalization of intrinsic motivation in CET research. Following an experimental manipulation (typically involving reward or feedback conditions), participants are left in a room with the target activity available along with competing options, and an experimenter covertly measures the amount of time participants spontaneously spend with the activity during an ostensibly unobserved period. The theoretical assumption is that time spent freely engaging with the activity — without any ongoing external contingency — indexes the individual's intrinsic motivation for it.
>
> **Common Misconception:** The free-choice measure is not equivalent to self-reported interest or enjoyment, and the two operationalizations do not always converge. Meta-analyses find somewhat different patterns for free-choice behavior vs. self-report outcomes, which has generated theoretical and methodological discussion about what each measure actually captures.
>
> **Research Status:** The free-choice paradigm has been criticized for conflating intrinsic motivation with satiation and for potential experimenter demand effects. Multiple studies use both measures simultaneously; the Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis addressed these concerns systematically.
>
