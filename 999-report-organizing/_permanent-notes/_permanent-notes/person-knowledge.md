# Person Knowledge

> [!definition] **Person Knowledge** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> The subcomponent of metacognitive knowledge concerning beliefs about human cognitive processes in general, beliefs about the cognitive characteristics of other specific individuals, and beliefs about one's own cognitive strengths, limitations, and typical patterns (intrapersonal knowledge). Intrapersonal person knowledge is the most extensively studied dimension; its accuracy (calibration) is one of the primary determinants of self-regulatory quality.
> 
> **Common Misconception:** Person knowledge is not equivalent to self-concept or academic self-efficacy, though these constructs are correlated. Person knowledge specifically concerns beliefs about cognitive functioning — how one processes information, what kinds of tasks are more or less demanding for one's specific cognitive profile — rather than general beliefs about one's academic competence.
> 
> **See also:** [[self-efficacy]], [[calibration]], [[metacognitive-calibration]], [[dunning-kruger-effect]], [[growth-mindset]]

## Spaced Repetition Seeds

> [!flashcard] **Card 1** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 1 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is metacognitive knowledge, and how does it differ from metacognitive regulation?
**A:** Metacognitive knowledge is stored, consciously accessible beliefs about cognitive processes, strategies, and states — the *content* of the metacognitive system residing in long-term memory. Metacognitive regulation refers to the executive processes (planning, monitoring, evaluating) that govern cognitive activity in real time. Knowledge provides the content from which regulation draws; regulation is the operational use of that content.
**Source:** Phase II.1
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** #metacognition #metacognitive-knowledge #definition

> [!flashcard] **Card 2** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 2 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What is the difference between declarative, procedural, and conditional metacognitive knowledge?
**A:** Declarative = knowing *that* (a strategy exists and what it does); Procedural = knowing *how* (the operational steps for executing a strategy); Conditional = knowing *when and why* (the contextual conditions under which a strategy is effective or ineffective). Conditional knowledge is the most sophisticated, most domain-specific, and most frequently neglected in instruction.
**Source:** Phase II.2
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #metacognitive-knowledge #declarative #procedural #conditional

> [!flashcard] **Card 3** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 3 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What are the three dimensions of Flavell's metacognitive knowledge taxonomy?
**A:** Person knowledge (beliefs about human cognition in general, specific others' cognition, and one's own cognitive profile); Task knowledge (beliefs about how task characteristics affect cognitive demands); Strategy knowledge (beliefs about cognitive and metacognitive strategies: what they are, how they work, when they are effective).
**Source:** Phase II.1
**Difficulty:** Basic
**Tags:** #metacognitive-knowledge #Flavell #person-task-strategy

> [!flashcard] **Card 4** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 4 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the monitoring-control hierarchy in the Nelson-Narens model, and what role does metacognitive knowledge play in it?
**A:** The model distinguishes an object level (where cognitive work occurs) and a meta-level (where processes are represented and managed). Monitoring flows upward (object → meta: the meta-level receives information about current processing). Control flows downward (meta → object: the meta-level modifies processing). Metacognitive knowledge provides the meta-level's representational content: the standards for monitoring and the inventory of control responses.
**Source:** Phase III.3
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #Nelson-Narens #monitoring #control #metacognitive-knowledge

> [!flashcard] **Card 5** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 5 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** What are metacognitive experiences (Efklides), and how do they differ from metacognitive knowledge?
**A:** Metacognitive experiences are online, phenomenological, affective-cognitive states that arise during task processing (feelings of knowing, difficulty, confidence, familiarity). They are situationally specific, affectively toned, and generated by heuristic processes (fluency, familiarity). Metacognitive knowledge is stored, stable, propositional, and retrievable. Metacognitive experiences serve as the primary triggers for regulatory responses and as the interface through which stored knowledge is engaged with ongoing activity.
**Source:** Phase II.3, Phase III.4
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #metacognitive-experience #metacognitive-feelings #Efklides #metacognitive-knowledge

> [!flashcard] **Card 6** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 6 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** What is the Knowledge-Activation Gap, and what mechanisms are hypothesized to produce it?
**A:** The Knowledge-Activation Gap is the systematic failure of stored, declaratively accurate metacognitive knowledge to activate in relevant regulatory contexts during task performance. Three mechanisms are proposed: (1) indexing insufficiency (knowledge indexed to instruction contexts, not task-embedded monitoring signals); (2) proceduralization deficit (insufficient supported practice to proceduralize declarative knowledge); (3) monitoring-knowledge decoupling (weak associative links between monitoring state representations and metacognitive knowledge elements). > [!flashcard] **Card 8** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 8 — Definition
> **A:** **Q:** What is pseudometacognition, and how is it distinguishable from genuine metacognitive knowledge?
**A:** Pseudometacognition is metacognitive-appearing knowledge or behavior that does not reflect genuine awareness of one's actual cognitive processes or accurately calibrated monitoring. It is characterized by context-insensitivity (same narrative regardless of task/performance variation) and failure to generate adaptive regulatory responses when monitoring signals indicate need. Distinguished from genuine metacognitive knowledge by: insensitivity to performance feedback, failure to update under disconfirming evidence, and absence of corresponding regulatory improvement.
**Source:** Phase V.3
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #pseudometacognition #metacognitive-knowledge #calibration #monitoring

> [!flashcard] **Card 9** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 9 — Evidence
> **A:** **Q:** What does calibration research reveal about the accuracy of metacognitive monitoring, and how can calibration be improved?
**A:** Calibration research consistently shows overconfident monitoring in novice and moderately experienced learners in domains with delayed or indirect feedback. The fluency illusion (smooth processing mistaken for deep comprehension) is the most common mechanism. Calibration is improvable through: retrieval practice (experience of recognition-recall gap recalibrates predictions), delayed JOLs (delayed self-assessments are more accurate than immediate ones), and explicit instruction in sources of miscalibration.
**Source:** Phase IV.5
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #calibration #metacognitive-calibration #fluency-illusion #retrieval-practice

> [!flashcard] **Card 10** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 10 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** Why is conditional metacognitive knowledge the most domain-specific dimension of the taxonomy, and what are the implications for transfer?
**A:** Conditional knowledge (knowing when and why a strategy is effective) requires knowledge of the specific task structures, epistemic conventions, and performance standards of a domain to be genuinely functional. Generic conditions (e.g., "use elaborative interrogation for expository text") are only marginally functional — truly conditional knowledge specifies conditions at the level of text genre, comprehension goal, prior knowledge threshold, and task demand. Transfer of conditional metacognitive knowledge across domains is poor because these conditions are domain-indexed.
**Source:** Phase III.6
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #conditional-metacognitive-knowledge #domain-specificity #transfer #metacognitive-knowledge

> [!flashcard] **Card 11** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 11 — Distinction
> **A:** **Q:** How does the domain-generality debate in metacognitive knowledge research resolve, and what are the implications for instruction?
**A:** Evidence supports a layered model: declarative metacognitive knowledge (broad cognitive principles) is domain-general and transfers; conditional metacognitive knowledge is domain-specific and transfers poorly. Implication: generic metacognitive workshops improve declarative metacognitive knowledge but not conditional; metacognitive instruction must be domain-embedded for conditional knowledge development. The general principles do not substitute for domain-specific conditional knowledge but accelerate its acquisition by providing conceptual frameworks for organizing domain-specific experience.
**Source:** Phase III.6
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #metacognitive-knowledge #domain-generality #domain-specificity #transfer #instruction

> [!flashcard] **Card 12** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 12 — Mechanism
> **A:** **Q:** What are the three pathways through which metacognitive knowledge is acquired?
**A:** (1) Experience-based acquisition: patterns of success/failure encoded as conditional knowledge through direct task experience — slow, susceptible to systematic biases, dependent on accurate feedback. (2) Instruction-based acquisition: explicit transmission of declarative metacognitive knowledge, providing conceptual categories that structure subsequent experience. (3) Social transmission: expert-novice dialogue, observational learning, and internalization of regulatory speech (Vygotsky) — transmits conditional knowledge that would otherwise require years to develop experientially.
**Source:** Phase IV.1
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #metacognitive-knowledge #acquisition #experience #instruction #social-transmission

> [!flashcard] **Card 13** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 13 — Application
> **A:** **Q:** What distinguishes effective from ineffective metacognitive knowledge instruction?
**A:** Effective metacognitive knowledge instruction: (1) is explicit — does not rely on implicit development alone; (2) is domain-embedded — develops conditional knowledge within domain-specific content; (3) targets the conditional dimension explicitly — addresses when and why, not just what and how; (4) is feedback-rich — structures retrieval-based feedback that makes calibration errors visible; (5) practices metacognitive knowledge in the contextual conditions where it should activate, not only in decontextualized instruction.
**Source:** Phase V.1
**Difficulty:** Intermediate
**Tags:** #metacognitive-knowledge #instruction #educational-application #conditional #feedback

> [!flashcard] **Card 14** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 14 — Process
> **A:** **Q:** Why is the retrieval timing of metacognitive judgments of learning significant for calibration accuracy?
**A:** Immediate JOLs are dominated by fluency and familiarity effects from recent processing — material that was just studied is highly accessible, producing inflated confidence. Delayed JOLs (made after a delay allowing processing-fluency effects to dissipate) are substantially more accurate predictors of actual retention because they depend more on genuine memory strength signals and less on transient fluency. This implies learners should use delayed JOLs and retrieval-based tests rather than rereading fluency as mastery indicators.
**Source:** Phase IV.5
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #JOL #judgment-of-learning #calibration #fluency-illusion #retrieval-practice

> [!flashcard] **Card 15** *(from [[metacognitive-knowledge-foundational-report-2026-03-31]])*
> **Q:** Seed 15 — Connection
> **A:** **Q:** How does Ann Brown's knowledge-regulation distinction relate to the Knowledge-Activation Gap construct proposed in this report?
**A:** Brown's distinction (knowledge of cognition vs. regulation of cognition) identifies the phenomenon — that accurate metacognitive knowledge and effective metacognitive regulation can dissociate — but does not specify the mechanisms of dissociation. The Knowledge-Activation Gap construct attempts to specify three mechanisms that could produce this dissociation: indexing insufficiency, proceduralization deficit, and monitoring-knowledge decoupling. The Knowledge-Activation Gap is thus a mechanistic elaboration of Brown's empirical observation, though its status remains speculative.
**Source:** Phase III.2, Phase IV.3
**Difficulty:** Advanced
**Tags:** #Brown #knowledge-regulation #knowledge-activation-gap #metacognitive-knowledge

## References

- **Flavell, J. H. (1979). Metacognition and cognitive monitoring: A new area of cognitive-developmental inquiry. *American Psychologist*, 34(10), 906–911. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.34.10.906**: The foundational paper that established the metacognition construct and introduced the person-task-strategy taxonomy. Essential primary source for understanding the original formulation of metacognitive knowledge. Remarkably brief and accessible given its theoretical significance; the taxonomy presented here has structured the field's conceptual architecture for over four decades.

**Recommended Sections:** The full paper (10 pages) should be read in its entirety; particular attention to pages 907–909 where the taxonomy is formally introduced.
- **Flavell, J. H. (1981). Cognitive monitoring. In W. P. Dickson (Ed.), *Children's Oral Communication Skills* (pp. 35–60). Academic Press.**: The 1981 chapter elaborates the metacognitive knowledge taxonomy and introduces metacognitive experiences as a fourth component of the metacognitive system. More developed than the 1979 paper in its treatment of metacognitive experiences and their relationship to stored knowledge.
- **Brown, A. L. (1978). Knowing when, where, and how to remember: A problem of metacognition. In R. Glaser (Ed.), *Advances in Instructional Psychology*, Vol. 1 (pp. 77–165). Lawrence Erlbaum.**: Brown's foundational chapter introduces the knowledge-regulation distinction that has proven as influential as Flavell's taxonomy. Particularly valuable for its treatment of reading comprehension monitoring and its review of developmental studies showing metacognitive deficits in young readers. A long chapter but one of the most important primary sources in the metacognitive knowledge literature.
- **Paris, S. G., Lipson, M. Y., & Wixson, K. K. (1983). Becoming a strategic reader. *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 8(3), 293–316. https://doi.org/10.1016/0361-476X(83)90018-8**: The source paper for the declarative-procedural-conditional taxonomy as applied to metacognitive knowledge and reading strategy instruction. Essential for understanding the conceptual foundations of the DPC framework and its original application to reading strategy development.
- **Nelson, T. O., & Narens, L. (1990). Metamemory: A theoretical framework and new findings. *Psychology of Learning and Motivation*, 26, 125–173. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-7421(08)60053-5**: The foundational paper for the monitoring-control hierarchy. Technically dense but provides the most precise account available of how metacognitive processes interact with object-level cognition. The framework presented here has been enormously generative for experimental research on memory monitoring and control.

**Recommended Sections:** Pages 125–145 for the theoretical framework; the remainder for empirical applications.
- **Schraw, G., & Moshman, D. (1995). Metacognitive theories. *Educational Psychology Review*, 7(4), 351–371. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02212307**: The best single theoretical synthesis of metacognitive frameworks available in the literature. Schraw and Moshman organize the field's competing accounts into a coherent comparative framework, clarifying the relationships among the major theories while identifying their points of genuine disagreement.
- **Schraw, G., & Dennison, R. S. (1994). Assessing metacognitive awareness. *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 19(4), 460–475. https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1994.1033**: The source paper for the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (MAI), the most widely used self-report instrument in metacognitive knowledge research. Essential for understanding the operationalization of metacognitive knowledge and for critically evaluating the self-report measurement approach.
- **Efklides, A. (2006). Metacognition and affect: What can metacognitive experiences tell us about the learning process? *Educational Research Review*, 1(1), 3–14. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2005.11.001**: A highly accessible overview of Efklides's framework on metacognitive experiences and their role in self-regulated learning. The best entry point into the phenomenological dimension of metacognitive processing.
- **Efklides, A. (2011). Interactions of metacognition with motivation and affect in self-regulated learning: The MASRL model. *Educational Psychologist*, 46(1), 6–25. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.538645**: Efklides's most comprehensive theoretical synthesis, presenting the Metacognitive and Affective Model of Self-Regulated Learning (MASRL). More technical than the 2006 paper but provides a fully integrated account of how metacognitive feelings, metacognitive knowledge, and affective states interact in the self-regulation of learning.
- **Pintrich, P. R. (2002). The role of metacognitive knowledge in learning, teaching, and assessing. *Theory Into Practice*, 41(4), 219–225. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_3**: Pintrich's accessible treatment of metacognitive knowledge within the SRL framework. Particularly valuable for its treatment of the four types of knowledge (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive) and their roles in self-regulated learning.
- **Koriat, A., & Bjork, R. A. (2005). Illusions of competence in monitoring one's knowledge during study. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 31(2), 187–194.**: Key empirical paper on the miscalibration of judgments of learning and the mechanisms through which learners overestimate their own knowledge during study. Foundational for the calibration literature reviewed in Phase IV.5.
- **Alexander, P. A. (1995). Superimposing a situation-specific and domain-specific perspective on an account of self-regulated learning. *Educational Psychologist*, 30(4), 189–193.**: A critical counterpoint to domain-general accounts of metacognitive knowledge. Alexander's domain-specific perspective informs the tension discussed in Appendix Section 3.
- **Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research*, 77(1), 81–112. https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487**: This review of feedback research is relevant to metacognitive knowledge development through its documentation of how feedback supports calibration. The Hattie-Timperley model's four levels of feedback (task, process, self-regulation, self) map directly onto the dimensions of metacognitive knowledge addressed in this report.

**Recommended Sections:** Pages 90–101 on feedback and self-regulation.
- **Dent, A. L., & Koenka, A. C. (2016). The relation between self-regulated learning and academic achievement across childhood and adolescence: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review*, 28(3), 425–474.**: Meta-analytic evidence for the relationship between self-regulated learning (including metacognitive knowledge components) and academic achievement. Provides quantitative estimates of effect sizes referenced in Phase III.5.
