> [!section-summary] Section 2 Key Takeaways
> 1. Working memory is the primary bottleneck of reading comprehension, but schemas stored in long-term memory function as cognitive compression units that dramatically expand effective processing capacity. Building domain knowledge is thus directly equivalent to expanding operational working memory for domain-specific texts.
> 2. The Verbal Efficiency Theory establishes that automaticity at word recognition is a prerequisite for higher-level comprehension, but automaticity with unfamiliar domain vocabulary remains a persistent obstacle in academic reading.
> 3. The Fluency Illusion — reading smoothly without comprehending deeply — is an architectural vulnerability created by the mismatch between decoding automaticity and situation model construction. Metacognitive training is the primary countermeasure, and its development is the subject of the next section.
> 4. The Matthew Effect means that comprehension development is not linear but compounding: early investment in domain knowledge and reading practice creates exponentially increasing returns in comprehension capacity.
> *— [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 2 Key Takeaways
> 1. **Three-process architecture**: Wittrock's SOI model identifies Selection, Organization, and Integration as the three core generative processes. Each is cognitively active, schema-guided, and necessary for meaningful learning; none is passive or automatic.
> 2. **Organization produces usable knowledge**: Organized knowledge — structured as mental models with relational connections — enables reasoning, inference, and transfer in ways that propositionally stored information does not. Organization bridges storage and thought.
> 3. **Integration requires prior knowledge**: Meaningful integration — the anchoring of new information to existing schemas — is possible only to the degree that relevant prior knowledge exists. This explains why domain knowledge predicts learning comprehension and why prior knowledge activation is a critical instructional intervention.
> 4. **Motivation is integral, not peripheral**: The generative model requires sustained cognitive effort; motivational conditions (interest, autonomy, self-efficacy) are not mere adjuncts to generative learning but necessary enabling conditions.
> *— [[generative-learning-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 2 Key Takeaways
> - [[metacognitive-knowledge|Metacognitive knowledge]] is Flavell's term for stably held beliefs about persons, tasks, and strategies as cognitive phenomena. Schraw extends it into declarative (*what*), procedural (*how*), and [[conditional-metacognitive-knowledge|conditional]] (*when/why*) forms.
> - Conditional metacognitive knowledge is the most developmentally advanced component and the strongest predictor of expert performance; it is also the most difficult to instruct because it requires diagnostic pattern recognition that develops through extensive experience.
> - The **knowledge-utilization gap** — the discrepancy between knowing effective strategies and deploying them — is one of the central challenges of applied metacognition. It is best addressed through environmental and instructional design rather than individual exhortation.
> - Early metacognitive knowledge is systematically overoptimistic in ways that may serve developmental functions; calibration toward realism proceeds through middle childhood and remains incompletely achieved even in many adults.
> *— [[metacognitive-development-foundational-report-2026-04-09_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 2 Key Takeaways
> 1. Metacognitive reflection has roots in Dewey's reflective thinking (felt difficulty → systematic inquiry), Flavell's metacognition framework (knowledge × experience × regulation), Brown's regulatory distinction, and the Nelson-Narens monitoring-control model.
> 2. Schön's contribution — reflection-in-action vs. reflection-on-action — extends the construct to embodied professional practice, emphasizing context-embeddedness and tacit knowing.
> 3. Zimmerman and Pintrich integrate metacognitive reflection as a named phase (Self-Reflection Phase) within comprehensive SRL models, connecting it to attributional processes and adaptive outcomes.
> 4. Contemporary neuroscience grounds metacognitive reflection in prefrontal networks, revealing the performance-metacognitive accuracy dissociation that establishes the limits of self-report.
> *— [[metacognitive-reflection-foundational-report-2026-04-09_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 2 Key Takeaways
> 1. Metacognition is a two-component construct: a knowledge dimension (person, task, and strategy knowledge) and a regulatory dimension (planning, monitoring, and evaluation) united by their common object — cognition itself.
> 2. Metacognitive experiences — the felt sense of knowing, not-knowing, comprehension, and confusion — are important monitoring signals but are systematically unreliable; the fluency illusion demonstrates that ease of processing misleads self-assessment of learning.
> 3. The domain-specificity versus domain-generality debate is resolved by a nuanced position: general regulatory processes exist and transfer, but their expression requires domain-specific knowledge that gives monitoring content and strategy selection meaning.
> *— [[metacognitive-skills-foundational-report-2026-04-09_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 2 Key Takeaways
> 1. **Schemas are processing engines, not storage containers.** They actively organize perception, encoding, and retrieval through hierarchical embedding, associative connectivity, chunking, and automaticity — each an organizational property, not a storage property.
> 2. **Memory reconstruction is schema-driven.** [[frederic-bartlett|Bartlett's]] original experiments and decades of subsequent research establish that retrieval reconstructs the past through the lens of present schemas. The PKB must support not just storage but the schematic organization that governs reconstruction.
> 3. **Schema development is constructivist.** Genuine learning requires accommodation — schema revision, not merely assimilation. A PKB designed only to smoothly organize existing understanding is a schema-confirming tool; a PKB designed to expose conceptual tensions is a schema-developing one.
> *— [[schema-externalization-pkb-long-term-memory-foundational-report-2026-04-05_report]]*
