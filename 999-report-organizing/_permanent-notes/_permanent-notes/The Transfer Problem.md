## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] The Transfer Problem
> Perhaps the most important and most honestly acknowledged limitation in CT education research is the *transfer problem*: skills developed in one context frequently fail to transfer to genuinely different contexts. A student who learns to identify logical fallacies in philosophy class may not apply this capacity when reading a politically charged news article. A medical student who develops excellent diagnostic reasoning may not apply the same evidence-evaluation standards to health claims in their personal life. Transfer appears to require: (1) explicit metacognitive awareness of the underlying principle (not just the specific skill), (2) practice across multiple and varied contexts, and (3) explicit instruction in *how* to notice when a new situation calls for CT. Educators who assume that teaching CT in one context automatically equips students for CT in all contexts are almost certainly mistaken.
> *— [[critical-thinking-educational-settings-foundational-report-2026-03-05_report]]*

> [!evidence] The Transfer Problem
> Perhaps the most significant unresolved question for education is whether formal deductive training in one domain — mathematics, logic, philosophy — transfers to other domains. The claim for "formal discipline" — the idea that logical training in any domain strengthens reasoning in all domains — was historically the primary justification for formal logic instruction. The empirical evidence, however, is mixed at best. Studies of logic instruction show modest positive effects on reasoning in domains similar to the instructional context, but limited evidence of broad transfer. This does not mean logic instruction is valueless — domain-specific deductive competence is valuable in itself — but it cautions against overstating the general cognitive benefits.
> *— [[deductive-reasoning-learning-foundational-report-2026-03-10_report]]*
