# Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)

> [!definition] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> Stored, relatively stable beliefs about persons (including oneself), tasks, and strategies as they relate to cognitive activity. Includes knowing that rereading is less effective than self-testing, knowing that one struggles with abstract reasoning under time pressure, and knowing that summarization aids comprehension. Distinguished from in-the-moment monitoring by its declarative, long-term-memory character. See [[metacognitive-knowledge]].

> [!evidence] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> The accumulated body of knowledge a person holds about their own cognitive processes and those of others, about the nature of tasks and how difficulty varies, and about strategies for learning and problem-solving. Metacognitive knowledge is declarative ("I know that I understand visual diagrams better than text") and can be described verbally. It does not, by itself, produce regulation — knowing what good study strategies exist does not ensure their use.

> [!evidence] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> Stored, consciously accessible beliefs and understandings concerning cognitive processes, states, and strategies — including those of the self, other persons, cognitive tasks, and regulatory strategies. Distinguished from metacognitive regulation (the executive processes that govern cognitive activity) and metacognitive experiences (the online phenomenological states that arise during cognitive activity). Resides in [[long-term-memory]] as a schematically organized knowledge network that provides the epistemic basis for self-regulated learning.
>
> **Historical Development:** The construct emerged from Flavell's developmental studies of memory monitoring in children and was refined through parallel work by Brown on reading comprehension regulation. The initial formulation was primarily cognitive-developmental; subsequent extensions by Schraw, Nelson and Narens, and Efklides have broadened and deepened the theoretical treatment.

> [!evidence] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> Stored knowledge about oneself as a cognitive agent, about cognitive tasks and their demands, and about strategies for managing cognitive work. Flavell identified three varieties: person knowledge (beliefs about one's own and others' cognitive capacities), task knowledge (understanding how task characteristics affect cognitive difficulty), and strategy knowledge (knowing what cognitive strategies exist and when to deploy them). Critically, metacognitive knowledge is fallible, domain-specific, and subject to all the biases that affect any declarative knowledge.
> *— [[metacognitive-scaffolding-focused-analysis-2026-03-20_report]]*

> [!evidence] Metacognitive Knowledge (Flavell, 1979)
> [[metacognitive-knowledge|Metacognitive knowledge]] refers to one's accumulated understanding of cognitive processes in general and one's own cognitive tendencies in particular. Flavell subdivided this into three interacting categories: **person knowledge** (beliefs about oneself and others as cognitive agents), **task knowledge** (understanding of how task demands influence cognitive performance), and **strategy knowledge** (awareness of which cognitive strategies are effective for which purposes). In the Nelson–Narrens framework, metacognitive knowledge is best understood as a relatively stable component of the meta-level's model — the background representations against which real-time monitoring signals are interpreted.
>
> **Boundary:** Metacognitive knowledge is not equivalent to metacognitive monitoring; the former is a stored knowledge base, the latter is an active, moment-to-moment process of information extraction.
