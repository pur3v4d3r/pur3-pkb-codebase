> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> 1. Prior knowledge — operationalized as domain schemas — is the single most powerful predictor of reading comprehension, more predictive than general reading ability for domain-specific academic texts.
> 2. Schema-activation strategies (advance organizers, structured previewing, prior knowledge questioning) are among the most efficient comprehension interventions for domain-novice readers precisely because they address the primary constraint on their comprehension.
> 3. Domain vocabulary development is not merely surface literacy work but direct schema-building — the lexical-conceptual architecture of a field is its conceptual structure, and vocabulary acquisition is the primary access route to domain schemas.
> 4. The Matthew Effect operates across both comprehension performance and metacognitive monitoring quality — poor domain knowledge degrades both what readers understand and their ability to detect when they have misunderstood.
> *— [[developing-reading-comprehension-and-metacognition-for-academic-texts-foundational-report-2026-04-08_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> 1. **CTML is GLT's multimedia extension**: Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning builds directly on Wittrock's SOI model, extending generative learning principles to dual-channel environments with explicit cognitive load management.
> 2. **Twelve principles as design specifications**: CTML's evidence-based principles (Coherence, Signaling, Contiguity, Modality, Redundancy, etc.) translate GLT's abstract prescriptions into concrete instructional design specifications, each with documented empirical support.
> 3. **Transfer advantage**: The primary learning advantage of generative multimedia learning appears on transfer measures, not retention of verbatim content. This reflects GLT's prediction that generative processing builds mental models (structural understanding) rather than verbatim records, and mental models are precisely what transfer requires.
> 4. **Generative drawing**: Learner-generated drawings produce superior learning to pre-provided drawings, directly paralleling the generation effect. The cognitive work of constructing a spatial representation, not the representation itself, is the active ingredient.
> *— [[generative-learning-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-03_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways

> 3. Autonomy-supportive communication produces higher-quality engagement and more durable outcomes than controlling communication, across educational, professional, and personal contexts.

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> - Metacognition is a core component of self-regulated learning but does not exhaust it; motivational, behavioral, and contextual regulation dimensions are equally important and interact with metacognition throughout the SRL cycle.
> - Key SRL frameworks (Zimmerman's cyclical model, Pintrich's 4×4 matrix, Winne-Hadwin COPES model) all place metacognitive monitoring and control at the operational center of the SRL cycle but contextualize them within broader motivational and standards-based processes.
> - Standards inadequacy — not merely monitoring inaccuracy — is an important and underemphasized source of metacognitive monitoring failure; standards enrichment through deep study of exemplary work is a corresponding developmental intervention.
> - Motivational orientation shapes metacognitive monitoring signal interpretation: growth versus fixed mindset determines whether difficulty signals trigger adaptive engagement or disengagement, making motivational development inseparable from metacognitive development.
> *— [[metacognitive-development-foundational-report-2026-04-09_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> 1. [[self-regulated-learning]] frameworks provide the most rigorous educational account of metacognitive reflection, locating it in the self-reflection phase of cyclical learning models (Zimmerman) and across all phases of the cognition column (Pintrich).
> 2. Within Zimmerman's framework, self-evaluation and causal attribution are the principal processes, with attribution quality — specifically, attribution to controllable, unstable strategy and effort factors — as the pivotal determinant of adaptive vs. maladaptive reflective outcomes.
> 3. Pintrich's 4×4 framework enriches the account by showing that reflection simultaneously revises cognitive plans, motivational orientations, behavioral commitments, and contextual assessments — it is not only a cognitive but an integrated self-regulatory event.
> 4. Metacognitive reflection directly constructs self-efficacy beliefs through attributional framing, meaning it serves a motivational-architectural function alongside its epistemic function.
> 5. The forethought-reflection-forethought loop accumulates metacognitive knowledge over learning cycles, explaining the development of expert intuitive self-knowledge from repeated high-quality reflective practice.
> *— [[metacognitive-reflection-foundational-report-2026-04-09_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> 1. Metacognitive development begins with theory of mind in early childhood and progresses through the differentiation of metacognitive knowledge types, the development of spontaneous strategy deployment (overcoming metacognitive inertia), and, in mature form, epistemological reflection on the frameworks by which knowledge is evaluated.
> 2. Expertise development produces qualitatively different metacognitive monitoring content: novices monitor surface features while experts monitor deep structural features, a difference that both reflects and creates the performance gap between levels.
> 3. Metacognitive development is both maturational and educational — biological maturation provides the substrate, but explicit instruction, feedback, and metacognitive scaffolding are required to actualize developmental potential, and their absence can maintain metacognitive naivety into adulthood.
> *— [[metacognitive-skills-foundational-report-2026-04-09_report]]*

> [!section-summary] Section 6 Key Takeaways
> The developmental roots of adult relational motivation quality lie in early attachment experiences and the degree of conditional versus unconditional regard in primary caregiving relationships. Attachment theory and SDT's conditional regard framework converge on the same developmental claim: non-contingent, responsive caregiving produces adult relational motivation oriented toward the autonomous end, while contingent, conditional caregiving — and its sequela of insecure attachment — produces controlled relational motivation styles (fear-based, guilt-driven, approval-contingent, self-worth contingent). The motivational consequences of these developmental histories are substantial but not fixed; earned security and corrective relational experiences can shift relational motivation quality across the lifespan, with implications for clinical intervention.
> *— [[relationship-motivation-theory-foundational-report-2026-04-01_report]]*
