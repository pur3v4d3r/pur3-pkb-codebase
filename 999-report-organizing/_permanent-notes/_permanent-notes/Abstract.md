> [!evidence] Abstract
> Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT) is the third and arguably most architecturally central mini-theory within [[self-determination-theory]] (SDT), the macro-theory of human motivation and personality development advanced by [[edward-deci]] and [[richard-ryan]] over more than four decades. BPNT proposes that three psychological needs — [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], [[competence-need|competence]], and [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] — are universal, innate, and essential to human psychological well-being, vitality, and integrated functioning. Unlike need hierarchies that treat needs as sequentially activated or interchangeable satisfaction systems, BPNT advances a necessity claim: all three needs require ongoing satisfaction for the maintenance of psychological health, and the frustration of any single need is sufficient to undermine well-being and promote ill-being. Central to BPNT's contemporary development is the satisfaction-frustration asymmetry, which holds that need satisfaction and need frustration are not simply opposite ends of a single continuum, but distinct psychological processes producing qualitatively different outcomes. Need satisfaction promotes vitality, authenticity, and growth; need frustration produces a characteristic profile of ill-being including rigidity, defensiveness, amotivation, and pathological functioning. This report traces the intellectual lineage of BPNT from White's (1959) effectance motivation and deCharms's (1968) personal causation through the contemporary dual-process model; provides architectural analysis of each need including definitional precision, scope conditions, and functional significance; examines the environmental conditions that support or thwart need satisfaction; investigates the cross-cultural evidence for universality; situates BPNT within the broader SDT mini-theory constellation; traces developmental pathways; and surveys the evidence base for BPNT-informed applications across education, healthcare, workplace, and therapeutic contexts. The report concludes with far-transfer analysis and original synthesis connecting BPNT to knowledge architecture and lifelong learning design.

> This report examines [[chunking|chunking]] as the cognitive mechanism through which human beings transcend the severe capacity limitations of [[working-memory|working memory]] and, over sustained periods of learning, develop the organized knowledge structures that constitute [[expertise|expertise]]. The analysis begins with the foundational constraint that shapes all human cognition: the narrow bandwidth of working memory, which permits the simultaneous manipulation of only a small number of independent information elements. It then traces how chunking — the process of encoding multiple lower-level elements into integrated higher-order units — serves as the mind's primary adaptive response to this constraint, enabling the progressive construction of increasingly complex and richly interconnected [[schema|schemas]] in [[long-term-memory|long-term memory]]. The report follows the developmental trajectory from novice to expert, demonstrating how the accumulation, refinement, and eventual automation of chunks transforms not only what a person knows but how that person perceives, reasons, and acts within a domain. Particular attention is given to the relationship between [[deliberate-practice|deliberate practice]] and chunk quality, to the role of [[schema-automation|schema automation]] in liberating cognitive resources for higher-order performance, and to the instructional implications that follow from [[cognitive-load-theory|cognitive load theory]]'s translation of chunking principles into educational design. The analysis integrates evidence from chess expertise, medical diagnosis, physics problem-solving, and athletic performance to construct a unified account of how structured knowledge organization underlies skilled human performance. Original contributions include the articulation of a *constraint-liberation dialectic* — the thesis that working memory limitation is not merely an obstacle to expertise but the structural precondition for the kind of learning that produces it — and the framing of chunking as the *unit currency of deliberate practice*, the atomic cognitive change through which effortful training yields measurable gains in performance.

> [!evidence] Abstract
> [[Cognitive-Load-Theory]] is most commonly encountered as a tripartite taxonomy — intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load — that one applies as a kind of accounting framework when designing instruction. This treatment is not wrong, but it is shallow, and it conceals the construct that has come to organize the entire theoretical edifice in its mature, post-2010 form: [[Element-Interactivity]]. What this report does is take element interactivity as its single focal point and zoom in across seven successive levels of magnification, beginning with how the construct appears at the surface, descending through the mechanism by which it generates intrinsic load, opening the substructure of what an "element" actually is and what makes two elements "interact," tracing the dynamics by which expertise transforms element interactivity over time, examining the edge cases where the standard story breaks down (biologically primary knowledge, the [[Expertise-Reversal-Effect]], the contested status of [[Germane-Cognitive-Load]]), and finally engaging the current research frontier where measurement instruments, individual differences, and neural correlates remain genuinely open questions. The report closes with informed speculation about where the construct may go next under pressure from predictive processing accounts of cognition and from AI-mediated learning environments capable of dynamic load measurement. The reader who finishes this report will not have surveyed CLT — that work belongs to a foundational report and is available elsewhere in this collection — but will have inhabited the construct that does the actual theoretical work in the modern theory.

> [!evidence] Abstract
> [[cognitive-load-theory]] (CLT), developed principally by [[john-sweller]] beginning in the 1980s, stands as one of the most empirically productive and practically consequential theories in [[educational-psychology]] and [[instructional-design]]. Grounded in the fundamental asymmetry between the severe processing limitations of [[working-memory]] and the effectively unlimited storage capacity of [[long-term-memory]], CLT provides a principled framework for understanding why certain instructional procedures succeed while others fail. The theory identifies three distinguishable sources of cognitive demand — [[intrinsic-cognitive-load]] (determined by the inherent complexity of the material and the learner's prior knowledge), [[extraneous-cognitive-load|extraneous load]] (imposed by suboptimal instructional design), and [[germane-cognitive-load|germane load]] (devoted to productive [[schema-construction]] and [[schema-automation|automation]]) — and articulates a rich catalog of empirically validated instructional effects, including the [[worked-example-effect]], the [[split-attention-effect]], the [[redundancy-effect]], the [[modality-effect]], and the [[expertise-reversal-effect]]. This report provides a comprehensive foundational treatment of CLT: its cognitive-architectural underpinnings, its theoretical mechanisms (particularly [[element-interactivity]] as the unifying construct), its extensive evidence base, its measurement challenges, and the significant theoretical debates that have reshaped the theory in recent decades — including the controversial reconceptualization of germane load and the theory's complex relationship with [[desirable-difficulties]], [[productive-failure]], and constructivist pedagogies. The analysis traces CLT from its origins in problem-solving research through its maturation into a comprehensive instructional design framework, critically examining both its remarkable explanatory power and its genuine limitations.

> This report undertakes a comprehensive, integrative examination of goal setting and assessment within [[personal-knowledge-management|personal knowledge management]] (PKM) systems, with particular emphasis on the design affordances of [[obsidian]]-based personal knowledge bases (PKBs). The central thesis advanced herein is that effective goal-setting in PKM cannot be understood as a merely procedural matter — a question of writing goals that satisfy conventional specificity and measurability criteria — but must instead be theorised as a deeply motivational, metacognitive, and regulatory phenomenon whose optimisation requires the coordinated application of insights from [[goal-setting-theory|goal-setting theory]] (Locke & Latham), [[achievement-goal-theory|achievement goal theory]] (Dweck, Nicholls, Elliot), [[self-determination-theory|self-determination theory]] (Deci & Ryan), and [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] models (Zimmerman, Pintrich). Throughout, the report attends to the under-theorised intersection between motivational science and PKM system design, proposing that the PKB, when properly architected, functions as an externalised metacognitive system whose structural properties can systematically scaffold the development of [[self-regulation|self-regulation]], [[intrinsic-motivation|intrinsic motivation]], and [[metacognitive-awareness|metacognitive awareness]].

> [!evidence] Abstract
> When Edwin Locke first proposed that conscious goals and intentions are the immediate regulators of human action, what he offered industrial-organizational psychology was not merely a performance-enhancement technique but a theory of how the mind translates desire into directed effort. Four decades of subsequent research — encompassing over a thousand empirical studies and multiple meta-analyses — have confirmed the core mechanism: that specific, challenging goals outperform vague exhortations to "do your best" by narrowing attention, mobilizing effort, increasing persistence, and catalysing strategy development. What this formidable empirical record does not address, however, is how goal-setting operates when the domain of application is not an externally assigned task but the self-authored construction of a personal knowledge system — a system whose purpose is not merely to store information but to scaffold the very cognitive processes that make learning possible. This report undertakes a comprehensive integration of [[goal-setting-theory|goal-setting theory]] (Locke & Latham), [[achievement-goal-theory|achievement goal theory]] (Dweck, Elliot, Ames), [[self-determination-theory|self-determination theory]] (Deci & Ryan), [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] models (Zimmerman, Pintrich, Winne & Hadwin), and [[metacognition|metacognitive]] frameworks (Flavell, Nelson & Narens) to analyse the language, design, and assessment of goals within [[personal-knowledge-management|personal knowledge management]] systems — specifically, Obsidian-based personal knowledge bases (PKBs). The central argument is that the *language* in which goals are framed is not a merely cosmetic feature of goal design but a causally operative variable that shapes motivational quality, regulatory behaviour, and metacognitive development. The report traces how goal framing interacts with [[autonomous-motivation|autonomous motivation]], [[mastery-goal|mastery orientation]], and [[metacognitive-monitoring|metacognitive monitoring]] to produce — or undermine — the conditions for genuine [[self-directed-learning|self-directed learning]], and it proposes design principles for goal-setting architectures within PKM systems that honour the full complexity of human motivational and cognitive architecture.
