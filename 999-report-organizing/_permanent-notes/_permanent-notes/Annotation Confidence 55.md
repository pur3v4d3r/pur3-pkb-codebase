## Core Explanation

> [!evidence] Annotation: Confidence 5/5
> **Source basis:** [[george-miller|Miller's]] (1956) foundational work on working memory capacity, substantially refined by [[Nelson-Cowan|Cowan]] (2001) and [[alan-baddeley|Baddeley's]] (2000) multicomponent model. The 4±1 estimate is from Cowan's embedded processes model, which is now the more widely accepted figure over Miller's classic "7±2."
>
> **Alternatives considered:** (1) That motivation or affect, rather than cognitive architecture, is the primary PKM constraint. Rejected because motivational factors modulate *how much* of working memory capacity is deployed, but cannot expand the capacity itself. (2) That modern digital tools have effectively eliminated the WM bottleneck through external storage. Partially valid — [[cognitive-offloading]] does reduce WM demands — but the bottleneck reasserts itself at the point of comprehension and integration, which cannot be fully offloaded.
>
> **Confidence rationale:** Maximum confidence because the WM capacity constraint is one of the most robustly established findings in [[cognitive-science]]. The application to PKM specifically is interpretive but follows directly from the established mechanism.
> *— [[cognitive-load-theory-and-pkm-annotated-critical-analysis-2026-04-12_report]]*

> [!evidence] Annotation: Confidence 5/5
> **Source basis:** Clark & Wilkes-Gibby (1986) demonstrated through referential communication tasks that interlocutors actively collaborate to establish what words mean in context — a process they called "grounding." Partners converge on shared labels through interactive negotiation, not one-way decoding. This finding has been replicated extensively in psycholinguistics. Grice (1975) formalized the cooperative principle showing that utterances underdetermine their own meaning: comprehension requires pragmatic inference about what the speaker *intended*, not just what the words *mean*.
>
> **Alternatives considered:** (1) The transmission model could be defended as a useful simplification that works for simple factual messages. This is partially true — for unambiguous factual messages in shared domains ("the meeting is at 3pm"), transmission framing mostly works. The problem is that most consequential communication — about values, goals, emotions, complex ideas — is not of this type. (2) Social constructivist accounts could be seen as overcorrecting, making all meaning radically indeterminate. Rejected because while meaning is not fixed, it is also not infinitely malleable — grounding processes converge on stable enough interpretations for coordination.
>
> **Confidence rationale:** 5/5 because the joint-achievement view is the current scientific consensus across cognitive linguistics, pragmatics, and communication science.
> *— [[how-to-communicate-effectively-annotated-critical-analysis-2026-04-04_report]]*

> [!evidence] Annotation: Confidence 5/5
> **Source basis:** Grice (1975) remains one of the most cited papers in philosophy of language and linguistics. The pragmatic reasoning schemas framework (Cheng & Holyoak, 1985) operationalizes similar intuitions in cognitive terms. Applied research on ambiguity, misunderstanding, and conflict in organizations consistently returns to Gricean violations as a proximate cause.
>
> **Alternatives considered:** Speech act theory (Austin, 1962; Searle, 1969) provides a complementary framework focusing on illocutionary force. Neither is wrong; they are compatible but focus on different aspects of communication competence. Relevance Theory (Sperber & Wilson, 1986) revises Grice, reducing the maxims to a single relevance principle. This is probably theoretically superior but practically the four-maxim framing is more usable for communication development.
>
> **Confidence rationale:** 5/5 for the basic Gricean framework; the specific claim about violations predicting misunderstanding is 4/5 because the empirical work is primarily experimental rather than ecologically valid natural conversation studies.
> *— [[how-to-communicate-effectively-annotated-critical-analysis-2026-04-04_report]]*

> **Source basis:** This two-component architecture is supported by [[flavell]]'s original framework (1976, 1979), elaborated by [[ann-l.-brown]] (1978, 1987), and refined by virtually every subsequent metacognition researcher including [[gregory-schraw]] (Schraw & Dennison, 1994), [[efklides]] (2008), and [[john-borkowski]] (Borkowski, Carr, & Pressley, 1987). The distinction between monitoring and regulation appears across multiple measurement instruments including the [[Metacognition-Awareness-Inventory]] (Schraw & Dennison, 1994).
>
> **Alternatives considered:** Some theorists, notably the [[nelson-narens-model]] proponents, prefer a monitoring-control framing rather than monitoring-regulation. I treat these as terminologically different but structurally equivalent. The more substantive alternative is Efklides's (2008) three-component model that separates metacognitive experiences as a third component. I acknowledge this but adopt the two-component framing for parsimony, noting that metacognitive experiences (feelings, judgements) can be treated as monitoring outputs.
>
> **Confidence rationale:** This is among the most well-established findings in [[educational-psychology]]. The two-component model has been replicated across age groups, cultures, and task domains for nearly five decades.
