# Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985)

> [!definition] Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The psychological meaning an event carries for the person who experiences it, specifically with respect to two possible aspects: the **controlling aspect** (the degree to which the event is experienced as pressuring the person to behave in a particular way, implying that the event is managing or directing behaviour) and the **informational aspect** (the degree to which the event is experienced as providing competence-relevant feedback, conveying information about how well one is doing relative to a standard). Every external event — reward, praise, deadline, feedback, surveillance — carries *both* aspects to some degree, but one tends to be more salient. It is this relative salience, not the objective nature of the event, that determines its motivational effect. Events whose controlling aspect is most salient shift PLOC external and undermine intrinsic motivation. Events whose informational aspect is most salient enhance intrinsic motivation by supporting perceived competence, provided PLOC remains internal.

> [!evidence] Functional Significance (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
> The functional significance of an external event refers to its perceived motivational role — the function the agent interprets the event as serving in relation to their behavior. CET identifies three functional aspects: (1) the *informational* aspect, in which the event conveys competence-relevant information that supports the autonomy of the individual; (2) the *controlling* aspect, in which the event applies pressure toward specific outcomes or behavioral compliance, thereby shifting the perceived locus of causality toward external; and (3) the *amotivating* aspect, in which the event conveys incompetence or signals the impossibility of effective action. Informational events tend to enhance intrinsic motivation; controlling events tend to undermine it; amotivating events tend to produce a collapse of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.
>
> **Boundary:** Functional significance is not an objective property of events but a product of the individual's interpretive process. The same event — say, a competitive ranking — can serve a primarily informational function for one person (confirming their mastery) and a primarily controlling function for another (exerting pressure to maintain status). Context, individual differences in [[causality-orientations-theory|causality orientations]], and the interpersonal climate of the event's delivery all influence the functional significance that is perceived.
>
