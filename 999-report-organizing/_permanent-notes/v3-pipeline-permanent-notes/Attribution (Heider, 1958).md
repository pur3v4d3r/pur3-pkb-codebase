# Attribution (Heider, 1958)

> [!definition] Attribution (Heider, 1958)
> The cognitive process of assigning a cause to an observed event, outcome, or behaviour. Attribution is the genus; causal attribution is the species most relevant to motivational theory. The process can be spontaneous or deliberate, rapid or extended, and its products — causal explanations — serve the forward-looking function of guiding future action and expectation.
>
> **Boundary:** Attribution is distinct from perception (which registers *what* happened), evaluation (which judges *whether* what happened was good or bad), and prediction (which estimates *what will* happen). Attribution occupies the specifically explanatory register.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The foundational concept from which the entire theoretical architecture derives.
>
> **See also:** [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]], [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]], [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]], [[fritz-heider]]

## Additional Material (Merged)

> [!definition] Causal Attribution (Heider, 1958; Weiner, 1985)
> [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]] refers to the cognitive process through which an individual identifies and assigns a cause to an observed event, outcome, or behaviour — constructing an explanatory account that connects what happened to why it happened. The process is not neutral: the particular cause selected reshapes the attributor's emotional experience, future expectations, and subsequent behavioural tendencies in systematic and predictable ways.
>
> **Boundary:** Causal attribution is distinct from mere description (noting *what* happened) and from evaluation (judging *whether* what happened was good or bad). It occupies the specifically explanatory register: the *because* that links event to origin.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Attribution theory's entire edifice rests on the claim that this process — seemingly simple, often automatic — is the mechanism through which objective events acquire their motivational meaning.
>
> **See also:** [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]], [[fritz-heider]], [[personal-causation]], [[bernard-weiner]]

> The specific form of attribution concerned with identifying *why* an event occurred — assigning a cause that connects outcome to origin. Causal attributions are the inputs to Weiner's three-dimensional taxonomy, and their dimensional coordinates determine their emotional and motivational consequences.
>
> **Boundary:** Not all attributions are causal. Responsibility attributions, for example, involve a moral judgment that goes beyond causal identification, incorporating notions of intention, controllability, and obligation.
