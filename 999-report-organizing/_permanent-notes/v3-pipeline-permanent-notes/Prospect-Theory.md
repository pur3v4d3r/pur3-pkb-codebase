# Prospect-Theory

> [!definition] Prospect-Theory
> - **Key-Term**: [[Prospect-Theory]]
> - **Definition**: Prospect-Theory is a behavioral economic theory that describes how people make decisions under risk, suggesting that individuals evaluate potential gains and losses differently than what would be predicted by expected utility theory.
> - **Domain**: other
> - **Status**: 🌱 budding | Confidence: speculative

## Core Explanation

> [!analytical-insight] Core Explanation
> Prospect-Theory posits that decision-making involves the evaluation of potential outcomes in terms of gains or losses relative to a reference point. It introduces two key concepts: value functions, which are S-shaped and indicate diminishing sensitivity to changes in wealth for both gains and losses, and probability weighting functions, which distort perceived probabilities, often leading to risk aversion for small probabilities and risk seeking for larger ones.

> [!analytical-insight] Explanation 2
> The theory was developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky through a series of experiments that demonstrated systematic deviations from expected utility theory. These findings suggest that people do not always act in ways predicted by traditional economic models, particularly when faced with risky choices.

> [!analytical-insight] Explanation 3
> Key nuances include the concept of loss aversion, where losses loom larger than equivalent gains, and the certainty effect, which describes a preference for certain outcomes over uncertain ones even if the expected value is lower.

## Practical Implications

> [!example] Application
> In finance, Prospect-Theory can explain why investors might be more willing to take risks in order to avoid perceived losses rather than to achieve equivalent gains.

> [!example] Application
> In public policy, understanding these biases can help design more effective interventions and communication strategies that account for how people perceive risk and value outcomes.
