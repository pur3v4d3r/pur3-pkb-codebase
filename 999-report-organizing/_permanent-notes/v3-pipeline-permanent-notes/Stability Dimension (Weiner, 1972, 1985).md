# Stability Dimension (Weiner, 1972, 1985)

> [!definition] Stability Dimension (Weiner, 1972, 1985)
> The second dimension, classifying causes as stable (enduring across time) or unstable (temporary, changeable). Primary determinant of expectancy change: stable causes produce the expectation that the same outcome will recur; unstable causes leave the future open.
>
> **Boundary:** Stability is perceived, not objective. The same cause (e.g., ability) can be classified as stable by an entity theorist and unstable by an incremental theorist.
>
> **See also:** [[stability-dimension]], [[the-stability-expectancy-mechanism]], [[expectancy-value-theory]]

## Additional Material (Merged)

> [!definition] The Stability Dimension (Weiner, 1972, 1985)
> The [[stability-dimension]] classifies causes as either stable (enduring, likely to persist across time and situations) or unstable (temporary, likely to change). Ability is typically perceived as stable; effort as unstable. Luck is unstable; task difficulty is relatively stable. This dimension is the primary determinant of expectancy change: stable causes produce the inference that the same outcome will recur, while unstable causes leave room for different outcomes in the future.
>
> **Boundary:** Stability is a property of the perceived cause, not of the outcome itself. The same outcome (failure) can be attributed to a stable cause (low ability) or an unstable cause (insufficient effort), with radically different implications for future expectancy.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The stability dimension is the linchpin of the entire attribution-expectancy mechanism. It is the pathway through which causal explanations exert their most consequential influence on motivation.
>
> **See also:** [[stability-dimension]], [[stability]], [[the-stability-expectancy-mechanism]], [[expectancy-value-theory]]

> [!definition] The Stability-Expectancy Principle
> The stability of a cause influences how expectations change following an outcome.

> [!evidence] The Stability-Expectancy Principle
> The stability of an attributed cause is the primary determinant of expectancy change following an outcome. Attributing an outcome to a stable cause shifts expectancy in the direction of that outcome (success → expect more success; failure → expect more failure). Attributing an outcome to an unstable cause produces little or no expectancy change, leaving the field open for different outcomes in the future. This principle — not the locus dimension, and not the controllability dimension — is the engine through which attribution most powerfully shapes motivational trajectories over time.
