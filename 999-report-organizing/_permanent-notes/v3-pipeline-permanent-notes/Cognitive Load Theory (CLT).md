# Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)

> [!definition] Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)
> A theoretical framework positing that instructional design must account for the limited processing capacity of working memory by managing three types of cognitive load: [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)|intrinsic load]] (determined by element interactivity of the material relative to learner expertise), [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)|extraneous load]] (imposed by suboptimal instructional design), and [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)|germane load]] (devoted to schema construction and automation). Effective instruction minimizes extraneous load, manages intrinsic load through sequencing and scaffolding, and optimizes germane processing.
>
> **Boundary:** CLT addresses the cognitive demands of learning, not motivation, affect, or social dimensions of instruction. Its prescriptions are strongest for well-structured domains and novice to intermediate learners.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** CLT provides the primary theoretical constraint system for the 4C/ID model. Every design decision in 4C/ID can be traced to a CLT principle regarding load management.
>
> **See also:** [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]], [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]], [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]], [[Technical Detail: The relationship between element interactivity and working-memory load]], [[working-memory]]

> [!definition] Element Interactivity (Sweller, 1994)
> The number of information elements that must be processed simultaneously — held in working memory at the same time — to achieve understanding. Element interactivity is the primary determinant of intrinsic cognitive load and varies as a function of both material complexity and learner expertise (expert schemas chunk multiple elements into single units, reducing interactivity).
>
> **Boundary:** Element interactivity is not a fixed property of content but a relational property of the content-learner interaction. The same material has high element interactivity for novices and low element interactivity for experts.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The construct that bridges CLT's architectural claims to its empirical predictions; all CLT effects are fundamentally effects of element interactivity management.
>
> **See also:** [[working-memory]], [[Chunk (Miller, 1956; Chase & Simon, 1973)]], [[Expertise Reversal Effect (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, Sweller, 2003)]]

> [!definition] Element Interactivity (Sweller, 2010)
> The degree to which elements of a learning task must be processed simultaneously in [[working-memory|working memory]] because they are logically or functionally interdependent. High element interactivity means that understanding any single element requires understanding its relationships with multiple other elements, generating intrinsic cognitive load proportional to the number of interacting elements.
>
> **Boundary:** Element interactivity is a property of the interaction between material content and learner expertise, not of the material alone. As expertise develops and schemas form, previously interacting elements are chunked into single elements, reducing effective element interactivity.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The primary driver of [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)|intrinsic cognitive load]] and the fundamental reason why complex learning cannot be reduced to simple learning accumulated.
>
> **See also:** [[Technical Detail: The relationship between element interactivity and working-memory load]], [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]], [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]], [[working-memory]]

> [!definition] Element Interactivity (formal)
> The number of *information elements* that must be processed *simultaneously* in [[working-memory]] for the to-be-learned material to be understood, where two elements are said to *interact* when the meaning of one depends on the meaning of the other in a manner that prevents independent serial processing.
> **Two operative variables:** *element count* (how many) and *relational structure* (how the elements depend on one another).
> **Critical relativization:** element interactivity is *not* a property of the material in isolation but a property of the material *relative to the learner's existing schemas*. A relation that two novices must hold as two separate elements may be encoded for the expert as a single element by virtue of [[Schema Construction]]. This relativization is the lever on which the modern theory turns.

> [!definition] Extraneous Cognitive Load (Sweller, Ayres, & Kalyuga, 2011)
> [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]] is the working memory demand imposed by the instructional format and presentation method rather than by the learning content itself. It is, by definition, unnecessary — a product of suboptimal design rather than inherent complexity. Poorly organized materials, redundant information sources, spatially separated related elements, and any design feature that forces the learner to engage in cognitive processing that does not contribute to [[Schema Construction]] generates extraneous load.
>
> **Boundary:** The identification of a processing demand as "extraneous" presupposes that the demand arises from design choices rather than content requirements. What counts as extraneous can shift with expertise level — a scaffolding device that reduces extraneous load for novices may become a source of extraneous load for experts (the [[Expertise Reversal Effect (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, Sweller, 2003)]]).
>
> **See also:** [[split-attention-effect]], [[redundancy-effect]], [[Modality Effect]]

> [!definition] Germane Cognitive Load (Sweller, van Merriënboer, & Paas, 1998)
> [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]] was originally defined as the working memory resources devoted to the construction and automation of schemas — the "productive" load that contributes directly to learning. Unlike extraneous load, which is to be minimized, germane load was conceptualized as load to be *optimized*: instruction should be designed to redirect working memory capacity freed by extraneous load reduction toward schema-building processes.
>
> **Boundary:** The concept of germane load underwent significant reconceptualization after Sweller (2010) argued that it does not constitute a genuinely independent source of load but rather describes what learners *do* with available cognitive resources. This reconceptualization is treated in depth in Section 4.
>
> **See also:** [[germane-processing]], [[Schema Construction]], [[Schema Automation]], [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]]

> [!definition] Intrinsic Cognitive Load (Sweller, 1994; Sweller & Chandler, 1994)
> [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]] is the working memory demand imposed by the inherent complexity of the material to be learned, determined by the number of information elements that must be processed simultaneously and the degree to which those elements interact. Intrinsic load is a function of two variables: the nature of the material itself and the learner's existing schema base — making it, critically, *not* a fixed property of content alone but a relational property of the content-learner interaction.
>
> **Boundary:** Intrinsic load cannot be altered by changing instructional format or presentation method. It can only be modified by changing what is being taught (e.g., simplifying the task, sequencing from simple to complex) or by changing the learner's existing knowledge (since schemas reduce element interactivity).
>
> **See also:** [[Technical Detail: The relationship between element interactivity and working-memory load]], [[Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)]], [[Schema Construction]]
