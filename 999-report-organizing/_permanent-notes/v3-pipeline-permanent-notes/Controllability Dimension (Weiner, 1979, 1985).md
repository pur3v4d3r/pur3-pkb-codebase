# Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)

> [!definition] Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)
> The third dimension, classifying causes as controllable (effort, strategy) or uncontrollable (ability, luck, illness) by the individual. Primary determinant of social-moral affects: controllable failure causes generate anger (in observers) and guilt (in actors); uncontrollable failure causes generate pity (in observers) and shame (in actors).
>
> **Boundary:** Controllability is a property of the *cause*, not the *outcome*. The question is not "Can you control what happened?" but "Can you control the factor that made it happen?"
>
> **See also:** [[Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)]], [[Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)]], [[controllability-moral-inference-bridge]]

## Additional Material (Merged)

> [!definition] The Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)
> The [[Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)]] dimension — the last of the three to be formally incorporated into the taxonomy — classifies causes as either controllable by the individual (effort, strategy choice, attention allocation) or uncontrollable (ability, mood, illness, luck). This dimension cuts across both locus and stability: effort is internal, unstable, and controllable; ability is internal, stable, and uncontrollable; task difficulty is external, stable, and uncontrollable.
>
> **Boundary:** Controllability is a property of the cause, not a judgment about the person. The question is not "Can you control the outcome?" but "Can you control the factor that caused the outcome?" This distinction matters because attributions to uncontrollable causes generate pity and sympathy from observers, while attributions to controllable causes generate anger and blame.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The controllability dimension is the primary bridge between attribution theory and moral judgment. It is through [[Controllability Dimension (Weiner, 1979, 1985)]] that explanations for behaviour become evaluations of character — the mechanism through which a failure attributed to laziness (controllable) provokes anger, while the same failure attributed to illness (uncontrollable) provokes compassion.
>
