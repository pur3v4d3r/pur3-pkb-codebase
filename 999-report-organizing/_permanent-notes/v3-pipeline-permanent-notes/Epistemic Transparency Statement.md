> [!methodology-and-sources] Epistemic Transparency Statement
> This report synthesizes knowledge from three primary intellectual traditions: (1) **cognitive psychology**, specifically the working memory and information-processing literature originating with Baddeley, Miller, and Cowan; (2) **educational psychology**, specifically the instructional design research programme initiated by Sweller and extended by Paas, van Merriënboer, Kalyuga, and Mayer; and (3) **the science of learning**, including the desirable difficulties framework (Bjork & Bjork), transfer research (Halpern, Perkins & Salomon, Barnett & Ceci), and self-regulated learning (Zimmerman, Winne).
>
> **Claim Type Taxonomy:**
>
> | Claim Type | Epistemic Status | Example from Report |
> |------------|-----------------|---------------------|
> | Working memory capacity limits | Established (empirical consensus) | ~4±1 elements for novel information |
> | CLT effect principles (worked example, split attention, etc.) | Established (replicated across decades) | Worked examples outperform problem-solving for novices |
> | Tripartite load taxonomy | Established but reconceptualized | Intrinsic + extraneous + germane → intrinsic + extraneous |

>
> **Distinction between established findings and original contributions:** All CLT effect principles, the working memory architecture, and the load taxonomy are well-established empirical findings. The reconciliation of CLT with desirable difficulties reflects an emerging consensus in the literature. The metacognitive load paradox and the load-motivation interface observations are original analytical contributions by this report's author (Claude, Anthropic) and carry speculative epistemic status — they are well-motivated by the evidence but have not been independently proposed or tested in the research literature.

> **Limitations:** This report draws exclusively on published English-language sources and may not represent CLT research conducted in other languages. The physiological measurement section reflects published literature through early 2025; rapid advances in neuroimaging may have expanded the field since. The report prioritizes the Sweller tradition of CLT; alternative information-processing accounts of instructional design (e.g., cognitive flexibility theory, anchored instruction) receive less attention than they merit.
