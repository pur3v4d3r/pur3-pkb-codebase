## Phase I: Orientation & Analytical Focus

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Schema Activation — Before You Begin**
> Before reading this analysis, take a moment to articulate your current understanding of how people explain their failures and successes. When a student fails a test, what explanations are available to them? Which explanations lead to persisting and trying harder, and which lead to giving up? What do you believe determines whether an explanation is motivationally adaptive or maladaptive? Write this down — not just in your head, but as an explicit record. The comparison between your starting position and where this analysis takes you is where the deepest learning lies.

Every person who has ever failed at something has had to answer a question that feels deceptively simple: *Why did that happen?* Attribution Theory, in its most accessible form, is the psychology of how people answer this question — and what those answers do to them motivationally. At this level, the theory seems almost self-evident: attributing failure to effort ("I didn't try hard enough") feels better for motivation than attributing it to ability ("I'm just not smart enough"), and we teach students accordingly.

This framing, though not wrong, substantially understates what [[attribution-theory]] actually claims and what makes it powerful. The standard account leaves out the theory's most important architectural feature: the emotion-mediated causal chain that converts causal cognition into motivational consequence. [[bernard-weiner]]'s mature model, developed across three decades from the 1970s through the 1990s, is not simply a theory about what people believe caused their outcomes. It is a theory about how specific causal beliefs generate specific emotional responses, which then — and only then — drive behavior. Remove the emotional mediation layer and the theory's predictive power collapses to the level of common sense. Restore it, and the theory reveals mechanisms invisible to cognitive-only accounts.

**The Central Analytical Question**: How does the structural architecture of causal attributions — specifically the three-dimensional classification by locus, stability, and controllability — generate *differentiated emotional responses* that then determine motivational trajectories, and what are the practical consequences of this mechanism for understanding maladaptive patterns and designing effective interventions?

This is not a question about whether attributions matter for motivation. They obviously do. It is a question about the *causal pathway* from attribution to motivation, and the answer is both more specific and more actionable than the common account implies.

**Why This Focus Matters**: The distinction between a cognitive and an emotion-mediated account of attribution has direct therapeutic and pedagogical implications. If the cognitive attribution (e.g., "I failed because of low ability") directly drives reduced effort, then the intervention target is the attribution itself — change the belief, change the behavior. But if the causal chain runs: attribution → specific emotion → motivation → behavior, then the emotion is a necessary intermediate variable. Interventions that successfully change cognitive attributions without producing corresponding affective change may fail to propagate through to behavioral change. This is not a theoretical quibble — it is a practical design constraint that the research largely validates.

**Scope and Boundaries**: This analysis focuses on Weiner's achievement-context formulation of attribution theory, with particular attention to the emotion-mediation mechanism and the self-sealing dynamics of the stability dimension. It does not provide a comprehensive survey of all attribution theories (Heider's naive psychology, Kelley's covariation model, Jones and Davis's correspondent inference theory), though it situates Weiner's contribution within that lineage. The treatment of [[Attribution-Retraining-Interventions]] is analytical rather than programmatic — this report is not a practitioner guide to attribution retraining, but an analytical examination of what the theoretical architecture implies about intervention design.

## Phase II: Analytical Framework — The Three-Dimensional Architecture

### 2.1 The Intellectual Lineage: From Heider to Weiner

[[attribution-theory]] as a formal research program begins with Fritz Heider's *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations* (1958), which introduced the fundamental distinction between internal and external causation — between attributing outcomes to the person versus attributing them to the environment. Heider's contribution was primarily conceptual: the observation that people function as "naive scientists," spontaneously constructing causal explanations for social events, and that these explanations have systematic properties that can be studied.

Harold Kelley's covariation model (1967) formalized Heider's insight into a quasi-statistical framework: people determine causation by examining whether an outcome covaries with the person (consistency), with the stimulus (distinctiveness), or with the context (consensus). Jones and Davis's (1965) correspondent inference theory focused on how people infer stable personal dispositions from single actions. These contributions established attribution as a central research domain in social psychology.

> [!definition] **Attribution (Heider, 1958)**
> **Definition:** A causal explanation assigned by an observer (or actor) to account for an outcome — specifically, the assignment of that outcome to an internal source (properties of the person) or an external source (properties of the environment or situation). Attributions are not neutral descriptions; they carry implications about the future, about responsibility, and about appropriate responses.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The basic internal/external distinction is the foundation on which Weiner's three-dimensional model builds — but the analysis will show that locus alone is insufficient to explain the motivational consequences observed in achievement contexts.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** A causal statement qualifies as an internal attribution when the speaker locates causation in the self ("I failed because I'm not good at math") versus an external attribution when causation is located outside ("I failed because the test was unfair").
>
> **Cross-References:** [[causal-induction]], [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], [[Actor-Observer-Bias]]

[[bernard-weiner]]'s distinctive contribution was to move from social perception (how do we explain *others'* behavior?) to achievement motivation (how do our explanations for *our own* outcomes shape our subsequent behavior?), and to argue that the internal/external distinction was insufficient — that two additional dimensions were required to explain the full pattern of motivational consequences.

### 2.2 The Three Dimensions

Weiner proposed that causes in achievement contexts vary along three independent dimensions:

> [!definition] **Locus of Causality (Weiner, 1979)**
> **Definition:** The location of a cause as internal to the person (ability, effort, mood, strategy) or external to the person (task difficulty, luck, other people's actions). This dimension is closely related to but distinct from [[perceived-locus-of-causality]] in SDT — the SDT construct refers to whether the *regulation of behavior* feels internally initiated, whereas Weiner's locus refers to the *location of the cause* of an outcome.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Locus primarily determines the emotional valence of self-directed emotions — specifically pride and shame. Internal attributions for success amplify pride; internal attributions for failure amplify shame. External attributions for either outcome attenuate these self-directed affects.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Ask whether removing the person from the situation would change the probability of the outcome. If yes, the cause is internal; if no, the cause is external.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[attribution-theory]], [[perceived-locus-of-causality]], [[self-determination-theory]]

> [!definition] **Stability (Weiner, 1979)**
> **Definition:** The temporal persistence of a cause — whether it is relatively fixed (stable: ability, task difficulty) or variable (unstable: effort, luck, temporary mood). Stability is the dimension that Weiner argued most powerfully determines *expectancy change* — the degree to which an outcome changes a person's prediction about future outcomes.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the analytically central dimension. Stable attributions create rigid expectancies that are resistant to disconfirmation; unstable attributions leave expectancies open to revision. The self-sealing architecture of maladaptive patterns largely runs through this dimension.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Ask whether the cause will likely be present in the next similar situation. Ability and task difficulty are prototypically stable; effort expenditure and luck are prototypically unstable.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[implicit-theory-of-intelligence]], [[incremental-theory-of-intelligence]], [[growth-mindset-theory]]

> [!definition] **Controllability (Weiner, 1979)**
> **Definition:** Whether the cause is subject to volitional control by the actor or by others. Effort is the prototypical controllable cause; ability, luck, and task difficulty are paradigmatically uncontrollable. Critically, controllability is orthogonal to locus — an internal cause (ability) may be uncontrollable, while an external cause (another person's behavior toward you) may be controllable by them.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Controllability is the primary driver of guilt (for controllable failures attributed to self) and anger (for controllable failures attributed to others). It also generates interpersonal moral inferences about the appropriateness of help and pity — a mechanism with significant ethical implications.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Ask whether the actor could have produced a different level of the causal factor through an act of will. If yes, the cause is controllable; if no, it is uncontrollable.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[autonomous-motivation]], [[achievement-goal-theory]], [[appraisal-theory]]

### 2.3 The Emotion-Mediation Framework

The framework's critical structural claim — and the one most commonly omitted in simplified accounts — is that the path from attribution to motivation is *not direct*. The full causal chain is:

**Outcome → Causal Attribution → Dimension Classification → Specific Affect → Motivational Consequence → Behavior**

> [!key-claim] **The Mediational Core**
> Weiner's mature model (fully articulated in *An Attributional Theory of Motivation and Emotion*, 1986) holds that cognitive causal attributions generate specific, differentiated emotional responses — not just valenced affect — and that these emotions are the proximate causes of motivational and behavioral consequences. The dimensions function as *emotion generators*: locus generates pride/shame, stability generates hopelessness/hope, and controllability generates guilt/anger/pity. The emotions do the motivational work; the dimensions do the affective work.

This emotion-mediation structure is what distinguishes Weiner's mature model from simple belief-behavior theories and puts it in close conversation with [[appraisal-theory]] (Lazarus, 1966, 1991), which independently developed the claim that cognitive appraisals generate differentiated emotional responses.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Conceptual Checkpoint**
> The argument from Phase III forward depends on a clear grasp of the dimensional structure. Before continuing: Can you construct the attribution profile for a student who fails a test and thinks "I failed because I didn't study, and I could have studied if I had chosen to"? What are the locus, stability, and controllability values? What emotions does Weiner's model predict? If you find yourself reaching for the language above rather than generating the analysis independently, the framework may not yet be fully integrated.

*Section-End Summary:* Phase II established that Weiner's contribution is not simply a taxonomy of cause types but a structural model in which causal dimensions function as emotion generators. The three dimensions are analytically independent — and this independence matters because each generates distinct emotions that drive distinct motivational consequences. This architecture sets the analytical problem for Phase III: what does the evidence say about the emotion-mediation mechanism specifically?

>

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Knowledge State — Before**

### 3.1 The Emotion-Attribution Correspondence: What the Evidence Shows

The core evidentiary question is whether specific attributional dimensions reliably generate the specific emotions Weiner's model predicts. The evidence on this correspondence is substantial but more nuanced than is often acknowledged.

Weiner, Russell, and Lerman's (1978, 1979) foundational studies asked participants to recall achievement outcomes and report both their causal attributions and their emotional responses. The pattern of results supported the dimensional emotion-assignment: locus predicted the pride/shame differential (internal success produced more pride than external success; internal failure produced more shame than external failure), and controllability predicted the distinction between guilt (controllable internal failure) and shame (uncontrollable internal failure). The stability-hopelessness link was confirmed by Abramson, Seligman, and Teasdale's (1978) reformulation of learned helplessness, which mapped directly onto Weiner's stability dimension.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **The Stability-Hopelessness Link Is the Strongest**
> Across the attribution literature, the stability-expectancy-hopelessness pathway shows the most consistent empirical support. Studies using both experimental manipulations (feedback framed as indicating stable versus unstable ability differences) and correlational designs (measuring [[attribution-style]] across individuals) reliably find that stable attributions for negative outcomes predict reduced persistence, lower subsequent effort investment, and affective states consistent with helplessness. The locus and controllability emotion-assignments, while supported, are more susceptible to cultural and individual moderators.

The evidence on guilt versus shame — the critical distinction enabled by the locus × controllability combination — is more complex. Tangney, Wagner, and Gramzow (1992) found that the guilt/shame distinction, while theoretically mapped onto controllability, is in practice highly sensitive to the *severity* of the failure and the degree of public versus private failure visibility. Shame, in particular, involves not just the attribution but a global self-evaluation ("I am bad") that goes beyond the specific causal inference ("I failed because of an uncontrollable internal factor"). This suggests that the controllability → shame pathway may be necessary but not sufficient — additional appraisal processes are involved.

> [!tension-identified] **The Cognitive-Emotion Priority Problem**
> A persistent tension in the empirical literature concerns whether the causal attribution genuinely *causes* the emotion (as Weiner's model claims) or whether prior emotional states *determine* the attribution (a mood-congruent attribution account). Studies by Forgas and colleagues using the Affect Infusion Model demonstrate that people in negative moods make more external, unstable attributions for others' behavior — suggesting that emotion influences attribution as much as attribution influences emotion. Weiner's model treats the cognitive appraisal as primary, but the evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship that the standard causal account cannot fully accommodate.

### 3.2 The Anger-Pity Asymmetry in Social Attribution

One of the most empirically robust findings in Weiner's research program concerns social attributions — how we attribute the causes of *others'* negative conditions — and the moral inferences that follow.

Weiner (1980, 1995) demonstrated that when observers attribute another person's negative condition (poverty, illness, failure) to controllable causes (e.g., perceived personal irresponsibility), they report anger and are less willing to help. When they attribute the same condition to uncontrollable causes (e.g., circumstance, disability), they report pity/sympathy and are more willing to help. This controllability → anger/pity → helping pathway has been replicated across numerous stigmatized conditions.

> [!evidence] **The Stigma and Helping Literature**
> Graham and colleagues (1992) showed that the controllability-helping pathway holds even for racially stigmatized conditions — specifically, controllability attributions mediated racial disparities in reported helping intentions. Reisenzein (1986) demonstrated the anger-helping link in behavioral (not just self-report) measures. The robustness of this finding across cultures is more variable — individualistic cultures show stronger controllability effects on helping, consistent with a framework that places more moral weight on personal agency.

This finding is analytically important because it reveals that [[attribution-theory]] is not only a descriptive theory of motivation but also — and this is frequently underappreciated — a theory of *moral cognition*. The controllability dimension functions as a moral judgment mechanism: perceived controllability of another's negative state triggers a judgment that the person is *responsible* and therefore *less deserving* of sympathy or assistance. This is not a peripheral application of the theory; it is central to how attribution shapes social behavior.

> [!analytical-insight] **Attribution Theory as Hidden Moral Philosophy**
> The controllability dimension's function as a moral trigger reveals something important about the structure of folk moral psychology: the boundary between causal explanation and moral judgment is far more permeable than is usually acknowledged. When a person perceives another's obesity as controllable, they are making a causal judgment AND a moral judgment in a single cognitive act — and Weiner's model suggests these two judgments are structurally fused rather than sequentially related. This has significant implications for debates about responsibility, stigma, and policy — domains that are typically treated as involving distinct cognitive operations.

### 3.3 The Self-Serving Bias and Attribution Style

A systematic pattern in the attribution literature complicates the emotion-mediation model: people do not make attributions randomly. The [[Actor-Observer-Bias]] (Jones & Nisbett, 1971) documents the reliable tendency to attribute one's own behavior to situational factors and others' behavior to dispositional factors. The self-serving attributional bias documents the parallel tendency to attribute successes to internal factors (ability, effort) and failures to external factors (bad luck, task difficulty).

[[attribution-style]] — the habitual pattern of causal attribution across situations — was formalized by Abramson, Seligman, and Teasdale (1978) in the context of depression research. Their "explanatory style" construct identified three dimensions — internal/external, stable/unstable, global/specific — and demonstrated that a depressive explanatory style (internal, stable, global attributions for negative events) predicted vulnerability to depression and learned helplessness. This work constitutes perhaps the most clinically significant application of Weiner's dimensional framework.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **The Stability × Globality Interaction Is Clinically Central**
> The most powerful predictor of helplessness and depression in the explanatory style literature is not any single dimension but the *stability × globality* interaction: attributing negative events to causes that are both temporally persistent AND cross-situationally general (e.g., "I'm fundamentally incompetent") generates the most severe expectancy deficits and the most pervasive motivational paralysis. Locus plays a secondary role — internal stable global attributions are worse than external stable global attributions, but both produce significant helplessness when stability and globality are high.

*Section-End Summary:* The evidence confirms the emotion-mediation mechanism's core claims while complicating three aspects: the bidirectionality of the attribution-emotion relationship, the insufficiency of controllability alone for generating shame (which requires a global self-evaluation component), and the cultural moderators of the controllability-helping pathway. Most robustly supported is the stability-expectancy-hopelessness pathway — this is the axis around which the most clinically and educationally significant patterns organize. This finding sets up the mechanism analysis in Phase IV.

> [!reflection] **Integrating the Evidence**
>
> **Comprehension**: What is the most important finding from the stigma/helping research, and what does it imply about the relationship between attribution and moral judgment?
>
> **Application**: Consider a colleague who consistently attributes their professional failures to bad luck and their successes to skill. What does Weiner's model predict about their long-term motivational trajectory? Is this pattern adaptive or maladaptive, and in what sense?
>
> **Extension**: Where do you find the evidence most compelling? Where do you find yourself resistant? What assumptions underlie your resistance?

## Phase IV: Mechanisms, Dynamics & Deep Analysis

> [!important] **Complexity Transition**
> This phase builds directly on the three-dimensional framework from Phase II and the evidence review in Phase III. The mechanism analysis assumes familiarity with the dimensional structure and the emotion-attribution correspondences. The argument here is about causal architecture — how attributional patterns produce self-reinforcing dynamics — and requires sustained attention to multi-step causal chains.

### 4.1 The Stability-Expectancy Lock: A Self-Sealing Architecture

The most mechanistically important phenomenon in achievement attribution is what I will call the **Stability-Expectancy Lock** — the self-reinforcing dynamic through which stable attributions for failure generate expectancy deficits that suppress the effort investment that would otherwise produce disconfirming outcomes.

The mechanism operates as follows. When a student fails an exam and attributes that failure to low ability — an internal, stable, uncontrollable cause — Weiner's model predicts that (1) they will expect future failures in similar domains (stability → low expectancy change), (2) they will experience hopelessness and shame (stable + internal + uncontrollable → shame + hopelessness), and (3) they will reduce effort investment (low expectancy → reduced instrumentality of effort). The reduced effort then makes future failures more likely. When the predicted failure materializes, it appears to *confirm* the original stable attribution. The attribution generates a behavioral pattern that produces confirming evidence for itself.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Confirmation Architecture of Stability Beliefs**
> The self-sealing property of stability attributions is not merely psychological — it has a behavioral feedback structure that makes disconfirmation genuinely difficult. Unlike purely cognitive biases (where the person selectively attends to confirming evidence), stability attributions in achievement contexts generate *actual performance reductions* through their effect on effort investment. This means that even an observer with perfect rationality who tracked the student's performance over time would likely see a pattern consistent with the stable attribution — not because the attribution is correct but because the attribution has made itself correct. This is a motivational analogue of the self-fulfilling prophecy, but with a more precise causal mechanism than Merton's original formulation.

This architecture explains why [[helpless-response-pattern|helpless response patterns]] are so resistant to simple informational correction. Telling a student "You can improve if you try harder" challenges the stable attribution, but if the student's reduced effort has already produced a performance record consistent with the ability attribution, the informational intervention must overcome not just the belief but the behavioral evidence that has accumulated in support of it.

The connection to [[growth-mindset-theory]] is direct. Dweck's distinction between entity theory (intelligence is fixed) and incremental theory (intelligence is malleable) maps precisely onto the stability dimension of Weiner's model: entity theorists make stable attributions for their intellectual performance, while incremental theorists make unstable attributions. The motivational divergence that Dweck's research documents — entity theorists avoiding challenge, incremental theorists seeking it — follows directly from Weiner's stability-expectancy mechanism. [[carol-dweck]]'s contribution was not to identify a new mechanism but to operationalize the stability dimension as an individual difference variable and demonstrate its developmental trajectory.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **The Stability Lock → Organizational Learned Helplessness**
> The stability-expectancy lock operates at the organizational level with striking structural fidelity to the individual level. Organizations that attribute persistent underperformance to stable factors ("this market is inherently difficult," "our workforce lacks the capability for this transformation") exhibit the same reduced investment in change-directed effort, the same expectancy deficits, and the same confirmation dynamics that individual-level research documents. The mechanism is domain-independent: stable causal attribution suppresses motivated action, which produces confirming performance, which reinforces the stable attribution. This is not a metaphor — it is the same causal structure operating at a different level of analysis.

### 4.2 The Controllability Dimension: Moral Cognition in Motivational Clothing

The controllability dimension is the most analytically complex of Weiner's three dimensions because it serves two functionally distinct roles: it generates guilt (for self-directed controllable failure) and it triggers moral judgment (for other-directed controllable failure). These are related but distinct mechanisms, and conflating them produces significant analytical confusion.

**The Guilt Mechanism**: When a person fails at a task they controlled (effort expenditure) and attributes the failure to that controllable factor, guilt is the predicted emotion. Guilt is motivationally adaptive in a specific sense: it is compatible with future effort investment because the causal factor (effort) is controllable and unstable — the person can change it. Compare this to shame (generated by internal, stable, uncontrollable attributions), which is motivationally maladaptive because the causal factor (ability) cannot be modified. This is the functional rationale for the widely-cited clinical distinction between guilt and shame: guilt says "I did something bad," shame says "I am something bad."

> [!definition] **The Guilt-Shame Asymmetry in Motivational Function**
> **Definition:** In Weiner's framework, guilt and shame are both negative self-directed emotions but are generated by different attribution profiles and have opposite motivational implications. Guilt — generated by controllable internal causal attributions — is motivationally facilitative because it implies that the cause is changeable. Shame — generated by uncontrollable internal causal attributions — is motivationally paralyzing because it implies that the cause is fixed.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The practical implication is that not all negative self-directed affect is motivationally equivalent. Interventions that reduce guilt without replacing the attributional substrate may inadvertently reduce a motivationally useful signal. The goal of attribution-based interventions is not to eliminate negative affect but to shift the *type* of negative affect toward guilt and away from shame.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Guilt typically accompanies specific behavioral regret ("I wish I had studied more"); shame typically accompanies global self-evaluation ("I'm just not capable of this").

**The Moral Judgment Mechanism**: The controllability dimension's second function — generating anger and reducing pity toward others whose negative conditions are attributed to controllable causes — is a moral cognition mechanism that operates largely automatically and has profound social consequences.

> [!tension-identified] **The Descriptive-Normative Tension in Controllability Attributions**
> Weiner's model documents that controllability attributions trigger reduced helping and increased anger — but it is not obvious that this is a *rational* or *normatively appropriate* response. The empirical finding that perceived controllability of stigmatized conditions (addiction, obesity, poverty) reduces helping motivation has been used both descriptively (to understand social behavior) and normatively (as an implicit justification for reduced social provision). The theory cannot adjudicate this normative question, but the analytical danger is that its descriptive accuracy is taken to imply normative warrant. The mechanism operates regardless of whether the controllability attribution is accurate — and there is substantial evidence that controllability attributions for complex conditions (addiction, mental illness, structural poverty) systematically overestimate personal control and underestimate systemic factors.

### 4.3 The Appraisal Theory Interface

A genuinely illuminating structural connection exists between Weiner's emotion-attribution architecture and [[appraisal-theory]] as developed by [[richard-lazarus|Lazarus]] (1966, 1991). Both frameworks claim that:

1. Cognitive appraisals generate differentiated emotional responses
2. The appraisal-to-emotion mapping is systematic and predictable
3. Emotions function as information and motivational resources, not simply as epiphenomena

Weiner's model can be read as a specialized application of appraisal theory to achievement contexts, with the three causal dimensions functioning as specific appraisal dimensions. The locus dimension maps onto Lazarus's ego-involvement appraisal (how relevant is this to my self-concept?). The stability dimension maps onto future-oriented coping potential appraisal (can anything change?). The controllability dimension maps onto agency appraisal (who is responsible?).

> [!analytical-insight] **Weiner as Applied Appraisal Theory — and What That Reveals**
> The structural correspondence between Weiner's attribution dimensions and Lazarus's appraisal dimensions is too precise to be coincidental — they are independently converging on the same underlying cognitive structure. What makes this observation useful is that Lazarus's framework has a more developed account of the *social and relational* dimensions of appraisal (particularly his concept of coping potential and the distinction between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping). Reading Weiner through Lazarus suggests that the stability dimension's motivational consequences may be partly mediated by *coping potential appraisals* — and that interventions targeting coping potential may be as effective as those targeting causal attributions directly. This is an underexplored intervention direction in the attribution retraining literature.

*Section-End Summary:* Phase IV has excavated three core mechanisms: the Stability-Expectancy Lock (the self-sealing behavioral feedback dynamic that makes stable attributions for failure self-confirming), the Guilt-Shame Asymmetry (the opposite motivational functions of two internally-generated negative emotions that attribution research often conflates), and the structural correspondence between Weiner's dimensional model and Lazarus's appraisal theory (which suggests intervention approaches beyond direct attribution change). The most powerful single mechanism is the Stability-Expectancy Lock, which explains why maladaptive attributional patterns persist even in the face of objective disconfirmation.

### 5.1 Implications for Intervention Design

The emotion-mediation mechanism generates specific, non-obvious implications for how attribution-based interventions should be designed.

**Implication 1: Cognitive Attribution Change Is Necessary But Not Sufficient**. If the causal chain runs attribution → emotion → motivation → behavior, then successfully shifting the cognitive attribution (e.g., convincing a student that their failure was due to insufficient effort rather than low ability) is necessary but not sufficient for behavioral change. The emotion generated by the new attribution must also shift. Research by [[wilson-and-linville|Wilson and Linville]] (1982, 1985) demonstrated that attributional interventions that produced cognitive change but not affective change showed weak and short-lived behavioral effects. Effective interventions must produce both cognitive and affective reorganization.

> [!best-practice] **Design Attribution Interventions for Affective Change, Not Just Cognitive Change**
> When designing [[Attribution-Retraining-Interventions]], build in explicit mechanisms for affective reorganization, not just cognitive reattribution. This might include: (1) having participants describe emotional responses to new attributional framings (to make the affective component explicit), (2) behavioral experiments that generate success experiences under the new attribution (providing affective confirmation of the new framework), and (3) explicit discussion of the emotion-attribution correspondence so participants understand why the intervention targets emotions as much as beliefs.

**Implication 2: The Target Dimension Matters**. Not all attribution changes are equivalent. Shifting attributions from external to internal (locus change) produces pride/shame differentiation but does not address expectancy or persistence. Shifting from stable to unstable (stability change) directly addresses the expectancy deficit and hopelessness that drive reduced persistence — this is the mechanism that [[attribution-retraining]] programs most often target, and the evidence supports this targeting. Shifting controllability attributions affects guilt/anger but has more complex effects on persistence, depending on the direction of the shift.

**Implication 3: The Self-Sealing Architecture Requires Behavioral Disconfirmation**. Because the Stability-Expectancy Lock generates behavioral evidence confirming the stable attribution, purely verbal or cognitive interventions are insufficient. Effective attribution retraining for established [[helpless-response-pattern|helpless response patterns]] must include behavioral components that disrupt the confirmation loop — specifically, experiences of success under conditions of sufficient effort that provide behavioral evidence for the unstable attribution. This is why Dweck's mindset interventions combine cognitive reframing with the provision of challenging tasks that students can succeed at with sufficient effort.

### 5.2 Limitations of the Framework

> [!warning] **The Measurement Problem**
> A persistent limitation of attribution research is the discrepancy between verbal attribution measures (what people say caused their outcomes) and actual causal cognitions (which may be less accessible to introspection and more automatic). Much attribution research relies on self-report scales or open-ended causal statement coding, but there is evidence that attribution-behavior relationships are stronger when attributions are measured closer in time to the outcome and weaker when measured retrospectively — suggesting that post-hoc verbal attributions may partially reconstruct rather than accurately report actual causal cognitions.

The theory's three-dimensional taxonomy, while powerful, has known limitations. First, the dimensions are not always orthogonal in practice — people's intuitive causal ontologies may not cleanly separate locus from controllability (many people treat internal causes as inherently controllable), which produces the conflation between shame and guilt that the theory analytically separates. Second, the emotion-attribution correspondences are more culturally variable than Weiner's original accounts acknowledged — particularly the pride/shame differentiation based on locus, which is substantially moderated by individualism/collectivism.

> [!warning] **The Cultural Universality Problem**
> Cross-cultural research on attributional patterns and their emotional consequences reveals significant variation. In collectivistic cultures, the locus-pride link is attenuated (public success credited to group rather than self), and internal attributions for failure are not always accompanied by reduced self-esteem (in some cultural contexts, self-criticism for controllable failures is normatively encouraged and motivationally facilitative). The stability-expectancy link appears more culturally robust than the locus-emotion correspondences, suggesting that the stability mechanism may be more fundamental to the architecture.

The theory also has limited scope with respect to the goals and values that give outcomes their significance. Attribution Theory assumes that outcomes are relevant to the person — that success and failure matter — without providing a theory of *why* they matter and *which* outcomes are consequential. This is where [[achievement-goal-theory]] provides a necessary supplement: performance goals (mastery and performance-approach/avoidance) determine what counts as success and failure, and thus which events trigger the attribution process at all. Attribution Theory and Achievement Goal Theory are better read as complementary frameworks than as competing alternatives.

*Section-End Summary:* The most important practical implication of the emotion-mediation mechanism is that effective attribution interventions must target affective reorganization as well as cognitive reattribution — a design constraint that much attribution retraining research has inadequately incorporated. The framework's core limitations are the measurement problem (verbal attributions may not accurately represent actual causal cognitions), cultural variability in the locus-emotion correspondence, and the theory's silence on what makes outcomes motivationally significant (a gap filled by Achievement Goal Theory).

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

### 6.1 Pulling the Threads Together

The analysis across four phases converges on a picture of [[attribution-theory]] that is significantly more architecturally complex than the standard "effort attributions good, ability attributions bad" account. The emotion-mediation mechanism reveals that the three dimensions function as an *affect generation system* that translates causal cognitions into motivationally differentiated emotional states. Of the three dimensions, stability is the most powerful driver of the most significant motivational outcome (persistence after failure), and it operates through a self-sealing behavioral feedback architecture that makes it uniquely resistant to simple informational correction.

The analysis also reveals that [[attribution-theory]] does a great deal of implicit moral work through the controllability dimension — attributional cognitions are not purely descriptive acts but are simultaneously evaluative ones, generating moral appraisals about responsibility and deservingness that organize social behavior. This moral-cognitive fusion is not a peripheral feature but an architectural property of the system.

### 6.2 An Original Synthesis: The Two-Stage Intervention Architecture

> [!original-synthesis] **The Two-Stage Attribution Intervention Architecture**
> The emotion-mediation mechanism, taken seriously as a design constraint, implies that attribution interventions should be structured in two stages rather than the single-stage cognitive reattribution that most programs implement. Stage 1 targets *cognitive attribution accuracy* — specifically, the stability dimension, shifting from stable to unstable causal explanations for failure. This is the standard content of attribution retraining. Stage 2 targets *affective reorganization* — working explicitly with the specific emotions generated by the new attribution profile to ensure that the affective consequence of the cognitive change is (a) experienced and (b) connected to the cognitive reattribution in the learner's model.
>
> The rationale is architectural: if hopelessness (the affect generated by stable attributions for failure) mediates the stability-persistence relationship, then a cognitive shift from stable to unstable attribution that does not produce a corresponding shift from hopelessness to hope has failed to propagate through the motivational chain. The new attribution generates a new affective prediction (hope rather than hopelessness) but this prediction must be tested through experience and confirmed behaviorally to be consolidated. Stage 2 provides this confirmation through structured success experiences under the new attribution framework, combined with explicit attention to the emotional experience of those successes. This is not a minor modification to existing programs — it requires a fundamentally different intervention structure that treats emotional experience as a necessary variable rather than an expected outcome.

### 6.3 The Central Question Revisited

The central analytical question was: how does the structural architecture of causal attributions generate differentiated emotional responses that determine motivational trajectories? The answer, distilled, is: through a three-stage mechanism in which causal attribution classification (locus, stability, controllability) generates specific, discrete emotions (pride/shame, hope/hopelessness, guilt/anger/pity), which then function as the proximate motivational signals driving behavior. The stability dimension is analytically central because it generates the most motivationally consequential emotion (hopelessness/hope) and because it operates through a self-sealing behavioral feedback structure that makes it uniquely resistant to change.

> [!analytical-insight] **Why the Common Account Undersells the Theory**
> The common account — "attributing failure to effort is better than attributing it to ability" — captures one output of the model but misses its generative mechanism. The reason effort attributions are motivationally superior is not that they are more "positive" but that effort is unstable (hence generates hope, not hopelessness) AND controllable (hence generates guilt, not shame) AND internal (hence preserves the self-evaluation that makes future success meaningful). The three-dimensional analysis shows that the motivational superiority of effort attributions is *overdetermined* — it is simultaneously produced by three independent mechanisms. This overdetermination is why effort-attribution interventions are consistently effective: they operate on all three dimensions simultaneously.

*Section-End Summary:* The synthesis reveals that the theory's predictive power is concentrated in the stability-expectancy-hopelessness pathway, that the emotion-mediation mechanism implies a two-stage intervention architecture (cognitive reattribution + affective reorganization), and that the moral-cognitive fusion in the controllability dimension makes Attribution Theory simultaneously a motivational theory and a theory of moral perception. The original contribution is the Two-Stage Intervention Architecture, which operationalizes the emotion-mediation mechanism as a design constraint.

## Phase VII: FAR Transfer — Structural Analogues Across Domains

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Transfer Application**
> The Stability-Expectancy Lock — the mechanism by which stable causal attributions for negative outcomes suppress the motivated action that would otherwise produce disconfirming experience — has now been identified as the architecturally central dynamic in attribution theory. Before reading the transfer analysis: can you independently identify a domain outside psychology where the same structural pattern might operate? What are the relevant analogues for "attribution," "stability," "expectancy," and "confirming behavior"? Your ability to generate these independently is the test of whether the mechanism has been genuinely abstracted.

### 7.1 Abstract Principle Extraction

**Principle 1: The Self-Sealing Causal Attribution**

The analysis revealed that a stable causal attribution for a negative outcome creates a self-confirming architecture through a specific pathway: stable attribution → expectancy deficit → reduced motivated action → actual performance deficit → apparent confirmation of stable attribution. At an abstract structural level, this instantiates a general principle: *when the causal explanation for an outcome suppresses the behavior that would generate disconfirming evidence, the explanation becomes epistemically insulated from correction*. This principle operates whenever an entity has a causal model of its own performance that (a) predicts low outcomes, (b) generates reduced action through expectancy effects, and (c) treats the resulting poor outcomes as confirmation rather than as consequential on the reduced action.

**Principle 2: The Emotion-Mediation Cascade**

The analysis established that cognitive appraisal generates specific, differentiated emotions that then drive motivation — the path from cognition to behavior is not direct but runs through affect. At an abstract structural level, this instantiates the principle: *in systems where behavioral action is governed by motivated states, interventions that change cognitive representations without changing the affective states those representations generate will fail to propagate through to behavioral change*. This principle operates in any domain where the mediating emotional variable is structurally necessary rather than epiphenomenal.

**Principle 3: The Controllability-Moral Inference Bridge**

The controllability dimension automatically triggers moral appraisals about responsibility and deservingness. At an abstract structural level: *perceived controllability of an agent over a negative outcome systematically activates moral evaluation — attributing negative outcomes to controllable causes merges causal description with moral judgment in a single cognitive act*.

### 7.2 Structural Analogues

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Stability Attribution Lock → Organizational Change Failure**
> In organizational change management, teams that attribute persistent underperformance to stable factors ("this culture will never change," "this workforce doesn't have the capability") exhibit the same self-sealing architecture as individuals with stable ability attributions. The stable organizational attribution generates reduced investment in change-directed effort (low expectancy of success makes effort seem wasteful), which produces performance patterns consistent with the original stable attribution. The analogy holds because both share the relational structure: stable causal attribution → expectancy deficit → reduced action → confirming evidence → attribution reinforcement. The diagnostic question is: is the organization reducing effort investment because of the attribution, or is the attribution tracking genuinely stable facts? The intervention prescription follows directly from the psychological literature — behavioral experiments that test the stability assumption by investing effort strategically in specific bounded change projects.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Emotion-Mediation Cascade → Psychotherapy and Cognitive-Behavioral Models**
> Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy's assumption that changing cognitive distortions will automatically improve emotional states represents an intervention architecture that the emotion-mediation mechanism directly challenges. CBT targets the cognitive level (catastrophic thinking, negative automatic thoughts) with the expectation that emotional change will follow. But if the relationship between cognition and emotion is bidirectional — as the attribution literature suggests — then purely cognitive interventions may insufficiently address the affective substrate. The structural analogue identifies why CBT works when it does (cognitive change that cascades into affective reorganization) and why it sometimes doesn't (cognitive change that fails to reorganize the affective patterns). The intervention prescription: cognitive reattribution should be paired with behavioral experiments that generate direct affective disconfirmation of the catastrophic prediction — not just cognitive evidence, but visceral affective experience.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Self-Sealing Architecture in AI Alignment**
> A structurally isomorphic dynamic appears in discussions of AI systems trained on their own outputs: a system that makes predictions about its own performance capabilities, reduces its exploration based on those predictions, and then interprets the reduced output variance as confirmation of its capability ceiling. The structural elements are all present — stable "attribution" about capability, reduced motivated exploration, confirming evidence. The transfer is imperfect (AI systems don't have emotions in Weiner's sense), but the *epistemic* structure of the self-sealing attribution — where the causal model insulates itself from disconfirmation by shaping the evidence generation process — is genuinely domain-independent and potentially valuable for understanding training dynamics.

### 7.3 Transfer Encoding

**For Principle 1 (Self-Sealing Causal Attribution)**:
- *Trigger pattern*: Observe an entity reducing action in a domain while citing stable causal factors as explanation
- *Diagnostic question*: Is the entity's current low performance a consequence of reduced action (caused by the stable attribution) or evidence of genuinely stable limiting factors independent of action?
- *Transferable prediction*: Behavioral experiments that force action investment — regardless of expectancy — will produce disconfirming evidence if the stability attribution is false, and will not if it is true. This is the intervention and the diagnostic simultaneously.

**For Principle 2 (Emotion-Mediation Cascade)**:
- *Trigger pattern*: An intervention that successfully changes stated beliefs or cognitive representations without producing corresponding behavioral change
- *Diagnostic question*: Did the cognitive change generate a corresponding change in the affective state that mediates the cognition-behavior link?
- *Transferable prediction*: Adding an affective reorganization component — structured experiences that generate visceral confirmation of the new cognitive framework — will improve behavioral uptake of cognitive interventions

**Application Bridge 1 — Instructional Design**: Apply Principle 2 to course design by pairing any conceptual reframing exercise (changing students' causal models of their learning) with immediate behavioral application tasks that generate affective confirmation of the new model. The key adaptation: the emotional experience must be connected explicitly to the attribution reframe, not just provided as a success experience.

**Application Bridge 2 — Clinical Coaching**: Apply Principle 1 to clients with fixed beliefs about their professional limitations by designing behavioral experiments specifically constructed to test the stability assumption — not to "prove" the client wrong, but to generate evidence that is genuinely informative about whether the cause is stable or malleable.

**Meta-Transfer Reflection**: The insights that transfer most readily from Attribution Theory are the ones that are structurally specified — the Stability-Expectancy Lock transfers because its mechanism is fully articulated (stable attribution → expectancy → action → evidence → confirmation), not because "beliefs affect behavior." The more precisely a mechanism is specified, the more tractable transfer becomes. This is a meta-principle about knowledge structure: mechanistic understanding transfers; pattern recognition does not.

*Section-End Summary:* The three abstract principles extracted from this analysis — the Self-Sealing Causal Attribution, the Emotion-Mediation Cascade, and the Controllability-Moral Inference Bridge — travel to organizational behavior, psychotherapy, instructional design, and clinical coaching with structural fidelity. In each domain, the mechanism is the same; only the specific actors and operations differ. The most practically valuable transfer is the diagnostic and intervention prescription implied by Principle 1: test stability assumptions through behavioral experiments, not through argument.

## Phase IX: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **The Three-Dimensional Causal Taxonomy (Weiner, 1979)**
> **Definition:** The classification of achievement causes along three independent dimensions — locus (internal/external), stability (stable/unstable), and controllability (controllable/uncontrollable) — that together determine the emotional and motivational consequences of causal attributions. The taxonomy replaces the simpler internal/external distinction with a structure sufficient to generate distinct predictions about pride, shame, guilt, anger, pity, hope, and hopelessness.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This taxonomy is the generative architecture of the theory — not a descriptive catalogue of cause types but a predictive mechanism for emotion and motivation.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Classify any stated cause using three binary questions: (1) Is the cause in the person or outside? (2) Will the cause likely persist in future situations? (3) Could the person have controlled the cause through volition?
>
> **Cross-References:** [[attribution-theory]], [[bernard-weiner]], [[causal-induction]]

> [!definition] **Attribution Style / Explanatory Style (Abramson, Seligman & Teasdale, 1978)**
> **Definition:** A habitual pattern of causal attribution across situations, characterized by three dimensions: internal/external, stable/unstable, global/specific. Depressive explanatory style (internal, stable, global attributions for negative events) predicts vulnerability to learned helplessness and depression.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Attribution style transforms the situational mechanism described by Weiner into an individual difference variable with developmental history and clinical implications.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Measured via the Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ) or the Children's Attributional Style Questionnaire (CASQ); coding of open-ended causal statements.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[attribution-style]], [[helpless-response-pattern]], [[growth-mindset]]

> [!definition] **The Stability-Expectancy Mechanism (Weiner, 1985)**
> **Definition:** The causal pathway by which stable attributions for outcomes (particularly failures) generate expectancy deficits — reduced estimates of future success — through the logical inference that a cause that was present and stable in the past will be present in the future. Expectancy deficits in turn reduce the perceived instrumentality of effort investment.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the analytically central mechanism of the report — the primary driver of the most educationally and clinically significant motivational outcomes.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Measured via post-outcome expectancy ratings; behavioral observation of persistence after failure; response to challenge conditions.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[implicit-theory-of-intelligence]], [[growth-mindset-theory]], [[achievement-goal-theory]]

> [!definition] **Emotion Mediation in Attribution (Weiner, 1986)**
> **Definition:** The structural claim that the causal path from attribution to behavior is not direct but runs through specific discrete emotions generated by dimensional classification. The attribution generates the emotion; the emotion drives the behavior. Interventions that change attributions without producing affective change fail to propagate through to behavior.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the most theoretically important and most frequently overlooked feature of Weiner's mature model.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Demonstrated by showing that controlling for emotion eliminates the attribution-behavior association; and that inducing emotions without attribution change produces behavior changes consistent with the emotion-behavior rather than the attribution-behavior link.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[appraisal-theory]], [[motivational-psychology]], [[attribution-retraining]]

> [!definition] **Controllability-Moral Inference Bridge (Weiner, 1993, 1995)**
> **Definition:** The automatic cognitive process by which perceived controllability of another person's negative condition triggers moral evaluation — specifically, reduced sympathy and increased anger — rather than purely descriptive causal cognition. The attribution and the moral judgment are generated in a single cognitive act rather than sequentially.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Reveals that Attribution Theory is simultaneously a descriptive motivational theory and an implicit moral philosophy — and that the normative use of controllability attributions in policy contexts is not automatically warranted by their descriptive accuracy.
>
> **Operational Indicators:** Operationalized via helping intention measures conditioned on controllability manipulations; anger and pity ratings as a function of controllability attributions for stigmatized conditions.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[Actor-Observer-Bias]], [[belonging-uncertainty]], [[attribution-style]]

### B. Key Figures & Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **Bernard Weiner (b. 1935)**
> **Core Contribution:** Developed the three-dimensional attribution model (locus, stability, controllability) and the emotion-mediation framework that constitutes the mature theory. His work transformed attribution theory from a social perception framework (focused on how people explain others' behavior) into an achievement motivation theory (focused on how self-attributions generate emotional and motivational consequences).
>
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Built directly on Heider's internal/external distinction and Kelley's covariation model; corresponds with Lazarus's independent development of appraisal theory; influenced Abramson, Seligman, and Teasdale's reformulated learned helplessness model; provided the theoretical foundation for Dweck's implicit theory of intelligence research.
>
> **Key Works:** *An Attributional Theory of Achievement Motivation and Emotion* (1985); *An Attributional Theory of Motivation and Emotion* (1986); *Judgments of Responsibility: A Foundation for a Theory of Social Conduct* (1995).
>
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** The emotion-mediation framework developed in his 1986 book is the specific theoretical contribution that this analysis excavates and applies.

> [!person] **Fritz Heider (1896–1988)**
> **Core Contribution:** Established the fundamental internal/external causal distinction and the "naive psychology" framework — the observation that people spontaneously construct systematic causal explanations for social events. His book *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations* (1958) initiated the attribution research program.
>
> **Relationship to Other Figures:** Intellectual grandfather of Weiner, Kelley, Jones and Davis. His phenomenological approach to social cognition positioned attribution as a study of common sense reasoning rather than formal logic.
>
> **Key Works:** *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations* (1958).
>
> **Relevance to This Analysis:** The internal/external distinction that Heider established is the foundation on which Weiner's three-dimensional model builds.

> [!person] **Lyn Abramson, Martin Seligman & John Teasdale (reformulation, 1978)**

### C. Conceptual Tensions & Open Questions

> [!tension] **Attribution as Cause vs. Attribution as Symptom**
> **Position A (Weiner's model):** Causal attributions are the proximate cognitive cause of differential emotional and motivational outcomes. Change the attribution, change the motivation.
>
> **Position B (Mood-congruence view):** Prior emotional states and motivational dispositions are the primary determinants of causal attributions — people in negative affective states make more external, stable attributions; failure-expecting students make stable attributions for failure not because of logical inference but as an expression of a broader negative self-concept.
>
> **Current Evidence State:** Bidirectional. Experimental studies can demonstrate both directions of influence. The most parsimonious account is a reciprocal causal model in which attributions and emotions mutually constitute each other over time — but this account is more complex and less therapeutically tractable than the unidirectional cognitive-primacy model.
>
> **Why This Matters:** If attributions are partly symptoms rather than causes, cognitive attribution retraining may have limited efficacy without addressing the underlying emotional and self-concept substrate. This is consistent with the clinical literature showing that attribution interventions have larger effects in early-stage helplessness than in established depressive episodes.
>
> **This Report's Stance:** The two-stage intervention architecture proposed in Phase VI partially dissolves this tension by treating the attribution-emotion relationship as bidirectional — the intervention targets both the cognitive attribution AND the emotional reorganization simultaneously.

> [!open-question] **The Cross-Cultural Stability of the Emotion-Attribution Correspondences**
> The evidence reviewed in this analysis is predominantly from Western, predominantly individualistic cultural contexts. The question of whether the specific emotion-attribution mappings (particularly the locus-pride/shame correspondence) hold cross-culturally remains genuinely open. Some cross-cultural evidence suggests that the stability dimension may be more cross-culturally robust than the locus dimension, but systematic comparative data on the full emotion-mediation chain across cultural contexts is limited. This matters for the generalizability of attribution retraining interventions in non-Western educational contexts.

### G. Practical Application Protocol

> [!protocol] **Two-Stage Attribution Retraining Protocol (Derived from Phase VI Synthesis)**
> **Context:** Use with individuals or groups showing established helpless response patterns — persistent attributions for failure to stable, uncontrollable causes (low ability, fixed personality traits), with associated expectancy deficits and reduced persistence.
>
> **Stage 1 — Cognitive Reattribution:**
> 1. Introduce the stability dimension explicitly — explain the distinction between stable and unstable causes, using examples from the person's own domain
> 2. Map the person's current attribution profile onto the dimensional model — make explicit which dimension is problematic
> 3. Present alternative unstable attributions for recent failure experiences (insufficient specific strategy, insufficient targeted effort, task unfamiliarity)
> 4. Review evidence that supports the unstable interpretation — prior successes in similar tasks, the role of practice and strategy in performance improvement
>
> **Stage 2 — Affective Reorganization:**
> 1. Identify the emotional consequence of the current stable attribution (hopelessness, shame, resignation)
> 2. Generate a prediction: if the unstable attribution is accurate, what emotion should follow? (Hope, guilt as motivation, interest in strategy)
> 3. Design and execute a behavioral experiment — a challenging task with high probability of success under sufficient effort and appropriate strategy
> 4. Process the emotional experience during and after the task — explicitly connect the affective experience to the attribution reframe
> 5. Repeat with incrementally increasing challenge
>
> **Success Criteria:** The intervention is working when the person (a) spontaneously generates unstable attributions for failures without prompting, (b) reports emotional responses consistent with guilt rather than shame after controllable failures, and (c) shows increased persistence under failure conditions.
>
> **Common Pitfalls:** (1) Stopping at Stage 1 — cognitive change without affective reorganization is insufficient; (2) using success experiences that are too easy and don't require genuine effort (undermines the attribution mechanism by providing unearned disconfirmation); (3) failing to connect the behavioral experiment explicitly to the attribution reframe, so the success experience is emotionally processed as "I got lucky" rather than "I succeeded because of unstable factors I can deploy again."
