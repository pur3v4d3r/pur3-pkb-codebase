## What Is Knowledge?

The classical definition of knowledge is *justified true belief* (JTB): a person knows that P if and only if (1) P is true, (2) the person believes P, and (3) the person is justified in believing P. [[justified-true-belief|Justified true belief]] served as the working definition from Plato's *Meno* through the mid-twentieth century.

[[gettier-problem|The Gettier problem]] (1963) devastated the classical analysis in three pages. Gettier's cases demonstrate that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge: one can have a justified true belief that is *accidentally* true in a way that intuitively fails to constitute genuine knowledge. The canonical example: you justifiably believe your watch reads the correct time (it always has), but unbeknownst to you the watch stopped 12 hours ago — you just happen to check it at the right moment. Your belief is justified and true but is not knowledge.

The Gettier problem generated an enormous industry of proposed solutions. The most influential responses add a fourth condition: a *safety* condition (your belief could not easily have been false), a *sensitivity* condition (had P been false, you would not have believed P), or a *no-false-lemma* condition. None has achieved consensus, and some philosophers have concluded that the JTB framework itself is flawed and should be replaced.

[[propositional-knowledge|Propositional knowledge]] (knowing *that* something is the case) is the target of most epistemological analysis. [[knowledge-by-acquaintance-vs-description|Knowledge by acquaintance vs description]] (Russell's distinction) separates direct experiential knowledge from mediated descriptive knowledge — a distinction that matters for understanding perception, testimony, and the structure of the mind.

## Theories of Justification

How does a belief come to be *justified* — appropriately or rationally held? This is the central question of epistemology and generates the major competing positions.

### Foundationalism

[[foundationalism|Foundationalism]] holds that justified beliefs form a hierarchical structure: some beliefs are *basic* — immediately justified without depending on other beliefs — and all non-basic beliefs derive their justification (directly or indirectly) from these foundations. Classic candidates for basic beliefs include incorrigible reports of sensory experience ("I seem to see something red") and self-evident a priori truths.

The strengths of foundationalism: it offers a clear solution to the [[epistemic-regress|epistemic regress problem]] — the apparent infinite regress of justificatory demands — by positing beliefs that require no further justification. Its vulnerabilities: which beliefs genuinely qualify as basic, and can they bear the justificatory weight of the entire edifice?

### Coherentism

[[coherentism|Coherentism]] rejects the foundationalist hierarchy. Justification is determined not by privileged foundations but by *coherence relations* among a system of beliefs: a belief is justified to the extent that it coheres with — fits into, explains, and is explained by — the web of one's other beliefs. No belief is inherently basic; all can be revised if coherence demands it.

[[standard-of-coherence|The coherence standard]] requires mutual support, explanatory integration, and absence of contradiction. Coherentism's strength is its flexibility and holism; its central vulnerability is the isolation objection — two entirely isolated coherent belief systems could both qualify as justified, even if they contradict each other.

### Reliabilism

[[reliabilism|Reliabilism]] shifts the focus from the *reasons* a believer has to the *process* that produced the belief: a belief is justified if (and only if) it was produced by a reliable cognitive process — one that tends to produce true beliefs. This naturally externalises justification: what matters is the reliability of the mechanism, not the believer's access to that mechanism.

[[virtue-epistemology|Virtue epistemology]] is a related approach that grounds justification in the *intellectual virtues* of the epistemic agent — reliable truth-conducive dispositions like perceptual attentiveness, open-mindedness, and intellectual conscientiousness. It hybridises reliabilism (focus on reliable processes) with virtue theory (focus on character).

> [!tension] The Core Three-Way Tension
> Foundationalism offers structural clarity but generates contentious foundations. Coherentism offers flexibility but faces the isolation problem. Reliabilism offers naturalistic grounding but sacrifices the first-person accessibility that many find intuitive to justification. No position has resolved the tensions; practitioners in epistemology typically have considered views on which trade-offs to accept.

## Internalism vs Externalism

The foundationalism/coherentism/reliabilism debate connects to a deeper dispute between *internalism* and *externalism* about justification.

[[epistemic-internalism|Epistemic internalism]] holds that justification is constituted by factors *internal to* — accessible from — the believer's first-person perspective. If you cannot access the fact that P is justified in a normal reflective state, then that fact cannot constitute your justification. Deontological theories of justification (justification as fulfilling one's epistemic duties) tend toward internalism.

[[epistemic-externalism|Epistemic externalism]] holds that justification can be constituted by factors external to the believer's access — including the reliability of cognitive processes, the truth-producing properties of perceptual mechanisms, or the reliability of testimony chains. Reliabilism and virtue epistemology tend toward externalism.

[[internalism-vs-externalism-epistemology|The internalism/externalism debate]] has practical stakes: if externalism is correct, ordinary unreflective believers who form beliefs through reliable processes are epistemically justified even if they cannot articulate why. If internalism is correct, epistemic justification requires cognitive work — reflection, evidence evaluation, argument analysis.

## Truth Theories

The nature of truth is a foundational question in both epistemology and metaphysics. The major competing analyses:

| Theory | Core claim | Key proponent |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| [[correspondence-theory-of-truth\|Correspondence]] | A proposition is true iff it corresponds to reality | Aristotle, Russell |
| [[coherence-theory-of-truth\|Coherence]] | A proposition is true iff it coheres with a system of truths | Bradley, Blanshard |
| [[pragmatic-theory-of-truth\|Pragmatic]] | A proposition is true iff it is useful to believe (or survives inquiry) | Peirce, James |
| [[deflationary-theory-of-truth\|Deflationary]] | "True" is a logical device; there is no substantive property of truth | Ramsey, Horwich |
| [[epistemic-theory-of-truth\|Epistemic]] | Truth is what would be believed under ideal epistemic conditions | Putnam |

[[correspondence-theory-of-truth|Correspondence theory]] is the intuitive default for scientific realism but faces the challenge of specifying what "correspondence" means — how does a proposition relate to a fact? [[deflationary-theory-of-truth|Deflationary theories]] dissolve this challenge by denying that "true" names a substantive property at all: to say "it is true that snow is white" is merely to say that snow is white.

## Epistemic Virtues and Threats

Epistemology is concerned not only with the structure of knowledge but with the character of good epistemic agents. [[virtue-epistemology|Virtue epistemology]] analyses these character traits:

**Core virtues**: [[epistemic-humility|epistemic humility]] (accurate assessment of one's limitations), [[epistemic-vigilance|epistemic vigilance]] (appropriate caution about testimony and perception), [[epistemic-trust|epistemic trust]] (appropriate reliance on others' testimony), [[epistemic-autonomy|epistemic autonomy]] (thinking for oneself rather than deferring to authority).

**Core threats**:
- [[epistemic-akrasia|Epistemic akrasia]]: believing P while believing one has better evidence for not-P — a failure of rational self-governance
- [[epistemic-cowardice|Epistemic cowardice]]: deliberately giving vague answers to avoid the discomfort of truth-telling
- [[epistemic-closure|Epistemic closure]]: refusing to consider evidence that might revise one's beliefs
- [[epistemic-luck|Epistemic luck]]: having true justified belief through fortunate coincidence rather than reliable process (the Gettier-family problem applied to character)

[[reflective-equilibrium|Reflective equilibrium]] (Rawls, extended to epistemology) is the method of iteratively revising both general epistemic principles and particular judgments until they achieve maximal coherence — not privileging either the abstract principles or the concrete intuitions, but seeking a stable integration.

## Philosophy of Science

[[philosophy-of-science|Philosophy of science]] is applied epistemology for the scientific enterprise — it analyses what makes scientific knowledge distinctive, how theories relate to evidence, and what makes science authoritative.

[[falsifiability|Falsifiability]] (Popper) is the proposed demarcation criterion between science and non-science: a claim is scientific iff there exists a possible observation that could, if made, falsify it. [[demarcation-problem|The demarcation problem]] remains philosophically contested — falsifiability is necessary but not sufficient for science, and its strict application would exclude much legitimate science.

[[scientific-realism|Scientific realism]] is the metaphysical position that successful science describes mind-independent reality and that theoretical entities (electrons, quarks, gene sequences) genuinely exist. [[underdetermination|Underdetermination]] is the central argument against realism: multiple incompatible theories are always consistent with any finite set of observations, so evidence alone cannot determine which theory is true.

[[theory-ladenness-of-observation|Theory-ladenness of observation]] (Hanson) is the epistemological claim that what scientists *see* when they observe is shaped by the theoretical expectations they bring to observation — there is no theory-neutral observational foundation. This complicates falsificationism: anomalous observations may be explained away rather than falsifying theories.

[[paradigm-shift|Paradigm shifts]] (Kuhn) are the model of scientific change that replaces naive cumulative progress with revolutionary restructuring — normal science operates within a paradigm that determines what counts as a problem and what counts as a solution; anomalies accumulate until crisis triggers a non-rational transition to a new paradigm.

## Social Epistemology

Knowledge is not only a solitary achievement — most of what we know, we know through testimony, social institutions, and collective inquiry. [[social-epistemology|Social epistemology]] analyses these epistemic dimensions.

[[testimony|Testimony]] is the primary vector of knowledge transmission. We know that Napoleon lost at Waterloo, that the Earth is ~4.5 billion years old, and that penicillin inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis entirely through testimony — not personal observation. The epistemological question is when and why testimony transmission is knowledge-generating.

[[collective-epistemology|Collective epistemology]] extends epistemic analysis to groups and institutions — when does a group count as knowing something? What conditions make institutions epistemically reliable?

[[testimonial-injustice|Testimonial injustice]] (Fricker) is the ethical-epistemic harm done when a speaker is assigned reduced credibility because of identity prejudice — credibility deficit tracks race, gender, class, and other social categories in ways that damage both the individual and collective knowledge. It is simultaneously an injustice and an epistemic dysfunction.

[[epistemic-injustice|Epistemic injustice]] as a broader concept encompasses both testimonial injustice and *hermeneutical injustice* — the harm of having one's experience be unintelligible due to gaps in collective interpretive resources.

## 🌅 Frontier & Open Questions

> [!frontier] Live debates
> - **Gettier patch**: No consensus fourth condition for knowledge has emerged; some philosophers argue the JTB analysis should be abandoned entirely in favour of virtue-theoretic or reliabilist replacements.
> - **Extended mind**: Does knowledge reside only in the head, or can notebooks, computers, and other external systems constitute part of the cognitive system that holds knowledge? (see [[extended-mind-thesis|extended mind thesis]])
> - **Epistemic injustice remedies**: Theoretical development has outpaced practical remediation — how institutions should be redesigned to correct credibility deficits is underspecified.

## Synthesis Packet

**Top 5 Takeaways:**

1. **JTB is necessary but not sufficient.** The Gettier (1963) problem is the single most productive three pages in 20th-century epistemology — it destabilised a 2,000-year-old definition and generated an entire research programme.
2. **The justification triangle is a genuine trilemma.** Foundationalism, coherentism, and reliabilism each solve one problem while introducing another. Virtue epistemology occupies the hybrid zone; no position has achieved consensus.
3. **The access axis is the deepest structural divide.** Internalism vs externalism is not merely a technical dispute — it determines whether ordinary unreflective cognisers count as epistemically justified and whether epistemic work is morally/intellectually obligatory.
4. **Science is epistemology applied.** The Popper → Hanson → Kuhn sequence shows how each philosopher complicates the previous account of scientific knowledge, culminating in the realism/underdetermination standoff that remains open.
5. **Social epistemology adds justice to epistemics.** Fricker's testimonial injustice is simultaneously an epistemic failure (credibility miscalibration) and a moral wrong — epistemology cannot be separated from social power structures.
