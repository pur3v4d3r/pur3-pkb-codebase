## Self-Determination Theory: The Integrative Framework

[[self-determination-theory|Self-Determination Theory (SDT)]], developed by Deci and Ryan over four decades, is the most comprehensive and empirically supported theory of human motivation. Its central thesis is that humans are [[the-active-organism-hypothesis|active organisms]] — we are inherently growth-oriented, tendency-driven toward integration, mastery, and connection — but this organismic tendency requires supportive environmental conditions to flourish.

SDT's theoretical architecture comprises six interrelated mini-theories, each addressing a different aspect of motivation. The integrative framework is the [[organismic-metatheory|organismic metatheory]] — the metatheoretical commitment to treating the person as an active agent who interacts with and is shaped by social contexts, not merely as a reactive responder to reinforcement contingencies.

### The Six Mini-Theories

```
SDT Architecture
══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
│  CET  │  OIT  │  BPNT │  COT  │  GCT  │  RMT  │
│ Cogn. │ Organ.│ Basic │Causal.│ Goal  │Relat. │
│ Eval. │ Integ.│ Psych.│ Orient│Conts. │ Motiv.│
│ Th.   │ Th.   │ Needs │  Th.  │  Th.  │  Th.  │
══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
All six share the organismic metatheory and three basic needs
```

1. **[[cognitive-evaluation-theory|Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)]]** explains the effect of external events (rewards, feedback, constraints) on intrinsic motivation. CET's key claim: external events have a functional significance — they can be perceived as informational (supporting autonomy and competence) or controlling (undermining them). [[the-replication-challenge-to-the-undermining-effect|The replication challenge to the undermining effect]] notes that the initial overjustification findings have proven less universal than originally theorised, with reward type and context moderating outcomes substantially.

2. **[[organismic-integration-theory|Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)]]** describes how external regulation becomes internalised — the process by which extrinsic motivation is gradually transformed into self-governed behaviour (see Internalization section below).

3. **[[basic-psychological-needs-theory|Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT)]]** specifies the three universal needs — autonomy, competence, relatedness — whose satisfaction is necessary for psychological growth and well-being.

4. **[[causality-orientations-theory|Causality Orientations Theory (COT)]]** describes individual differences in orientations toward autonomy, control, and impersonal causality as stable personality-like constructs.

5. **[[goal-contents-theory|Goal Contents Theory (GCT)]]** distinguishes [[intrinsic-vs-extrinsic-goal-contents|intrinsic vs extrinsic goal contents]] — goals oriented toward growth, relationships, and community (intrinsic) vs. wealth, fame, and image (extrinsic) — and shows that intrinsic goal pursuit is more conducive to well-being and sustained motivation.

6. **[[relationship-motivation-theory|Relationship Motivation Theory (RMT)]]** extends SDT to close relationships, arguing that need satisfaction in relational contexts is a distinct determinant of relationship quality.

## Basic Psychological Needs: The Fuel

[[the-three-basic-psychological-needs|The three basic psychological needs]] — autonomy, competence, and relatedness — are the motivational substrate of SDT. Need satisfaction produces psychological growth and vitality; need frustration produces defensiveness, alienation, and diminished functioning.

### Autonomy

[[autonomy-need|Autonomy]] in SDT is not independence or freedom from social influence — it is the experience of *volition*, of acting from one's own values and choices rather than under external pressure or coercion. [[autonomy-sdt|Autonomy as a need]] is universal: even in highly collectivist cultures, where interdependence is the norm, the experience of choosing interdependent actions from a position of genuine endorsement satisfies autonomy, whereas being coerced into them does not.

[[autonomy-support|Autonomy support]] — the teaching or parenting style that acknowledges the learner's perspective, provides meaningful rationale for requests, and minimises pressure — is the single most consistently positive environmental predictor of intrinsic motivation, learning quality, and well-being in educational research.

> [!tension] Autonomy vs Structure
> [[autonomy-structure-dialectic|The autonomy-structure dialectic]] is a conceptual confusion that generates practical mistakes: many practitioners assume that structure (rules, schedules, expectations) is inherently autonomy-undermining. SDT evidence rejects this. [[autonomy-supportive-structure|Autonomy-supportive structure]] — clear expectations delivered with rationale, acknowledgment of the learner's perspective, and invitations to problem-solve — is both structuring *and* autonomy-supportive simultaneously. The opposition is autonomy support vs control, not autonomy vs structure.

### Competence

[[competence-need|Competence]] is the need to feel effective in interactions with the environment — to master challenges and achieve desired outcomes. It is satisfied not by success on trivially easy tasks but by *optimal challenge* — tasks at the frontier of current ability that stretch but don't overwhelm.

[[feedback-design-for-autonomy-and-mastery|Feedback design for autonomy and mastery]] is the practical implication: feedback must be informational rather than controlling, competence-affirming rather than ego-threatening, and calibrated to the learner's current level. Positive feedback that is perceived as evaluative surveillance undermines intrinsic motivation; positive feedback experienced as genuine information about capability supports it.

### Relatedness

[[relatedness-need|Relatedness]] is the need to feel connected to others — cared for and caring, belonging to a community. Its satisfaction is not merely about pleasurable social contact; it is about authentic engagement in which the person feels recognised and valued as an individual rather than treated as an interchangeable social object.

In educational contexts, relatedness to teachers and peers predicts engagement, intrinsic motivation, and persistence, even when autonomy and competence needs are not fully met. [[need-frustration|Need frustration]] — the active thwarting of basic needs — is more harmful than mere need absence; controlling, cold, or dismissive environments do not merely fail to support motivation, they actively undermine it.

### Need Satisfaction, Frustration, and Thwarting

[[need-satisfaction|Need satisfaction]] and [[need-thwarting|need thwarting]] are not merely poles of a single continuum — they are distinct processes with distinct consequences. Satisfaction promotes growth and integration; frustration triggers defensive motivation (e.g., introjected regulation, ego-involvement, controlled motivation). [[need-frustration-as-an-active-pathogenic-process|Need frustration as an active pathogenic process]] underlies the psychopathology of motivation: chronic need thwarting predicts depression, anxiety, rigid goal pursuit, and alienation.

## Internalization: From Compliance to Commitment

[[organismic-integration-theory|Organismic Integration Theory]] maps the process by which externally regulated behaviours are progressively transformed into self-governed ones. The [[internalization-continuum|internalization continuum]] spans from pure external regulation to full intrinsic motivation:

```
INTERNALIZATION CONTINUUM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EXTRINSIC ←─────────────────────────────────────────► INTRINSIC
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
External    Introjected   Identified   Integrated    Intrinsic
Regulation  Regulation    Regulation   Regulation    Motivation
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
"To get     "To avoid     "This is     "This is      "This is 
 reward"     shame"        important    part of       inherently
                           to me"       who I am"     enjoyable"
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

[[external-regulation|External regulation]] is controlled by rewards and punishments — purely instrumental behaviour. [[introjected-regulation|Introjected regulation]] has been taken in but not genuinely owned — one does the behaviour to avoid guilt or maintain self-esteem (ego-involvement). [[identified-regulation|Identified regulation]] involves genuinely valuing the activity's goal even if the activity itself is not inherently enjoyable. [[integrated-regulation|Integrated regulation]] involves the activity being fully aligned with one's values and identity. [[intrinsic-motivation|Intrinsic motivation]] requires neither external incentive nor value endorsement — the activity is inherently satisfying.

[[the-internalization-phenomenon|The internalization phenomenon]] is practically powerful: behaviours that begin as externally regulated can become genuinely valued through appropriate conditions (autonomy-supportive rationale, acknowledgment of difficulty, minimal surveillance). Conversely, intrinsically motivated behaviours can have their intrinsic motivation eroded by controlling external rewards — the overjustification effect.

[[autonomous-motivation|Autonomous motivation]] (identified + integrated + intrinsic) predicts higher quality learning, greater persistence, better well-being, and more creative performance than [[controlled-motivation|controlled motivation]] (external + introjected). [[autonomous-vs-controlled-commitment-integrating-sdt|The autonomous/controlled distinction]] is the most practically significant axis in SDT.

## Goal Theories: Direction and Achievement

### Achievement Goal Theory

[[achievement-goal-theory|Achievement Goal Theory]] (Dweck, Ames, Elliot) addresses the *direction* of motivation — specifically, what standard learners use to define success. [[mastery-vs-performance-goals|The fundamental distinction]] is between:

- **[[mastery-goal-orientation|Mastery goals]]** (also task or learning goals): the aim is to develop competence, understand the material, and improve relative to one's past performance.
- **[[performance-goal-orientation|Performance goals]]**: the aim is to demonstrate competence relative to others, or to avoid demonstrating incompetence.

[[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework|The 2×2 achievement goal framework]] (Elliot & McGregor, 2001) crosses approach/avoidance with mastery/performance:

| | Approach | Avoidance |
|--|----------|-----------|
| **Mastery** | Mastery-approach: improve and learn | Mastery-avoidance: avoid failing to improve |
| **Performance** | Performance-approach: outperform others | Performance-avoidance: avoid looking incompetent |

Mastery-approach goals predict deep processing, help-seeking, task persistence, and intrinsic motivation. Performance-avoidance goals predict anxiety, self-handicapping, and reduced learning. Performance-approach goals show more complex effects — they can boost performance on competitive tasks but undermine learning, collaboration, and intrinsic motivation.

### Goal Setting Theory

[[goal-setting-theory|Goal Setting Theory]] (Locke & Latham) establishes that specific, challenging goals consistently produce higher performance than vague or easy goals — the classic finding that "do your best" instructions produce less effort than "achieve 90%" goals. The four mechanisms are: directing attention to goal-relevant activities, energising effort, sustaining persistence, and motivating strategy search.

[[goal-specificity|Goal specificity]] and [[goal-difficulty|goal difficulty]] are the primary moderators of goal effectiveness, interacting with [[goal-commitment|goal commitment]]: highly specific, challenging goals only produce superior performance when the person is genuinely committed to achieving them. Without commitment — common when goals are externally imposed without rationale — they can produce anxiety and withdrawal.

## Self-Efficacy: The Confidence Engine

[[self-efficacy|Self-efficacy]] is Bandura's concept of one's belief in one's capacity to organise and execute the courses of action required to produce given outcomes. Crucially, it is task-specific and domain-specific — global self-confidence is not self-efficacy.

### The Four Sources

[[self-efficacy-theory|Self-efficacy theory]] identifies four sources in decreasing order of power:

1. **[[mastery-experience|Mastery experience]]** (enactive mastery): actually succeeding at the task is the most powerful efficacy builder. [[enactive-mastery-experience|Enactive mastery experience]] produces durable, generalisation-supporting efficacy; failure undermines it more when efficacy is already fragile.

2. **[[vicarious-experience|Vicarious experience]]**: observing similar others succeed raises efficacy through social comparison; watching them fail lowers it. Coping models (who struggle but succeed) are often more informative than mastery models.

3. **[[verbal-persuasion|Verbal persuasion]]**: being told you have the capability by a credible source temporarily boosts efficacy, but cannot sustain it without subsequent performance confirmation.

4. **[[physiological-and-affective-states|Physiological and affective states]]**: how one interprets arousal and emotion affects efficacy judgments. Reinterpreting anxiety as energising excitement rather than debilitating fear — [[cognitive-reappraisal|cognitive reappraisal]] — can maintain efficacy under pressure.

> [!key-claim] Efficacy Is Not Accuracy
> High self-efficacy does not require calibration with actual ability. **Moderate over-confidence** is more conducive to performance than perfect accuracy — it sustains effort past setbacks and motivates more ambitious goal-setting. The practical problem is *severe* miscalibration in either direction.

## Attribution Theory: Explaining Outcomes

[[attribution-theory|Attribution theory]] addresses how people explain their own and others' outcomes. [[weiner-s-attribution-theory]] (here tracked through [[causal-attribution-in-motivation|causal attribution in motivation]]) is the most developed account in achievement contexts.

### Weiner's Dimensional Model

Causal attributions vary along three dimensions:

| Dimension | Pole A | Pole B | Effect of A |
|-----------|--------|--------|-------------|
| [[locus-of-causality|Locus]] | Internal | External | Pride/shame amplified |
| [[stability-dimension|Stability]] | Stable | Unstable | Expectancy change |
| [[controllability-dimension|Controllability]] | Controllable | Uncontrollable | Guilt vs anger |

[[the-locus-of-causality-dimension|Locus]] determines the *emotional* intensity of success and failure (internal attributions generate more pride and shame). [[stability-dimension|Stability]] determines *expectancy change* — attributing failure to stable causes (low ability) produces expectancy decline; attributing to unstable causes (effort, strategy, luck) maintains hope. [[the-controllability-dimension|Controllability]] determines whether the emotion is guilt (failed to try hard enough) or helplessness (truly unable).

The practical intervention target is stability + controllability. [[attribution-retraining|Attribution retraining]] — systematically restructuring failure attributions from "I'm not smart enough" (internal-stable-uncontrollable) to "I didn't use the right strategy" (internal-unstable-controllable) — demonstrably improves persistence and performance. [[the-two-stage-architecture-of-attribution-retraining|The two-stage architecture]] involves first helping students articulate their current attributional pattern, then introducing alternative causal framings through evidence and guided practice.

## Academic Emotions: The Affective Dimension

Motivation and emotion are not separable in learning contexts. [[control-value-theory|Control-Value Theory]] (Pekrun) provides the most comprehensive account of achievement emotions in academic settings.

[[academic-emotions|Academic emotions]] are defined by two appraisal dimensions: **subjective control** (perceived ability to influence the activity or outcome) and **subjective value** (importance of the activity or outcome to the learner). The cross-classification generates distinct emotional profiles:

```
CONTROL-VALUE THEORY: Academic Emotion Map
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════
           │  HIGH VALUE      │   LOW VALUE      │
───────────┼──────────────────┼──────────────────┤
HIGH       │ ENJOYMENT,       │ RELAXATION       │
CONTROL    │ HOPE, PRIDE      │ RELIEF           │
───────────┼──────────────────┼──────────────────┤
LOW        │ ANXIETY,         │ BOREDOM          │
CONTROL    │ HOPELESSNESS,    │ INDIFFERENCE     │
           │ SHAME            │                  │
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════
```

[[boredom-and-learning|Boredom]] — the neglected achievement emotion — combines low control with low value. It is not motivationally neutral: chronic boredom produces disengagement, procrastination, and eventually identity-based disidentification from academic domains. [[test-anxiety|Test anxiety]] is the canonical high-value, low-control emotion: the student cares intensely about performance but perceives little control over outcomes.

[[academic-anxiety|Academic anxiety]] more broadly undermines performance through two mechanisms: it diverts working memory resources to rumination, and it triggers avoidance strategies (studying less, leaving exams early) that are negatively reinforcing.

## 🌅 Frontier & Open Questions

> [!frontier] Live debates
> - **The undermining effect replication crisis**: The overjustification effect (tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation) shows inconsistent results across laboratories. Reward type, context, and individual differences moderate effects in ways not fully theorised.
> - **Need universality**: Is the three-need structure truly universal across cultures, or does need salience vary systematically by cultural context?
> - **Implicit vs explicit motivation**: Measures of implicit motivation (TAT-based) and explicit self-report motivation frequently diverge. The conditions under which each predicts behaviour are not fully specified.

```
## Visual Aid 7: Weiner's Attribution Dimensional Model

Purpose: Maps all three causal attribution dimensions, their poles, and the
distinct emotional and expectancy consequences of each.

```
╔══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╗
║   WEINER'S ATTRIBUTION MODEL [[attribution-theory]] — 3D CONSEQUENCE   ║
║                              MAP                                        ║
╚══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╝

THREE CAUSAL DIMENSIONS AND THEIR CONSEQUENCES:
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

DIMENSION 1: LOCUS [[locus-of-causality]]
  Internal ◄────────────────────────────► External
  (ability, effort, strategy)             (task ease, luck, teacher)

  Effect on SUCCESS:
    Internal locus → Pride amplified, ownership of achievement
    External locus → Luck/gratitude, not pride-generating

  Effect on FAILURE:
    Internal locus → Shame amplified, identity implication
    External locus → Anger, reduced shame, less motivational damage

  ★ Locus governs EMOTIONAL INTENSITY of outcomes.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

DIMENSION 2: STABILITY [[stability-dimension]]
  Stable ◄──────────────────────────────► Unstable
  (fixed ability, trait difficulty)        (effort, strategy, mood, luck)

  Effect on SUCCESS:
    Stable attribution → High future expectancy maintained
    Unstable attribution → Luck/effort, future uncertain

  Effect on FAILURE:
    Stable attribution → Expectancy DECLINE, hopelessness
    Unstable attribution → Expectancy MAINTAINED, hope survives

  ★ Stability governs FUTURE EXPECTANCY CHANGE.
    This is the primary attribution-retraining target.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

DIMENSION 3: CONTROLLABILITY [[controllability-dimension]]
  Controllable ◄────────────────────────► Uncontrollable
  (effort, strategy selection)            (ability, luck, others' actions)

  Effect on FAILURE:
    Controllable attribution → Guilt ("I didn't try hard enough")
                              → Motivation to change behaviour
    Uncontrollable attribution → Helplessness ("I just can't do this")
                               → Withdrawal, depression

  ★ Controllability governs GUILT vs HELPLESSNESS after failure.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE MOST DAMAGING ATTRIBUTION PATTERN:
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  "I failed because I'm not smart enough"                        │
  │                                                                  │
  │  Locus: INTERNAL → deep shame                                   │
  │  Stability: STABLE → expectancy collapse, future failure assumed │
  │  Controllability: UNCONTROLLABLE → helplessness, withdrawal     │
  │                                                                  │
  │  → Learned helplessness pathway → Identity-based disidentif.   │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

THE ATTRIBUTION RETRAINING TARGET:
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  "I failed because I didn't use the right strategy"             │
  │                                                                  │
  │  Locus: INTERNAL → some accountability (not shame-free)         │
  │  Stability: UNSTABLE → expectancy maintained, change possible   │
  │  Controllability: CONTROLLABLE → guilt → motivation to change   │

```
## Synthesis Packet

### Top 5 Takeaways

1. SDT is the integrative architecture, not just another theory. Its six
   mini-theories together address the full motivational landscape: what
   external events do (CET), how regulation becomes internal (OIT), what
   the underlying needs are (BPNT), individual differences in orientation
   (COT), what goals support vs undermine needs (GCT), and relational
   contexts (RMT). No other framework covers this range with equal empirical
   depth.

2. Need satisfaction and need frustration are not poles of the same
   continuum. Frustration is an active pathogenic process with distinct
   consequences (depression, alienation, rigid goal pursuit) that mere
   need absence does not produce. Intervention design must address
   thwarting, not just add support.

3. The internalization axis (controlled → autonomous) is the single most
   practically significant dimension in motivation design. The jump from
   introjected to identified regulation marks the threshold where quality
   of learning, creativity, and well-being all improve. Most instructional
   designs inadvertently cultivate introjection.

4. Attribution retraining targets stability × controllability, not locus.
   The practical goal is not to make learners attribute failure externally,
   but to shift from stable-uncontrollable ("I'm not smart") to unstable-
   controllable ("I used the wrong strategy") — maintaining accountability
   while preserving hope and motivation to change.

5. Academic boredom is not motivationally neutral. Low control + low value
   activates a disengagement trajectory that, if chronic, produces identity-
   level disidentification from the domain — far more difficult to remediate
   than acute anxiety.
```
