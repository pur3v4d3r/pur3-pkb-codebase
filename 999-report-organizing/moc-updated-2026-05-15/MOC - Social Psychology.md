## Social Cognition

[[social-cognition|Social cognition]] is the study of how people process, store, and apply information about other people and social situations. It is the interface between cognitive psychology and social psychology — applying the concepts of attention, memory, schemas, and inference to the social domain.

[[theory-of-mind|Theory of mind]] (ToM) is the capacity to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions — to others and to oneself. ToM enables social coordination, deception, and empathy; its absence or impairment (as in autism spectrum conditions) produces characteristic social difficulties. ToM is both a cognitive achievement (developing through childhood) and a social resource (continuously deployed in adult social reasoning).

[[social-perception|Social perception]] — how we form impressions of others — operates quickly and automatically. Within milliseconds of seeing a face, we make attributions of trustworthiness, dominance, and competence. These automatic impressions, while quick, are systematically biased by [[halo-effect|halo effects]] (one positive trait colours overall evaluation), attractiveness stereotypes, and racial/gender stereotypes.

[[self-perception-theory|Self-perception theory]] (Bem) proposes that we sometimes infer our own attitudes and traits from observing our own behaviour — much as we would infer another person's dispositions from watching them act. This produces counterintuitive predictions: behaving as if you are confident can generate genuinely increased confidence, because you observe yourself acting confidently and infer the corresponding attitude.

## Attribution in Social Context

Attribution — how we explain behaviour and outcomes — operates differently in social contexts than in individual achievement contexts (see [[MOC - Motivation Psychology]] for the achievement-focused analysis).

### The Fundamental Attribution Error

[[the-fundamental-attribution-error|The fundamental attribution error]] (FAE; also *correspondence bias*) is the pervasive tendency to over-attribute others' behaviour to internal dispositions and under-attribute it to situational factors. When someone cuts in a queue, we assume they are rude rather than considering that they might be responding to a genuine emergency.

[[fundamental-attribution-error-correspondence-bias|The FAE/correspondence bias]] is asymmetric: when explaining our own behaviour, we show the opposite pattern — the actor-observer asymmetry. We attribute our own behaviour to situations ("I was rude because I was stressed") and others' to stable traits.

The FAE has profound practical implications: it underlies blaming victims for their circumstances, attributing poverty to laziness rather than structural factors, and penalising employees for failures that were situationally determined.

### Self-Serving Bias

[[self-serving-bias|Self-serving bias]] is the tendency to attribute successes to internal causes (ability, effort) and failures to external causes (bad luck, unfair treatment). It serves ego-protective functions and is modulated by self-esteem: people with higher self-esteem show stronger self-serving biases.

[[diffusion-of-responsibility|Diffusion of responsibility]] in group contexts is an attribution effect with life-or-death consequences: the [[bystander-effect|bystander effect]] is partly explained by the attribution that *someone else* will help — responsibility diffuses across the group. The murder of Kitty Genovese (however mythologised) catalysed the research programme establishing that larger audiences reduce individual helping behaviour.

## Social Influence

Human behaviour is extraordinarily sensitive to actual and perceived social norms, authority, and group pressure. The social influence research programme remains some of psychology's most cited and debated work.

### Conformity

[[conformity|Conformity]] — adjusting one's beliefs or behaviours to match group norms — operates through two distinct mechanisms: *informational influence* (the group is providing information about reality that I should update on) and *normative influence* (I need to fit in to avoid social sanction, regardless of my private belief). Asch's line experiments demonstrated conformity to manifestly incorrect group judgments — 75% of participants conformed at least once in the original studies.

[[pluralistic-ignorance|Pluralistic ignorance]] is the social dynamic in which most members of a group privately reject a group norm but publicly comply with it, each mistakenly assuming the others genuinely endorse it. It explains why unhealthy or unjust norms can persist even when privately opposed by most group members.

### Obedience

[[obedience-to-authority|Obedience to authority]] — Milgram's (1963) experiments — demonstrated that ordinary people would administer apparently lethal electric shocks to an innocent person under instruction from an authority figure. ~65% of participants went to the maximum voltage level. The finding, despite methodological critique, has been partially replicated and remains among social psychology's most significant demonstrations.

The mechanism is not sadism or malice — it is the *agentic shift*: participants transferred moral responsibility to the authority, experiencing themselves as executors of another's will rather than as autonomous moral agents.

### Social Proof and Persuasion

[[social-proof|Social proof]] is the heuristic of inferring correct behaviour from what others do — one of Cialdini's six principles of persuasion. It is rational under uncertainty (if I don't know whether to queue or not, the fact that others are queuing is evidence I should too) but can produce systematic errors when the underlying behaviour is irrational or when the reference group is unrepresentative.

## Group Dynamics

Groups systematically alter individual behaviour and cognition in ways that are not merely additive. The major group dynamics phenomena:

[[groupthink|Groupthink]] (Janis) is the deterioration of rational decision-making in cohesive groups through excessive pressure for consensus. The symptoms — illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalisation, belief in group morality, stereotyping of out-groups, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity — have been used to analyse political decision-making disasters (the Bay of Pigs invasion, the Challenger disaster). [[groupthink|Groupthink]] is contested as a formal theory (the causal structure is unclear) but remains a useful phenomenological description.

[[social-facilitation|Social facilitation]] is the finding that the presence of others *improves* performance on easy/well-learned tasks but *impairs* performance on difficult/novel tasks. Zajonc's drive theory explains this as arousal increasing dominant responses — for easy tasks, dominant responses are correct; for hard tasks, dominant responses are errors.

[[social-loafing|Social loafing]] is the reduction of individual effort in group tasks when individual contributions are not identifiable. It is the opposite of social facilitation: when lost in the crowd, people exert less effort. The implication for group learning: individual accountability is essential for cooperative learning to work.

[[deindividuation|Deindividuation]] is the loss of self-awareness and individual identity in group contexts, producing reduced self-regulation and increased susceptibility to group norms and situational cues. It underlies mob behaviour, online disinhibition, and some forms of organisational misconduct.

## Social Identity and Intergroup Relations

[[social-identity-theory|Social Identity Theory]] (Tajfel & Turner) is the dominant framework for understanding intergroup behaviour. The theory proposes that people derive part of their self-concept from their group memberships (*social identity*), and are motivated to maintain positive social identity through *social comparison* with relevant out-groups.

Social identity theory's core finding: even *minimal group* conditions — arbitrary group assignment with no history, interaction, or shared interest — generate in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination. The implication is profound: intergroup conflict does not require realistic competition over resources; the mere categorisation into groups is sufficient.

[[self-categorization-theory|Self-categorisation theory]] (Turner) extends social identity theory: people shift between personal identity and social identity as a function of context. When social identity is salient, behaviour is guided by group norms and inter-group comparison; when personal identity is salient, individual traits and interpersonal comparison guide behaviour.

[[stereotype-threat|Stereotype threat]] (Steele & Aronson) is the phenomenon in which awareness of a negative stereotype about one's social group impairs performance on tasks relevant to the stereotype — not because of actual ability differences but through cognitive interference (working memory drain from managing stereotype-related anxiety). The finding has robust replication in multiple domains (racial achievement gaps, gender gaps in mathematics), though effect sizes and moderators are actively debated.

[[in-group-out-group-bias|In-group/out-group bias]] operates pervasively in social cognition: we process information more favourably about in-group members, attribute their failures more charitably, and are more susceptible to their persuasion. This has direct implications for educational and organisational design — relatedness to group (teacher/peer community) modulates learning engagement.

## 🌅 Frontier & Open Questions

> [!frontier] Open questions
> - **Replication crisis in social psychology**: Many flagship findings (Milgram, Asch, IAT-behavior correlation) have failed to replicate fully. What is the actual effect size of social influence on behaviour in real-world contexts?
> - **Online social dynamics**: Social influence, conformity, and identity dynamics in digital/online contexts show both similarities and striking differences from face-to-face equivalents. The mechanisms are undertheorised.

## Synthesis Packet

**Top 5 takeaways:**

1. **Attribution errors are a system, not isolated quirks** — the FAE, actor-observer asymmetry, self-serving bias, and bystander effect share one mechanism (situation under-weighting) and their downstream harms are predictable from it (Aid 3).

2. **Social influence has three structurally distinct routes** — conformity, obedience, and social proof differ in where moral agency sits and how durable the influence is; interventions must target the right route (Aid 4).

3. **Group dynamics are governed by two axes** — arousal and identifiability together predict facilitation, loafing, groupthink, and deindividuation. Individual accountability is the primary design lever (Aid 5).

4. **SIT's minimal group finding is the bedrock** — even arbitrary categorisation generates in-group bias. All intergroup design (classrooms, teams, institutions) must reckon with this rather than assuming goodwill is sufficient (Aid 6).

5. **Replication robustness varies enormously** — some flagship findings (Milgram, Asch exact figures, stereotype threat effect sizes) have shrunk substantially; minimal group, bystander, and social facilitation effects are robust. Calibrate confidence accordingly (Aid 9).
