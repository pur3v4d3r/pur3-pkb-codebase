# Opportunity Cost

> [!definition] Opportunity Cost
> **Opportunity cost** is the value of the *next-best alternative foregone* by choosing a particular course of action. It is the *true cost* of a choice, in contrast to the *accounting cost* (cash, hours, materials directly expended), because it accounts for the value of what could have been done with the same resources but was not.
>
> **Defining property**: the cost is *invisible by construction* — it accrues to a path not taken, leaves no receipt, generates no entry in any ledger. Recognizing it requires the discipline of *imagining the alternative* and valuing it explicitly.
>
> **See also**: [[mental-model]], [[expected-value]], [[trade-off]], [[counterfactual-reasoning]]

## In-Depth Definition

The concept is foundational to economics — Friedrich von Wieser coined the German term *Opportunitätskosten* in 1914, and the underlying insight had been articulated more vividly half a century earlier by Frédéric Bastiat in *Ce qu'on voit et ce qu'on ne voit pas* (1850). Bastiat's parable of the broken window remains the cleanest demonstration: when a shopkeeper's window is broken and he pays the glazier six francs to repair it, a naive observer sees stimulated economic activity — the glazier gains six francs, which he spends elsewhere, which gains another tradesman, and so on. Bastiat's insight: *the shopkeeper would have spent those six francs anyway*, on (say) shoes or a book. The broken window did not create economic activity; it *redirected* it, and at the cost of a now-functional window. The tradesman who *would have* received the six francs is invisible — he is *that which is not seen*.

The discipline generalizes far past economics. Every choice incurs opportunity cost: a Saturday spent at one event is a Saturday not spent at another; capital allocated to one project is capital not allocated to a different one; attention spent on one problem is attention not spent on another. The model is one of the most-transferable in the lattice precisely because *every act of choice* — across domains, across time scales, across stakes — instantiates it.

The cognitive difficulty is not conceptual but *attentional*. The chosen option is in the field of attention by virtue of being chosen; the foregone alternative is, by construction, not. Empirical research on decision-making (Kahneman 2011 on attention substitution; Frederick et al. 2009 on opportunity-cost neglect) consistently finds that even economically literate respondents fail to elicit opportunity costs spontaneously when asked to evaluate single options. The fix is structural — *force* the alternative into attention by reframing the decision as a comparison ("X vs. the next-best use of the same resources"), and by writing down the alternative *before* committing.

The model has a corollary that is at least as important: *low-opportunity-cost moments are valuable*. A free Tuesday afternoon when nothing else competes for the time has lower opportunity cost than a Saturday evening; a slack period in a career has lower opportunity cost than a high-leverage moment; capital sitting in cash has lower opportunity cost than capital deployed in a moderate-return investment. Recognizing the *variance* of opportunity cost across moments allows for strategic timing — concentrating high-cost activities in low-cost moments, and reserving high-cost moments for high-value uses.

> [!boundary] Scope of Valid Application
> **Applies when**: (a) resources are scarce — time, money, attention, opportunity slots all qualify; (b) genuine alternative uses exist (not merely hypothetical ones); (c) the alternative is *valuable* (otherwise the opportunity cost is trivially zero).
>
> **Does NOT apply when**: (a) resources are genuinely non-rival — using sunlight to read does not preclude others using sunlight to read, and the opportunity cost of one's own sunlight-time may be zero if no alternative use exists; (b) the choice is *forced* — being conscripted into military service does not have meaningful opportunity cost from the conscript's perspective because the alternative branch was not actually available to them; (c) at *temporal scales* where alternatives are not consciously discriminated (the opportunity cost of one specific second of one's life is not a useful concept).
>
> **Far-transfer caveats**: the model is genuinely universal in scope but *operationally hardest* in domains where the alternatives are *open-ended* (what is the opportunity cost of one hour of leisure?). Crisp opportunity-cost analysis requires a reasonably bounded comparison set; with no bounded set, the analysis degrades into vague self-criticism rather than usable decision input.

## Mechanism / How It Works

```
Naive cost-thinking:        chosen option's accounting cost
                            (e.g., $X spent, T hours used)

Opportunity-cost-thinking:  chosen option's accounting cost
                            +
                            value of best foregone alternative
                            (what would have been done with the same
                             $X, T hours, attention, slot)

Decision quality test:      is the chosen option's value > the next-best
                            alternative's value?
                            If yes: rational choice
                            If no:  re-evaluate; current path is dominated
```

The mechanism is decompositional. The practitioner identifies (a) the resource being committed (time, capital, attention), (b) the chosen option's full value, (c) the explicit set of alternative uses that resource could be put to, (d) the value of the *best* alternative use, and (e) compares (b) and (d). The choice is rational only if (b) > (d). The model's power comes not from precise valuation — alternatives are usually only roughly valuable — but from the *structural insistence* that the comparison happen at all.

## Related Mental Models (Latticework Position)

> [!key-claim] Comparative-Valuation Family
> Opportunity cost is the *cost-side* member of a comparative-valuation family that includes [[expected-value]] (the *probabilistic* refinement), [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] (the *anti-pattern* it explicitly negates), and [[trade-off]] (the *structural* statement that gives rise to it). To master opportunity cost is to acquire most of practical microeconomics in compact form.

> [!warning] When NOT to Reach for This Model
> 1. **Genuinely non-scarce resources**: when no alternative use of the resource exists, opportunity cost is zero and analysis is wasted.
> 2. **Forced choices**: when the alternative was not actually available, calling the foregone path's value an "opportunity cost" is technically correct but practically pointless — no decision was made.
> 3. **Decision paralysis through over-comparison**: practitioners who rigorously compute opportunity cost on every micro-choice end up unable to act. The model's depth of analysis must be bounded by the choice's stakes.

## Real-World Examples

> [!example] Canonical Example (economics)
> **Bastiat's broken window (1850)**. The window costs six francs to repair. The glazier earns six francs. Naive observer: "the breakage stimulated the economy by six francs." Bastiat: the shopkeeper would have spent the six francs on shoes; the shoemaker is the *invisible* loser. Net: economy is *down by one window* and the labor sequence is rearranged but not augmented. The case is canonical because (a) it isolates the model in its purest form, (b) it identifies the cognitive failure (the foregone alternative is invisible), and (c) it remains pedagogically useful 175 years later precisely because the invisibility of the alternative remains a robust cognitive bias.

> [!example] Far-Transfer Example (career time-allocation)
> A programmer offered $200,000 to leave their startup for a corporate role faces an opportunity cost: not the $200,000 they would gain, but the equity upside of the startup foregone. If the startup has a plausible 10% chance of a 50× exit, the expected opportunity cost of leaving is $200,000 × 50 × 0.10 = $1M against a present-value certain $200,000. The transferability is real: the same comparative-valuation logic applies to choosing between job offers, choosing between research projects, choosing between graduate programs, choosing between relationships. In each case the *visible* metric (salary, prestige, immediate satisfaction) is only the accounting cost; the *invisible* foregone alternative completes the picture.

> [!example] Personal Application
> When I committed to the latticework project I did not crisply name the foregone alternative — which was *finishing the SPES component library*. The decision felt costless because both were "PKB work," but the alternative was not doing nothing; it was completing a different high-value project that had been ~70% built. Eliciting the foregone option crisply (3 months on lattice = 3 months not on SPES + the option-decay of leaving SPES half-built) did not reverse the decision but *did* change how I bounded it: I added an explicit return-by-date for SPES (Phase 8, ~Session 12) rather than letting it drift indefinitely as undated future work. The decision's quality improved not by being changed but by being *correctly framed as a comparison*.

## Research & Empirical Foundation

The empirical literature is concentrated in **(1) Behavioral economics on opportunity-cost neglect** — Frederick, Novemsky, Wang, Dhar, & Nowlis (2009) in *Journal of Consumer Research* showed that even MBA-level decision-makers fail to spontaneously elicit opportunity costs when evaluating single purchase decisions, and that explicit prompting to consider the alternative substantially shifts choices toward more reflective evaluation. **(2) Decision-research on framing effects** (Tversky & Kahneman 1981; subsequent literature) demonstrates that single-option evaluations systematically diverge from comparative evaluations, with the latter typically incorporating opportunity-cost considerations the former omits. **(3) Capital-allocation literature** (cost-of-capital theory in corporate finance, Markowitz portfolio theory) makes opportunity cost the *foundational* concept: the discount rate applied to a project's future cash flows is, definitionally, the opportunity cost of the capital deployed.

> [!cite] Bastiat, F. (1850)
> *Ce qu'on voit et ce qu'on ne voit pas* ("That Which Is Seen and That Which Is Not Seen"). Paris. The founding essay; the broken-window parable is the canonical pedagogical example and remains in active citation across economics teaching.

> [!cite] Frederick, S., Novemsky, N., Wang, J., Dhar, R., & Nowlis, S. (2009)
> "Opportunity Cost Neglect." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 36(4), 553–561. Empirical demonstration that the opportunity-cost concept, even when known to respondents, is not spontaneously elicited; explicit framing changes choice behavior measurably.

> [!cite] Sowell, T. (2010)
> *Basic Economics: A Common Sense Guide to the Economy* (4th ed.). Basic Books. Sustained pedagogical articulation of opportunity-cost reasoning across micro and macro contexts; an extended case for the model's underuse in public policy debate.

## Pitfalls & Limitations

> [!warning] Failure Mode 1 — The Invisible-Alternative Problem
> The next-best foregone option, by construction, is not present in the field of attention. Practitioners must elicit it *deliberately*; passive reflection systematically misses it. This is the primary cognitive obstacle to the model's use, and the literature documents it across decades of behavioral research.

> [!warning] Failure Mode 2 — Confusing Opportunity Cost with Sunk Cost
> Opportunity cost is *forward-looking* — the foregone *future* alternative. Sunk cost is *backward-looking* — already-paid past expense. They are categorically distinct, but practitioners frequently conflate them, often by treating sunk costs as if they had ongoing opportunity-cost relevance ("I've already invested so much, I can't quit now"). The discipline is to evaluate only *current* opportunity costs against *current* alternatives, regardless of past investment.

> [!warning] Failure Mode 3 — Open-Endedness Paralysis
> Asking "what is the opportunity cost of this hour?" can degenerate into existential anxiety because the alternative-set is essentially unbounded. The fix is to bound the comparison set realistically — what would I *actually* be doing with this hour, given my real constraints — not what I might *theoretically* be doing in some optimal counterfactual life.

## Practical Exercises

1. **The "instead of" prompt**: before committing to any non-trivial choice, complete the sentence: "instead of doing X, I would be doing ___." If the blank is hard to fill, the choice has not yet been properly evaluated.
2. **The pre-mortem variant**: ask "if I do X and it goes well, what alternative use of these resources would I most regret not having pursued?" This forward-counterfactual surfaces foregone alternatives more reliably than after-the-fact reflection.
3. **Opportunity-cost-by-resource audit**: pick a major resource (a quarter's worth of weekend time; a year's discretionary capital) and enumerate the top three uses you actually deployed it on, then estimate the value of the *next* three options you considered but did not pursue. The exercise calibrates your sense of what your opportunity costs typically are.

## Case Studies

> [!case-study] Decisive Capital Allocation at Berkshire Hathaway
> Warren Buffett's published explanations of Berkshire's investment decisions are unusually explicit about opportunity-cost reasoning. The 1996 letter to shareholders states the rule: every prospective investment is compared against the *best alternative*, which Buffett operationalizes as "buying more of what we already own and understand." The implication is that even *individually attractive* investments are routinely declined because their expected return does not exceed the return of additional purchases of well-understood existing positions. This is opportunity-cost reasoning in pure form — the *visible* opportunity (a new investment) is rejected because the *less visible* opportunity (deepening existing positions) is judged superior. The case is paradigmatic because (a) it is publicly documented across multi-decade letter series, (b) it produces a counterintuitive operational policy (decline good investments), and (c) the empirical track record validates the discipline. Most practitioners reading the same letters fail to *internalize* the rule because the discipline of constantly re-eliciting the alternative is harder than knowing the principle.
