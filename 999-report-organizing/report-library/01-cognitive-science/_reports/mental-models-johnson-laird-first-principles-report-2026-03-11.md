# Mental Models (Johnson-Laird): A First Principles Report

> *Deconstructing the Model-Based Account of Human Reasoning*

---

## Phase I: The Conventional Understanding

### The Standard Account

The theory of mental models, developed most systematically by Philip Johnson-Laird in his landmark 1983 monograph and elaborated through decades of subsequent empirical work, occupies a central position in cognitive science's account of human reasoning. Its core claim is one of the more striking in the field: human beings do not reason by applying formal logical rules to propositional representations — they reason by constructing internal, analogically structured models of the states of affairs described by premises, inspecting those models, and reading off conclusions that hold in all of them.

> [!key-claim] **The Central Thesis of Mental Models Theory**
> When people reason — whether about spatial relations, conditional statements, syllogistic arguments, or causal sequences — they construct one or more mental models that represent possible states of the world described by the premises. A conclusion is endorsed if it holds in all constructed models. Reasoning errors occur not because inference rules are applied incorrectly, but because people typically construct an incomplete subset of the logically possible models, and a conclusion that seems to hold across their constructed models may be falsified by a model they failed to build.

The theory presents itself as an account of both *competence* (how people could in principle reason correctly) and *performance* (why they characteristically make the specific errors they do). This double-duty role — explaining both success and failure from a single mechanism — is one of the theory's most appealing features.

### The Standard Narrative

Johnson-Laird's account traces a line of intellectual descent from Kenneth Craik's 1943 proposal that the mind works by constructing "small-scale models of reality" used to anticipate events, and from Tarski's model-theoretic semantics, which provides formal grounding for the idea that truth is correspondence between sentences and models of the world. The narrative goes roughly like this: formal logic, despite its normative authority, cannot be psychologically realistic because people do not have access to their own inference rules, training in logic does not dramatically improve everyday reasoning performance, and the specific *pattern* of errors people make is not random but systematic — and systematically explicable by the hypothesis that people build partial models.

The canonical demonstration involves syllogistic reasoning. Given premises like "All the artists are beekeepers" and "Some of the beekeepers are chemists," most people find it difficult to determine what, if anything, follows necessarily. The mental models account explains difficulty in terms of the number of distinct models needed to represent the premises: problems requiring only one model are easy; problems requiring three models are hard. This model-number effect on difficulty is one of the theory's most replicated empirical achievements.

> [!observation] **Where the Conventional View Lives**
> Mental models theory is taught in cognitive psychology courses as the leading alternative to rule-based (syntactic) theories of reasoning. It informs research in cognitive linguistics, instructional design, human-computer interaction (especially in Norman's influential popularization of "mental models" in design contexts), and educational psychology. The term "mental model" has become so widespread that it functions as a loose synonym for "conceptual representation" in many fields far removed from Johnson-Laird's precise technical usage — a diffusion that has both extended and diluted the theory's reach.

### What the Conventional View Enables

The mental models framework has enabled a genuinely productive research program. It generates specific, falsifiable predictions about the relative difficulty of different inference problems. It provides a unified account of reasoning across multiple content domains — spatial, temporal, propositional, syllogistic — under a single representational hypothesis. It connects psychological research on reasoning to philosophical semantics in a principled way. And it offers a psychologically grounded account of what it means to *understand* a sentence: to understand is to construct a model, not merely to parse a syntactic structure.

> [!insight] **The Theory's Embedded Commitment**
> There is an implicit philosophy of mind embedded in the standard account: mental representations come in two fundamentally different formats — the propositional (language-like, discrete, rule-governed) and the analogical (model-like, continuous, structurally resemblant to what it represents). The theory is not merely about reasoning; it is a theory about the fundamental architecture of human thought. This commitment will prove important when we examine its assumptions.

### The First Hint of Trouble

The theory's power rests on a deceptively simple analogy: just as formal model theory provides truth conditions for logical sentences by defining what it means for a sentence to be true in a model, Johnson-Laird proposes that psychological reasoning works by constructing models and checking whether candidate conclusions hold in them. But Tarski's models are abstract mathematical objects, not mental states. The move from formal semantics to cognitive psychology requires a series of bridging claims — claims about what makes a representation "analogical," about how models are individuated, about why people build the models they do rather than others — that the standard account often leaves underspecified. This is where the first hint of trouble appears: the theory is doing a great deal of explanatory work with concepts whose psychological reality is much harder to establish than the theory's appealing formalism suggests.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Articulating Your Priors**
> Before we begin dismantling assumptions, take a moment to consider: what do you currently believe about mental models? Do you think human reasoning is fundamentally model-based, rule-based, or something else? Did you arrive at this view by examining evidence, or by absorbing it from coursework, popular accounts (Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things*), or the professional community you inhabit? Note that Johnson-Laird's precise technical sense of "mental model" and the loose colloquial sense are radically different — which one have you been thinking with?

## Phase II: The Assumption Audit

The mental models account, carefully unpacked, rests on a cluster of assumptions — some well-supported, some inherited from the formal tradition it draws on, and some that do not survive sustained scrutiny. What follows is a systematic audit.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 1: The Format Distinction**
> **Statement**: There are two fundamentally distinct representational formats in cognition — propositional (language-like, amodal, symbolic) and analogical (image-like, structurally resemblant to what they represent) — and mental models belong to the second category.
> **Origin**: Inherited from philosophical debates about imagery and cognitive architecture (Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn debates of the 1970s–80s); also from model-theoretic semantics.
> **Evidence For**: Neuroimaging shows format-specific activation patterns for spatial vs. verbal tasks; dual-coding theory (Paivio) supports independent verbal and imagistic systems; rotation and scanning studies on mental imagery suggest analog properties.
> **Evidence Against**: Pylyshyn's critique that apparent analog properties of imagery could be produced by propositional descriptions with tacit knowledge of visual properties; the "imagery debate" was never fully resolved; intermediate representational formats (distributed representations in connectionist networks) fit neither category cleanly.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Yes — the entire contrast with rule-based theories depends on this distinction. If all mental representations are ultimately propositional, the theory collapses into the accounts it opposes.
> **Verdict**: Revised ⚠ — The binary is probably too coarse. There are likely multiple representational formats (Barsalou's perceptual symbol systems, embodied simulation accounts), and "analogical" may describe a continuum of structural resemblance rather than a discrete category.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 2: Model Individuation**
> **Statement**: Mental models are countable, discrete objects; reasoning difficulty is a function of the *number* of distinct models that must be constructed to represent the premises.
> **Origin**: Derived from the need to make the theory predictive; model number is the primary variable manipulated in experimental tests.
> **Evidence For**: Model-number effects on difficulty are robustly replicated across many decades of syllogistic and relational reasoning research; more-model problems take longer and produce more errors.
> **Evidence Against**: The principle by which models are individuated is theoretically underdetermined — what makes two situations "one model" versus "two models"? The answer varies by theory version and has been criticized as post-hoc; model number often correlates with other variables (working memory load, alternative conclusion density) making causal attribution difficult.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Highly — this is the primary mechanistic claim generating quantitative predictions.
> **Verdict**: Suspended ? — Model number effects are real, but whether they reflect *model individuation* as opposed to working memory demands, semantic complexity, or search space size remains unresolved.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 3: Structural Resemblance**
> **Statement**: Mental models are structurally resemblant to the situations they represent — they share the relational structure of those situations in a way that propositional representations do not.
> **Origin**: Core philosophical commitment derived from Craik and analogical representation theory.
> **Evidence For**: Difficulty scaling with spatial layout complexity in relational reasoning; apparent ability to "read off" conclusions from spatial arrangements in model.
> **Evidence Against**: The notion of structural resemblance is philosophically contentious — what makes one structure "resemble" another requires a specification of the relevant mapping, and that specification may itself be propositionally encoded; Nelson Goodman's argument that resemblance is too promiscuous a relation to do representational work.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Yes — distinguishes the theory from propositional alternatives.
> **Verdict**: Revised ⚠ — Structural resemblance in a weak sense (same relational structure across domains) is plausible and empirically supported; in the strong sense (literally picture-like, continuous) it is philosophically fraught and probably unnecessary for the theory's empirical predictions.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 4: The Model-Completeness Hypothesis**
> **Statement**: People make reasoning errors primarily because they construct an *incomplete* set of models — they fail to consider all logically possible models of the premises, and so endorse conclusions that hold in their partial model set but are falsified by models they did not build.
> **Origin**: Central explanatory mechanism; connects the representational claim to the performance data.
> **Evidence For**: Counterexample-finding tasks support the idea that people can reject initially endorsed conclusions when guided to search for falsifying models; explicit instruction to consider alternatives improves performance.
> **Evidence Against**: The incompleteness account is extremely flexible — almost any error can be attributed to missing a model, making it difficult to falsify; alternative accounts (belief-bias, probabilistic heuristics, dual process shortcuts) explain the same error patterns with equal or greater parsimony; if incompleteness is the cause, why do people systematically *prefer* certain incomplete sets?
> **Load-Bearing?**: Extremely — this is the primary explanation for reasoning failures.
> **Verdict**: Revised ⚠ — Incomplete model search is a real phenomenon but probably interacts with prior beliefs, relevance assessments, and cognitive effort allocation in ways the pure incompleteness account does not capture.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 5: Rule-Based vs. Model-Based Reasoning as a Dichotomy**
> **Statement**: The primary theoretical choice in reasoning research is between rule-based accounts (mental logic, inference schemas) and model-based accounts; these are mutually exclusive explanations.
> **Origin**: The framing of the field from the 1980s onward, with Rips' PSYCOP and Braine's mental logic as the foils against which Johnson-Laird positioned his theory.
> **Evidence For**: Different empirical predictions in controlled cases; theoretical parsimony argues for one account over two; some neuropsychological dissociations suggest distinct processing modes.
> **Evidence Against**: Dual-process frameworks (Evans, Stanovich) propose that both analytic and heuristic processes contribute to reasoning; computational models increasingly suggest multiple processes; schema-based reasoning (Cheng & Holyoak's pragmatic reasoning schemas) fits neither category cleanly.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Conceptually central but not empirically essential — the empirical predictions can be made without the exclusivity claim.
> **Verdict**: Dissolved ✗ — The dichotomy is probably a theoretical artifact. Multiple representational formats and multiple processes almost certainly operate in reasoning, and the interesting question is when each dominates, not which one is "the" mechanism.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 6: Model Construction is Driven by Semantic Content, Not by Syntax**
> **Statement**: Reasoning proceeds by constructing models of the *meaning* of premises, not by operating on their syntactic form; this is why content effects are central to model-based accounts.
> **Origin**: Reaction against formal rule accounts which treat logical form as the primary determinant of reasoning.
> **Evidence For**: Content and context dramatically affect reasoning — the Wason selection task is solved far more accurately with social contract content; thematic materials facilitate reasoning; pragmatic interpretations of conditionals vary with content.
> **Evidence Against**: Content effects are fully compatible with rule-based accounts augmented with domain-specific schemas (pragmatic reasoning schemas); the direction of causality is unclear — content may affect which rules are retrieved, not whether rules vs. models are used.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Moderate — used to argue against rule theories, but the evidence does not unambiguously favor models.
> **Verdict**: Retained ✓ — Content matters for reasoning. But this assumption survives in a theoretically neutral form: content effects are real regardless of the representational format that mediates them.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 7: Working Memory Supports Model Maintenance**
> **Statement**: The bottleneck in reasoning is working memory capacity for maintaining and manipulating mental models; individual differences in reasoning track working memory span.
> **Origin**: Developed in later work; operationalizes the cognitive demands of model construction.
> **Evidence For**: Working memory span correlates with reasoning ability across numerous studies; dual-task paradigms show reasoning degradation under concurrent working memory load; more-model problems make greater working memory demands.
> **Evidence Against**: Working memory correlates are predicted equally well by rule-based accounts that require maintaining intermediate proof steps; the causal mechanism (it's specifically *models* that load WM) is not independently established.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Moderate — needed for the developmental and individual-differences program.
> **Verdict**: Retained ✓ — Working memory involvement is well-established; the claim that it specifically supports model (vs. rule) maintenance is revised to suspend judgment.

> [!assumption] **Assumption 8: Johnson-Laird's Models Are Psychologically Realized**
> **Statement**: The formal models described in the theory — with explicit tokens representing individuals, relations explicitly encoded, explicit and implicit information distinguished — correspond to actual cognitive structures, not merely computational metaphors.
> **Origin**: Required for the theory to be a *psychological* theory rather than a competence model.
> **Evidence For**: Reaction time and error patterns consistent with formal model predictions; neuroimaging showing spatial processing in relational reasoning tasks.
> **Evidence Against**: The formal models are specified at a functional level that underdetermines implementation; the same input-output behavior could be produced by very different underlying mechanisms; connectionist and probabilistic accounts generate the same predictions without positing discrete model tokens.
> **Load-Bearing?**: Yes — distinguishes the theory from instrumentalist accounts.
> **Verdict**: Suspended ? — Whether the formal description is implemented as described or merely simulated by other mechanisms remains an open empirical question.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Noticing Your Resistance**
> Which of these assumption audits produced resistance? If you reacted to the dissolution of the rule/model dichotomy with "but that's obviously one or the other," note that reaction. The dichotomy is one of the field's inherited framings — it shaped careers, textbooks, and funding priorities — and its dissolution is therefore unsettling even when the evidence supports it. Productive discomfort here is the signal that we've found something load-bearing in the conventional understanding.

> [!reflection] **After the Audit**
> 1. The most surprising dissolution is probably Assumption 5 — the rule/model dichotomy. If this is an artifact, the *entire framing* of the debate changes, not just the resolution.
> 2. Assumption 6 (content matters) survives but in theoretically neutral form — it no longer serves as evidence *for* models *against* rules.
> 3. The conventional understanding is less stable than it appears: three assumptions dissolved or are suspended, two require significant revision, and only two survive relatively intact.

## Phase III: Decomposition to Axioms

Having cleared away unjustified assumptions, what remains? The following four axioms represent propositions that survive rigorous scrutiny — propositions that are supported by strong evidence or logical necessity and do not depend on the assumptions we dissolved.

> [!principle-point] **Axiom 1: Systematicity of Reasoning Errors**
> **Statement**: Human reasoning errors are not random. Across a wide range of inference types and content domains, the pattern of errors people make is systematic — specific types of inferences are reliably harder than others, specific error types are reliably more frequent, and the pattern holds across individuals and cultures.
> **Type**: Empirical axiom
> **Justification**: This is one of the most robustly replicated findings in cognitive psychology. The difficulty ordering of syllogistic figures, the Wason selection task error profile, the systematic confusion of necessary and sufficient conditions in causal reasoning — none of these require any specific representational theory to establish. They are brute empirical facts that any adequate theory must explain. They cannot themselves be explained by simple resource accounts (difficulty should then be random by content), nor by formal logic (from logic's standpoint, easy and hard problems are equally valid).
> **Scope**: Holds for formal reasoning tasks; extends to probabilistic and causal reasoning with appropriate modifications. Does not require that *every* error be systematic — only that reliable patterns exist.
> **Derived From**: Meta-analyses of syllogistic reasoning (Evans, Newstead & Byrne, 1993); Wason selection task replications; cross-cultural reasoning studies.

> [!principle-point] **Axiom 2: Representation is Constructive, Not Readout**
> **Statement**: The cognitive representations used in reasoning are not passively read from perceptual input or linguistic input but are actively constructed — they can incorporate information not explicitly present in the input, default assumptions, and prior knowledge integrated with current content.
> **Type**: Empirical axiom
> **Justification**: This is established across multiple research traditions independent of mental models theory. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, schema theory, situation model research (van Dijk & Kintsch), and the extensive literature on pragmatic inference all converge: what people represent goes substantially beyond what is literally stated. Crucially, this constructive process is *not* error — it is adaptive elaboration of sparse input to a rich situational understanding. The errors arise from specific features of the constructive process, not from construction per se.
> **Scope**: Universal for language comprehension and reasoning; the specific constructive mechanisms vary by domain and task.
> **Derived From**: Bartlett (1932); van Dijk & Kintsch (1983) situation model framework; Bransford & Johnson (1972) constructive comprehension studies.

> [!principle-point] **Axiom 3: Reasoning Performance is Resource-Sensitive**
> **Statement**: The accuracy and completeness of human inference is not fixed but varies systematically with the cognitive resources available — specifically, the amount of information that must be held in mind simultaneously, the demands of searching through alternative possibilities, and the time and motivation allocated to the task.
> **Type**: Empirical axiom
> **Justification**: Dual-task paradigms, working memory span correlations, time pressure manipulations, and individual difference studies all establish this beyond reasonable dispute. The resource-sensitivity is not merely that "hard things take more effort" (trivially true) but that specific resource manipulations produce specific effects on specific reasoning components. Moreover, resource-sensitivity interacts with content: some contents facilitate resource-efficient processing (chunking, schema use, domain expertise), which explains why domain knowledge dramatically improves reasoning without necessarily changing the formal demands.
> **Scope**: Holds across all studied reasoning domains; the specific resources implicated vary by task type.
> **Derived From**: Extensive working memory × reasoning correlational literature; dual-task experimental designs; expertise studies.

> [!principle-point] **Axiom 4: Reasoning Operates Over Semantic Content, Not Merely Syntactic Form**
> **Statement**: The content, context, and pragmatic interpretation of a reasoning problem materially affect how it is processed and what conclusions are drawn — independently of its logical form. Formally equivalent problems yield different performance when content varies.
> **Type**: Empirical axiom
> **Justification**: The Wason selection task is the canonical demonstration — logically identical problems yield dramatically different accuracy rates depending on whether they concern abstract rules or deontic social contracts. But the finding extends far beyond this: syllogistic reasoning is affected by believability of conclusions, conditional reasoning is affected by pragmatic interpretation of "if," causal reasoning is affected by mechanism knowledge. These content effects are not noise — they have their own systematic structure. Any adequate theory of reasoning must accommodate them as first-class phenomena.
> **Scope**: Ubiquitous in adult reasoning; modulated but not eliminated by formal training; cross-cultural.
> **Derived From**: Griggs & Cox (1982) facilitation studies; Evans (1984) belief-bias paradigm; Cheng & Holyoak (1985) pragmatic reasoning schemas; cross-cultural Wason task replications.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Testing the Axioms**
> For Axiom 1: Can you imagine a world in which reasoning errors are random? What would that world's cognitive architecture look like? The fact that it's hard to imagine is evidence of the axiom's robustness — randomness would require that all systematic influences on reasoning (working memory, content, prior knowledge) have *zero net effect*, which is implausible.
> For Axiom 5 (the one we lack): Notice that none of these axioms specify *what kind of representation* is doing the work. That absence is not an oversight — it reflects the genuine state of the evidence.

> [!reflection] **The Foundations Revealed**
> 1. There are *four* axioms, not Johnson-Laird's rich representational architecture — far fewer than the theory deploys.
> 2. Notably, none of the four axioms require the concept of a "mental model" as technically defined. They require: systematicity, construction, resource-sensitivity, and content-sensitivity. These are compatible with model-based, rule-based, probabilistic, and hybrid architectures.
> 3. The most important missing axiom — the one we cannot establish — is the representational format axiom: whether reasoning proceeds specifically via analogical models. That is not a bedrock truth; it is a theoretical hypothesis about the mechanism that implements the four genuine axioms.

## Phase IV: Reconstruction from Fundamentals

Starting from the four axioms alone, what can we derive? The reconstruction proceeds in stages, noting where it converges with or diverges from the conventional mental models account.

**From Axiom 1 + Axiom 2**: Systematic reasoning errors arise from features of the constructive process itself, not from random failures or simple resource exhaustion. When people construct representations of reasoning problems, the constructive process introduces *specific biases and tendencies* that produce specific error patterns. This is what we can derive: the cause of systematic errors is systematic in the representations people build, not in noise or fatigue. [CONVERGES] with Johnson-Laird's account — errors arise from the representations people construct. [DIVERGES] in that this does not tell us whether those representations are analogical models or something else.

**From Axiom 3 + Axiom 1**: The difficulty ordering of reasoning problems reflects the cognitive demands of the constructive process under resource constraints, not properties of the logical problems per se. A hard syllogism is hard because the constructive process that represents its content is more resource-demanding — requires more information to be held simultaneously, more alternatives to be evaluated. [CONVERGES] with the model-number effect — harder problems make greater demands. [DIVERGES] in that "number of models" is one measure of representational demand but not the only one, and the evidence does not rule out that the relevant demands are about proof steps, alternative conclusions, or other variables that correlate with model number.

**From Axiom 4**: Content effects in reasoning are not a residue to be explained away but a primary datum demanding a positive account. Any adequate theory must explain *why* content facilitates or hinders reasoning. From this axiom alone, we can derive that people do not process reasoning problems as abstract formal objects — there is no format-neutral reasoning that then gets distorted by content. Content is primary; formal structure is the abstraction. [CONVERGES] with mental models theory's emphasis on semantic processing. [CONVERGES] also, surprisingly, with rule-based accounts augmented with domain-specific schemas — Cheng & Holyoak's pragmatic reasoning schemas are rule-like but content-sensitive.

> [!key-claim] **The Central Derived Conclusion**
> From the four axioms together, the following conclusion follows: human reasoning is a *constructive, resource-sensitive, content-sensitive process that produces systematic patterns of success and failure*. This conclusion is robust. What does *not* follow from the axioms is the representational format of the construction — whether the constructed representations are analogical mental models, propositional rule applications, probabilistic estimates, or some multi-format combination. The axioms are format-agnostic.

**From Axiom 2 + Axiom 4**: [NOVEL] A crucial insight emerges that neither the standard mental models account nor its rule-based competitors makes sufficiently central: the constructive process is *pragmatically guided*. People do not construct all possible representations — they construct the representations that are most useful for the task at hand, given their goals, prior knowledge, and pragmatic interpretation of the discourse. This means the question "why did they fail to construct model M₃?" is not answered by "working memory limits" alone but by "model M₃ was not constructed because it was not pragmatically relevant given the interpreter's goals." Errors are not merely computational failures; they are often adaptive responses to task demands under resource constraints.

> [!key-insight] **The Pragmatic Construction Insight**
> The deepest revision that emerges from reconstruction is this: what looks like a reasoning error from the standpoint of formal logic may be an *accurate representation of what is pragmatically relevant* in context. The model-completeness failure (failing to build model M₃) is not simply negligence — it reflects an implicit assessment that M₃ is unlikely or irrelevant. When that implicit assessment is wrong (because the experimenter has contrived a situation where M₃ is critical), the "error" is detectable. When the assessment is right (in everyday life, M₃ really is rare or irrelevant), no error occurs. The gap between laboratory and everyday reasoning performance is predicted by this insight: everyday pragmatics calibrates well to everyday base rates.

> [!insight] **Convergence with Probabilistic Accounts**
> This reconstruction converges with a point made forcefully by Oaksford & Chater's probabilistic model of the Wason selection task: human performance on selection tasks is approximately Bayesian optimal under realistic prior probabilities for everyday conditional rules. What looks like a failure of deductive reasoning is revealed, under reconstruction, to be reasonable probabilistic inference. This is not merely "excusing" errors — it is showing that the axioms lead to a different normative standard than deductive validity.

**From Axiom 3 + Axiom 2**: Individual differences in reasoning ability reflect individual differences in the resources available for the constructive process — but also individual differences in the *content* of prior knowledge that can scaffold construction, reducing resource demands through chunking and schema use. This explains why domain expertise improves reasoning performance without necessarily improving abstract reasoning ability: expertise provides richer schematic structure that reduces the resources needed for construction.

> [!example] **Spatial Reasoning as a Test Case**
> Consider spatial relational reasoning — "A is to the left of B; B is to the left of C; therefore A is to the left of C." This is easy. "A is to the left of B; C is to the right of A; where is B relative to C?" — this is harder. The mental models account explains this by the number of models needed. Our reconstruction says: the constructive process for the second problem requires more simultaneous information. Both accounts predict the same difficulty ordering. The axiom-based reconstruction does not tell us whether people use a spatial analog or a propositional representation — but it does tell us that whatever they use, it must be sensitive to simultaneity demands. Neuroimaging, which shows parietal (spatial) activation for these tasks, is consistent with analog representation but does not prove it — spatial computations can be performed on non-spatial representations.

> [!thought-experiment] **The Underdetermination Test**
> Construct any experiment that tests mental models theory. Now ask: does the predicted result uniquely support *model-based* over *rule-based* representation, or does it support the four axioms, which are compatible with either? In virtually every case, the experiment tests the axioms, not the representational format. The format question requires evidence of a fundamentally different kind — evidence about the *intrinsic structure* of representations, not their *functional properties*. That kind of evidence is extremely difficult to obtain in psychological research.

> [!reflection] **The Rebuilt Understanding**
> 1. The convergence that is most reassuring: reasoning errors are systematic and arise from the constructive process. This confirms that Johnson-Laird was right to target the *representations* people build as the locus of errors, not the inference rules they apply.
> 2. The divergence that is most unsettling: the specific representational format (analogical mental models) is not established by the evidence that supports the theory's empirical predictions. The question may be unanswerable at the level of behavioral experiments.
> 3. The practical change: interventions aimed at improving reasoning should target the *constructive process* — teaching people to generate alternative representations, to consider what they may have omitted, to interrogate their pragmatic assumptions — rather than specifically "thinking in models" or "applying rules."

## Phase V: What Changes — Novel Insights and Revised Understanding

### The Revised Understanding

Rebuilt from first principles, mental models theory is best understood not as a theory of *representational format* but as a theory of *constructive reasoning processes* — a theory whose central insights are:

1. Reasoning proceeds via active construction, not passive rule application.
2. The systematic structure of reasoning errors reflects the systematic structure of that constructive process.
3. Content, pragmatics, and prior knowledge shape what gets constructed, not just how quickly.
4. Resource demands constrain the completeness of construction, and completeness failures have predictable error patterns.

These insights are genuine and important. The representational format claim — that the constructed representations are specifically analogical mental models — remains a hypothesis rather than an established axiom.

> [!key-takeaway] **What Johnson-Laird Got Fundamentally Right**
> The most important contribution of mental models theory is not the *model* part but the *construction* part. The shift from viewing reasoning as rule application to viewing it as situation-model construction was a genuine theoretical advance. Understanding a text or a set of premises is not decoding symbols — it is building a representation of a state of the world that goes beyond what is literally stated. This insight, shared with situation model theory in discourse comprehension, is among the most important ideas in cognitive science of the past fifty years.

### Practical Implications

[WHAT CONVENTIONAL UNDERSTANDING GETS WRONG] The practical import of the standard mental models account is often interpreted as: help people reason better by training them to "build mental models" — to think visually, to construct diagrams, to reason spatially. This is a reasonable heuristic but rests on the unestablished format assumption. The more robust practical implication from the axiom-based reconstruction is: help people reason better by training them to *consider alternatives they might otherwise omit* (addressing Axiom 1 + incompleteness), *make their constructive assumptions explicit* (addressing Axiom 2), *manage the simultaneity demands of complex problems* (addressing Axiom 3), and *interrogate their pragmatic interpretations* (addressing Axiom 4). These interventions work regardless of the representational format.

> [!best-practice] **Evidence-Based Reasoning Interventions**
> Based on the axiom-level reconstruction, the following interventions have the strongest theoretical and empirical support for improving reasoning:
> - **Alternative generation training**: Explicitly prompting people to ask "what other scenarios are compatible with these premises?" targets the incompleteness problem without committing to a representational format.
> - **Pragmatic assumption elicitation**: Teaching reasoners to make explicit the background assumptions that shape their interpretation targets Axiom 4's content sensitivity.
> - **Worked example diversity**: Exposing reasoners to the same logical form across many content domains builds the abstraction that makes formal reasoning available across contexts.
> - **Argument mapping**: Externalizing reasoning structure reduces the working memory demands identified in Axiom 3, regardless of whether internal representations are models or rules.

> [!warning] **What the Conventional View Gets Wrong**
> The conventional view's conflation of Johnson-Laird's technical mental models with the folk-psychological notion of a "mental model" (as in "the user's mental model of the system") has obscured important distinctions. In HCI and design, "mental model" typically refers to a user's understanding of how a system works — which is closer to what cognitive psychologists call a *schema* or *naïve theory*, not to Johnson-Laird's token-based, semantics-respecting models. Design practice based on "fixing users' mental models" often targets the wrong level of cognitive organization.

### What Convention Gets Right

The conventional view correctly identifies that humans are not logic machines, that reasoning is sensitive to semantic content, and that specific patterns of errors recur across people and tasks. These are robust findings that the axiom-based reconstruction preserves and explains. The conventional view also correctly identifies that expertise improves reasoning within domains — the schema enrichment story is preserved under reconstruction, even if its mechanism is reinterpreted.

> [!important] **A Critical Limitation of This Analysis**
> This first-principles analysis is conducted at the level of behavioral and cognitive data available through 2024. The representational format question — are mental models analogical or propositional? — may become more tractable as cognitive neuroscience develops finer-grained methods for identifying the intrinsic structure of mental representations. Current fMRI and EEG data constrain but do not resolve the question. This analysis should be revisited as neuroimaging methodology advances.

### Open Questions

The reconstruction surfaces several genuinely open questions that neither the conventional account nor this analysis can resolve:

1. **The format question**: Is there ultimately a fact of the matter about whether mental representations used in reasoning are analogical or propositional, or is this a pseudo-question — like asking whether a neural network "really" represents information in distributed or local form?

2. **The normativity problem**: If systematic reasoning errors are partly adaptive (pragmatically guided), what is the appropriate normative standard for human reasoning? Is deductive validity, Bayesian probability, or some other standard the right benchmark?

3. **The individuation problem**: Even granting that some form of model-building occurs, how are the "relevant" models for a given inference task determined? What determines which possible worlds count as the models of a set of premises for a given reasoner?

> [!reflection] **Living with Revised Understanding**
> 1. This analysis should produce more confidence in the *empirical* core of mental models research (the error patterns, the difficulty effects) and less confidence in the *theoretical* interpretation (that these effects prove analogical representation). One can be a thoroughgoing mental models researcher while agnostic about format.
> 2. The single most important change: the rule/model dichotomy was always a theoretical framing, not a discovery. Dissolving it opens space for pluralistic accounts — perhaps the most important implication for researchers.
> 3. For practitioners: the implication is not "stop teaching mental model building" but "be agnostic about whether the benefit comes from the analogical format or from the process of active construction and alternative generation."

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Limitations & PKB Integration

### The Arc of the Argument

This report began with a theory that offers a unified, elegant account of human reasoning: people construct analogical mental models, reason by inspecting them, and err by failing to construct the full set. The assumption audit revealed that this elegant account rests on a binary (propositional vs. analogical) that is probably too coarse, an individuation principle that is underdetermined, a model-completeness hypothesis that is unfalsifiably flexible, and a dichotomy with rule-based theories that is almost certainly a theoretical artifact.

Stripping to axioms, we find four robust empirical truths: reasoning errors are systematic, representations are constructed, reasoning is resource-sensitive, and content-sensitivity is primary. None of these axioms requires the analogical format hypothesis. Reconstructing from these axioms yields an account that preserves what is most valuable in Johnson-Laird's program — the focus on constructive processes, the semantic richness of reasoning, the error-pattern predictions — while releasing the unestablished commitment to a specific representational format.

The single most important insight that reconstruction reveals — invisible within the conventional framing — is the pragmatic guidance of construction: people build the representations that are pragmatically appropriate, not the representations that are logically complete. This reframes "errors" as contextually calibrated responses rather than failures of a defective inference machine.

### Methodological Honesty

This analysis has its own limitations. The analyst is working at the level of behavioral data and established theoretical critique; the representational format question may be empirically tractable in ways not yet realized. The axiomatic decomposition reflects judgments about what counts as "fundamental" — a different analyst might draw the boundaries differently. The dissolution of the rule/model dichotomy is not new; it reflects a consensus developing in the field since the early 2000s that the analyst is reporting, not discovering. And the pragmatic construction insight, while not prominent in Johnson-Laird's own framing, is implicit in the theory and has been developed by theorists including Sperber & Wilson (relevance theory) and Oaksford & Chater (probabilistic accounts).

## Phase VII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Mental Model (Johnson-Laird technical sense)**
> A cognitive representation of a state of affairs that is: (a) structurally analogous to that state of affairs rather than merely encoding propositional descriptions of it; (b) constructed from semantic content rather than syntactic form; (c) token-based (containing representations of specific individuals, not just variables); and (d) capable of underwriting inference by inspection rather than rule application. Distinguished from: (i) propositional representations, (ii) images in the narrow visual sense, (iii) "mental model" in the folk-cognitive or HCI sense.

> [!definition] **Mental Model (folk/HCI sense)**
> A person's conceptual understanding of how a system, process, or domain works — their intuitive theory or schema about the causal and structural organization of a domain. Related to but distinct from Johnson-Laird's technical usage; closer to *naïve theory* or *domain schema* in the cognitive science literature. Confusion between these senses has caused considerable miscommunication across disciplines.

> [!definition] **Propositional Representation**
> A cognitive representation in a language-like format: discrete, compositional, amodal (not tied to any sensory modality), and rule-governed. Operations on propositional representations are syntactic — they apply to the form of the representation regardless of its content. The mental logic tradition (Rips' PSYCOP, Braine's natural deduction) posits that deductive reasoning operates by applying inference rules to propositional representations.

> [!definition] **Analogical Representation**
> A cognitive representation whose internal structure resembles the structure of what it represents — shares some non-trivial structural properties with its object. Mental models are held to be analogical: a model of "A is to the left of B" has A-token spatially arranged relative to B-token in a way that mirrors the described spatial relation. Contrast with propositional representations, which encode relations symbolically without structural mirroring.

> [!definition] **Model-Completeness Hypothesis**
> The hypothesis, central to Johnson-Laird's performance theory, that reasoning errors arise from constructing an *incomplete* set of models — failing to consider all logically possible models of the premises. A conclusion appears to hold because it holds in the partial model set constructed, but fails because a non-constructed model falsifies it. The primary explanation for the difficulty effects and error rates in syllogistic and propositional reasoning.

> [!definition] **Pragmatic Reasoning Schemas**
> Domain-specific, content-sensitive rule-like structures (proposed by Cheng & Holyoak) that govern reasoning in particular pragmatic contexts — especially permission schemas (if action then precondition must be satisfied) and obligation schemas. Represent a hybrid position: rule-like in form but content-sensitive in application. Used to explain the facilitation of Wason selection task performance with thematic materials without positing mental models.

> [!definition] **Assumption 5: Rule/Model Dichotomy**
> The framing of reasoning research as a choice between rule-based (mental logic) accounts and model-based (mental models) accounts — treating these as mutually exclusive. This report's audit identifies this as a dissolved assumption: the dichotomy is probably a theoretical artifact rather than a genuine description of cognitive architecture.

> [!definition] **Situation Model**
> In discourse comprehension theory (van Dijk & Kintsch, 1983), a representation of the *situation* described by a text — not merely a propositional encoding of the text's surface content but a multidimensional representation including spatial, temporal, causal, and intentional structure. Closely related to mental models theory but developed independently and with a stronger empirical tradition in comprehension research.

> [!definition] **Axiom 1: Systematicity of Reasoning Errors**
> The foundational empirical axiom of this reconstruction: reasoning errors are not random but form systematic patterns across individuals and content domains. The difficulty ordering of syllogistic figures, the Wason task error profile, and belief-bias effects are all instances. This axiom must be explained by any adequate theory of reasoning.

> [!definition] **Pragmatic Construction**
> The novel insight from Phase IV: representation construction in reasoning is guided not by logical completeness but by pragmatic relevance — people construct the representations that are most informative and relevant given their goals and context. This explains why "reasoning errors" in laboratory tasks are often adaptive responses to realistic prior probabilities, and why everyday reasoning performs much better than laboratory deductive reasoning studies suggest.
