# The Vocabulary of the Inner Voice: Attribution, Self-Efficacy, and Motivational Inner Speech in Self-Regulated Academic Reading

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Analytical Focus

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Schema Activation — Before You Begin**
> Before reading this analysis, take a moment to recall a recent experience of reading a challenging academic text. What were you saying to yourself as you read — especially when you encountered difficulty? Were you explaining your struggle to yourself, and if so, how? Did you reference your own ability, the text's difficulty, your level of preparation, or something else? Writing down even one or two sentences of your typical inner commentary during difficult academic reading will activate the conceptual structures this analysis is designed to develop. The gap between your starting vocabulary and the vocabulary you leave with is where this analysis does its real work.

There is a persistent paradox in the study of academic reading that motivational psychology has only recently begun to take seriously. Two readers can sit down with an identical text, possess equivalent domain knowledge, deploy comparable reading strategies, and arrive at radically different motivational states by the time they reach the final paragraph. One reader finishes with a sense of competence, a clear set of goals for follow-up investigation, and an eagerness to apply what was encountered. The other finishes with diminished confidence, vague discomfort about what was or was not understood, and a subtle reluctance to engage with similar material again. The text cannot account for this divergence. The strategies cannot account for it. The knowledge base cannot account for it. What can account for it is something far less visible — the quality, vocabulary, and structure of the inner speech that accompanied the reading.

This report examines a specific and underexplored question: **How does the vocabulary deployed in inner speech during silent academic reading shape attributional patterns, self-efficacy beliefs, goal construction quality, and the depth of self-regulated learning?** This is not a question about what readers *do* while reading — the strategy literature handles that adequately. It is a question about what readers *say to themselves* while reading, and more specifically about the particular words they use to narrate their cognitive and motivational experience.

The analytical scope is deliberately narrow. This report is not a survey of inner speech research, a review of reading comprehension strategies, or a general treatment of motivation in education. Each of those topics has its own foundational treatment in this knowledge base. Here, the focus is the precise intersection where vocabulary, motivation, and self-regulated academic reading meet — and the claim that this intersection constitutes a trainable cognitive-motivational architecture that most learners are leaving completely undeveloped.

The report draws primarily on [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]] (especially [[bernard-weiner]]'s dimensional model), [[self-efficacy-theory]] (Bandura's sources framework), [[self-determination-theory]]'s regulatory language continuum, and [[achievement-goal-theory]]. These are not arbitrarily assembled — they are precisely the theoretical frameworks whose empirical predictions most directly implicate linguistic and vocabulary mechanisms. Each predicts that *the specific language used to interpret learning experiences* has measurable downstream effects on motivation, persistence, goal quality, and ultimately comprehension and critical thinking.

The analysis proceeds through seven analytical phases. Phases II and III establish the conceptual and evidentiary foundations. Phase IV — the analytical core — dissects the specific mechanisms through which vocabulary shapes motivational and cognitive outcomes. Phase V translates the analysis into practical tools you can begin using immediately. Phase VI synthesizes an original model: the **Motivational Lexicon** — the structured vocabulary repertoire that constitutes the hidden infrastructure of advanced self-regulated reading. Phase VII extracts transferable structural principles. The report closes with PKB connections (Phase VIII) and a full appendix.

## Phase II: Analytical Framework — The Three-Theory Architecture

### Defining the Territory: What Is Motivational Inner Speech?

> [!definition] **Inner Speech (Motivational)**
> The internalized verbal stream that accompanies and partially constitutes cognitive activity, carrying evaluative, attributional, regulatory, and goal-related content. Distinguished from subvocalization (phonological encoding of text during reading) by its self-referential and interpretive character — it is speech *about* the learning experience, not speech *of* the text. Rooted in [[lev-vygotsky]]'s account of the developmental internalization of social speech, but extended here to cover the specifically motivational register of inner commentary that monitors, evaluates, and directs self-regulated activity.

Several distinctions sharpen the analytical focus. *Motivational* inner speech is the subset of inner speech concerned with causal explanation ("Why am I not getting this?"), self-evaluation ("I'm doing well here"), regulatory self-direction ("I need to slow down and re-read"), and goal construction ("I want to understand this argument fully before moving on"). This is categorically different from the phonological rehearsal involved in decoding text, which belongs to the [[working-memory-in-reading]] literature. It is also distinct from the elaborative self-explanation of content (the [[self-explanation-effect]]), though the two frequently co-occur and interact.

> [!key-claim] **The Vocabulary Hypothesis**
> The specific lexical choices within motivational inner speech — not just the general emotional valence — are functionally significant. "I can't do this" and "I don't have the right strategy yet" may produce similar surface affect but generate categorically different attributional inferences, self-efficacy predictions, and goal constructions. Vocabulary is not packaging for pre-formed meaning; it is partially constitutive of the motivational state itself.

### The Three-Theory Architecture

Three bodies of theory, each with strong empirical grounding, converge on the claim that motivational vocabulary matters. Together they constitute the analytical framework.

**Pillar 1: Attribution Theory and the Dimensional Vocabulary of Cause.** [[bernard-weiner]]'s [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]] (1985, 2010) argues that the causal explanations learners construct for their successes and failures are not simple emotional reactions but structured dimensional inferences that carry specific motivational consequences. Weiner identified three critical dimensions along which causal attributions vary: *locus* (internal vs. external to the learner), *stability* (stable vs. unstable over time), and *controllability* (controllable vs. uncontrollable). Critically for this analysis, every attribution is encoded in language, and the vocabulary used to articulate a cause tends to pre-load a specific dimensional configuration. The word "ability" encodes internal, stable, and uncontrollable. The word "strategy" encodes internal, unstable, and controllable. The word "luck" encodes external, unstable, and uncontrollable. These are not arbitrary conventions — they are semantic commitments with downstream motivational force.

**Pillar 2: Self-Efficacy Theory and the Verbal Persuasion Source.** [[albert-bandura]]'s [[self-efficacy-theory]] (1977, 1997) identifies four sources through which self-efficacy beliefs are constructed and revised: mastery experiences, vicarious learning, physiological/affective states, and *verbal persuasion*. Most discussions of verbal persuasion focus on encouragement from others. But inner speech is, in the most direct sense, verbal persuasion from the self. The evaluative commentary that accompanies reading — "I've been working at this correctly and making progress" versus "I'm just not a natural at this kind of material" — functions as real-time self-efficacy updating through the verbal persuasion mechanism. [[self-efficacy]] is not just a belief stored somewhere that occasionally surfaces; it is continuously constructed and revised through precisely this kind of running self-commentary.

**Pillar 3: SDT Regulatory Language and the Vocabulary of Autonomy.** [[self-determination-theory]]'s [[internalization]] describes a regulatory spectrum from external regulation through [[introjected-regulation]], [[identified-regulation]], to [[Integrated Regulation]]. Each regulatory style has a characteristic vocabulary. Introjected regulation speaks in "should," "must," "ought to," and "have to." Identified and integrated regulation speaks in "I want to," "I'm choosing to," "this matters to me," and "I find this interesting." Research by Deci, Ryan, and colleagues establishes that *the regulatory vocabulary used in self-talk is not merely descriptive of an underlying motivational state — it is partially constitutive of that state*. Reading because you "have to" and reading because you "choose to" are not simply subjectively different; they produce measurable differences in depth of processing, persistence, and transfer.

**The Achievement Goal Theory Extension.** [[achievement-goal-theory]] adds a fourth dimension: the vocabulary of *goal orientation*. Mastery-oriented goal language ("Did I understand this better than before?") and performance-oriented goal language ("Did I look competent relative to what I expect of myself?") prime different monitoring processes, different error-interpretation patterns, and different responses to challenge. In the context of academic reading, goal vocabulary in the [[forethought-phase]] predicts the quality of monitoring and the quality of the [[self-reflection-phase]] attributions that follow.

> [!definition] **Attributional Vocabulary**
> The specific words and phrases used to explain the causes of academic outcomes. Functions as a dimensional commitment: particular vocabulary choices pre-load locus, stability, and controllability inferences. Importantly, this vocabulary operates in both the reflection phase (explaining past outcomes) and the forethought phase (predicting future outcomes), creating a cross-phase vocabulary cascade.

> [!definition] **Regulatory Self-Talk**
> Inner speech that carries the regulatory register — the "why am I doing this" and "how am I directing this effort" layer of motivational inner speech. SDT's regulatory language research establishes that controlled regulatory vocabulary (should/must/have to) produces a functionally different motivational state than autonomous regulatory vocabulary (want/choose/find meaningful), even when describing identical behavior.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Conceptual Checkpoint**
> The framework distinguishes three types of motivational inner speech: *attributional* (explaining causes), *evaluative* (assessing efficacy), and *regulatory* (directing engagement). Before proceeding, can you generate one example of each type from your own typical reading experience? If you find yourself reaching for generic phrases rather than your own authentic inner speech patterns, this is itself valuable metacognitive data — it suggests your inner speech vocabulary may be operating below the level of conscious access, which is exactly what this analysis is designed to change.

The three-theory architecture generates a specific analytical prediction: that deliberate cultivation of attributional vocabulary, self-efficacy vocabulary, and regulatory vocabulary will produce measurable improvements in motivation, goal quality, and ultimately comprehension — and that this is achievable through targeted practice rather than requiring personality change or circumstantial luck.

*This framework selects these three theories not because others are irrelevant but because they are the frameworks whose core constructs are most directly lexical in character — they make predictions specifically about the language through which motivation is constructed, not merely about the motivational outcomes that result. This narrows the analytical scope in a way that makes the practical vocabulary-development work tractable.*

> **Application**: Reflect on your habitual vocabulary in each of the three registers (attributional, evaluative, regulatory). Which register do you think has the most undeveloped vocabulary?

## Phase III: Critical Examination of Evidence

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Knowledge State — Before**

### The Attribution Vocabulary Research Base

The empirical evidence for the vocabulary-attribution connection comes from two converging research traditions: [[attribution-retraining]] interventions (most prominently [[Attribution-Retraining-Interventions]]) and the naturalistic assessment literature.

[[attribution-retraining]] studies, pioneered by researchers in the Weiner tradition including Carol Dweck and Martin Seligman, consistently demonstrate that learners can be trained to shift their attributional patterns — and that these shifts produce downstream motivational and performance effects. Crucially, the mechanism in virtually all successful attribution retraining is *linguistic*: learners are explicitly taught different vocabulary for explaining their outcomes. Dweck's classic studies with learned helplessness in mathematics showed that children who received feedback framing errors as strategic rather than ability-based ("You tried a strategy that didn't work here" rather than "You got that wrong") showed significantly greater persistence and performance recovery. The vocabulary was not mere packaging — it was the active ingredient.

> [!evidence] **Weiner's Attributional Dimensional Research**
> Across dozens of studies, Weiner and colleagues established that the stability dimension of attribution is most strongly predictive of expectancy change (whether the learner expects to do better or worse next time), while the locus dimension most strongly predicts emotional outcomes (pride/shame for internal attributions, gratitude/anger for external). Controllability predicts whether effort mobilization or helplessness follows. The vocabulary that encodes each dimension differs systematically: ability vocabulary (stable-internal-uncontrollable) reliably produces the most damaging motivational sequence — shame without expectancy change without effort mobilization.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **Ability Vocabulary Is the Primary Motivational Toxin**
> The convergent evidence across attribution research suggests that ability-referencing vocabulary ("I'm not good at this," "I don't have the mind for this kind of material," "I'm just not a strong reader") is not merely inaccurate — it activates the most motivationally destructive attributional configuration simultaneously: internal, stable, and uncontrollable. Of all vocabulary choices available to a struggling reader, ability vocabulary is uniquely catastrophic because it forecloses all three motivational pathways: it assigns shame (internal), eliminates expectancy change (stable), and removes the basis for effort mobilization (uncontrollable). No other single vocabulary pattern does all three simultaneously.

### The Self-Efficacy Verbal Persuasion Research

The verbal persuasion source of self-efficacy — typically studied in the context of externally delivered feedback — has received less attention as an *internally delivered* mechanism, but the evidence that inner speech functions as self-administered verbal persuasion is consistent and theoretically well-grounded. Bandura's own framework implies that any verbal statement conveying capability information will update self-efficacy beliefs, regardless of source. The distinction between externally and internally delivered verbal persuasion is not categorical; it is a matter of credibility weighting, and internal sources carry substantial credibility precisely because they are not perceived as strategic.

> [!evidence] **The Credibility Premium of Internal Verbal Persuasion**
> Research on source credibility in persuasion establishes that perceived expertise and objectivity are the primary determinants of persuasive impact. Inner speech carries a credibility premium in the domain of self-assessment because we experience it as unmediated access to our own cognitive state, not as an attempt to influence us. This means that negative inner speech — "I'm struggling because I'm not intelligent enough for this material" — may have *higher* persuasive impact on self-efficacy than the same message delivered externally, because it is perceived as an objective self-report rather than a motivated evaluation. The implication is significant: correcting inner speech vocabulary matters more, not less, than correcting externally delivered feedback.

The relationship between [[self-efficacy]] and reading comprehension is well established in the reading research literature. High self-efficacy for reading predicts greater use of deep processing strategies, greater persistence through difficult text, greater willingness to engage critically rather than accept text surface features passively, and better calibration between perceived and actual comprehension. The causal pathway from self-efficacy to comprehension runs partly through strategy engagement and partly through goal quality — both of which, this analysis argues, are mediated by vocabulary.

### The SDT Regulatory Language Evidence

The most directly relevant evidence for the vocabulary-motivation connection comes from SDT research on regulatory language. Studies by Deci, Connell, Ryan and their collaborators established that the vocabulary used by teachers to communicate tasks (controlled vs. informational language) produces measurable differences in intrinsic motivation, deep processing, and conceptual understanding. Vansteenkiste and colleagues extended this to academic contexts, showing that frame-framing identical tasks with autonomous-regulatory language ("you might find it interesting to explore") versus controlled-regulatory language ("you must learn this") produced significantly different engagement depth and transfer quality.

The extension to inner speech is not a large theoretical leap: if the same words carry motivational force when delivered externally, the same words carry motivational force when delivered internally. Several studies on self-talk in sport psychology provide convergent evidence here — athletes instructed in autonomous regulatory self-talk language show performance and motivation advantages over those using controlled regulatory self-talk, with the effect size comparable to externally administered motivation interventions.

> [!tension-identified] **The Authenticity Problem in Regulatory Vocabulary**
> A genuine tension exists in the SDT evidence base here. SDT's internalization research suggests that the movement from controlled to autonomous regulation cannot be faked — it requires actual value alignment, not vocabulary substitution. Yet the regulatory language intervention research suggests that vocabulary shifts precede and partly cause the underlying motivational state change. The resolution may lie in [[internalization]] theory itself: the mechanism of internalization involves initially adopting the language of a regulatory style before that style becomes genuinely one's own — exactly as children adopt the language of social norms before those norms are fully internalized. This suggests a bootstrapping possibility: deliberate vocabulary practice with autonomous regulatory language can initiate the internalization process rather than merely describing it. But the tension remains active: purely performative vocabulary without engaged reflection may produce a kind of motivated reasoning about one's own motivation rather than genuine regulatory shift.

### The Achievement Goal Theory Evidence

[[achievement-goal-theory]]'s evidence base bears on vocabulary through the goal orientation construct. Mastery orientation (focused on learning, understanding, and personal improvement) and performance orientation (focused on demonstrating competence relative to others or to some standard) produce different reading behaviors, and these orientations are reliably activated by vocabulary cues. Elliot's experimental research demonstrated that priming performance-avoidance goal vocabulary before a reading task produced shallower processing and lower comprehension even when participants had equivalent prior knowledge. The vocabulary of goal orientation functions as an attention-allocation cue: it tells the cognitive system what counts as successful completion.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **Goal Vocabulary in Forethought Shapes Monitoring Sensitivity**
> The evidence from AGT research, taken together with the metacognitive monitoring literature, suggests a specific mechanism: mastery-oriented goal vocabulary in the [[forethought-phase]] sensitizes the monitoring system to *understanding cues* (noticing when you do not understand something, noticing when an inference does not follow), while performance-oriented goal vocabulary sensitizes the monitoring system to *completion cues* (noticing when you have finished the page, noticed that it took a long time). Monitoring sensitivity to understanding cues is, by definition, what [[metacognitive-monitoring]] is supposed to deliver — and the vocabulary choice in goal-setting appears to determine which cues the monitoring system is tuned to track.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Prediction Point**
> Before reading Phase IV: if the evidence base is correct that vocabulary shapes monitoring sensitivity, this implies something non-obvious about what happens during the *performance phase* (the actual reading) as a consequence of forethought vocabulary. What would you predict happens differently when a reader begins with mastery-oriented goal vocabulary versus completion-oriented goal vocabulary — not just in their goals, but in their real-time reading behavior?

*The evidence base established here converges on a single core claim: across three distinct theoretical traditions, with multiple independent research teams, the specific vocabulary used in self-referential speech about academic learning produces reliable, measurable, and practically significant differences in motivational and cognitive outcomes. The mechanisms are not fully understood, but the directional predictions are robust. What remains largely absent from this literature is a synthetic account of how these mechanisms interact specifically in the context of silent academic reading — which is what Phase IV provides.*

## Phase IV: Mechanisms, Dynamics & Deep Analysis

> [!important] **Complexity Transition**
> This phase moves from evidence summary to mechanism analysis — a significant step up in abstraction. It requires solid fluency with the Weiner dimensional model (locus/stability/controllability), Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy, and Zimmerman's three-phase SRL cycle (Forethought → Performance → Self-Reflection). If any of those feel uncertain, reviewing Phase II and the foundational reports on [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]], [[self-efficacy]], and [[Zimmerman's-Cyclical-SRL-Model]] will pay dividends before proceeding.

### Mechanism 1: The Attributional Vocabulary Loop

The first and most critical mechanism is what this analysis identifies as the **Attributional Vocabulary Loop** — a self-amplifying cycle in which vocabulary in the [[self-reflection-phase]] generates attributional inferences, which shape goal vocabulary in the subsequent [[forethought-phase]], which structures monitoring and interpretation during the performance phase, which produces outcome experiences that feed back into the same reflective vocabulary.

The loop works as follows. During the self-reflection phase, a reader uses some vocabulary to explain why they did or did not understand something: "I'm just not smart enough for this material" (ability vocabulary) or "I need a better approach to this kind of argument" (strategy vocabulary). The vocabulary chosen encodes dimensional commitments: ability vocabulary encodes internal-stable-uncontrollable; strategy vocabulary encodes internal-unstable-controllable. These dimensional commitments then propagate forward: stable attributions reduce expectancy for future success (no point setting an ambitious goal), and uncontrollable attributions reduce the basis for effort-based goal setting (no point planning specific strategies). The goal vocabulary in the next forethought phase consequently becomes either absent or vague ("I'll try again") rather than specific and strategy-focused ("I'll approach the argument structurally, mapping the premises before evaluating them"). Vague goal vocabulary produces coarser monitoring during reading — the reader is checking less frequently whether they understand, because the goal is not well enough defined to generate specific comprehension checkpoints. Coarser monitoring produces less effective error detection and repair. Reduced comprehension follows, which then "confirms" the original ability attribution in the next reflective cycle.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Vocabulary Loop is Self-Sealing**
> The most consequential property of the Attributional Vocabulary Loop is not that it produces bad outcomes in a single cycle — it is that it becomes increasingly self-sealing over repeated cycles. Each turn of the loop narrows the vocabulary available to the reader by providing "evidence" for the attributed cause: the reader who uses ability vocabulary and subsequently reads less effectively experiences this as confirmation that they indeed lack ability. Over time, the vocabulary repertoire for self-explanation contracts toward the attributed cause, because alternatives are systematically disconfirmed by experience. This means early vocabulary intervention is dramatically more efficient than late intervention — the loop compounds in the destructive direction just as surely as a growth mindset compounds in the constructive direction.

The loop can also run in the constructive direction. Strategy vocabulary in self-reflection generates unstable-controllable attributions, which preserve expectancy and motivate specific goal setting, which produces precise monitoring, which enables error detection and repair, which produces better comprehension, which provides evidence for the effectiveness of strategic approaches. Over multiple reading sessions, the vocabulary repertoire expands — not randomly, but in the direction that the loop has been running.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **The Compounding Interest Analogy: Attribution Vocabulary → Financial Returns**
> The Attributional Vocabulary Loop has a precise structural analogue in the mathematics of compound interest. The vocabulary "rate" (how growth-enabling or stagnation-enabling the attribution vocabulary is) determines not just a single outcome but the trajectory of a compounding function. A vocabulary "portfolio" marginally shifted toward strategy, effort, and approach attributions rather than ability attributions does not just produce marginally better outcomes — it produces outcomes that compound over time, because each constructive cycle expands the vocabulary available for the next cycle. The lesson from compound interest is the same as the lesson here: small rate differences produce dramatically different long-term outcomes, and the time-value of early change is enormous. This is why vocabulary development is not a cosmetic adjustment but a structural investment.

### Mechanism 2: Self-Efficacy Calibration Through Inner Speech

[[self-efficacy]] beliefs are not stored as discrete items updated only after significant outcomes — they are continuously calibrated through real-time self-commentary. During academic reading, this continuous calibration happens at multiple timescales: sentence-by-sentence ("I followed that"), paragraph-by-paragraph ("I'm losing the thread here"), section-by-section ("I understood the first argument but missed the second"), and whole-document ("That was above my current level").

The vocabulary deployed at each calibration point either contributes to accurate, modifiable self-efficacy beliefs or to inaccurate, fixed ones. The critical distinction is between *performance-referencing vocabulary* ("I understood that paragraph") and *ability-referencing vocabulary* ("I'm good at this kind of reading"). Performance-referencing vocabulary maintains what might be called *efficacy flexibility* — the belief that efficacy is responsive to performance, and that performance is responsive to strategy and effort. Ability-referencing vocabulary — even when positive — gradually hardens efficacy into a trait attribute that is no longer experienced as modifiable.

> [!analytical-insight] **Positive Ability Vocabulary Is a Motivational Liability in Disguise**
> One of the less obvious implications of the self-efficacy calibration mechanism is that positive ability-referencing inner speech ("I'm naturally good at this") is motivationally problematic in a different way than negative ability-referencing speech, but in the same structural family. Both encode efficacy as stable and uncontrollable. Positive ability vocabulary produces two specific failure modes: it reduces the probability of strategic effort allocation (why invest in strategy if you're naturally gifted?), and it creates fragility — when difficulty eventually arrives, as it always does in academic learning, there is no strategy-and-effort vocabulary available to interpret the experience except as evidence that the ability attribution was wrong. Dweck's research on [[growth-mindset]] demonstrates exactly this dynamic: children praised for ability (positive ability vocabulary) subsequently chose easier tasks, showed more fragility under challenge, and performed worse than children praised for effort (strategy-process vocabulary). The vocabulary of praise — delivered externally in Dweck's studies but self-delivered in real-world reading — determines whether efficacy is flexible or brittle.

The vocabulary of physiological and affective states is also implicated here. Bandura's fourth source of self-efficacy — physiological and affective cues — is subject to interpretive vocabulary. The physical experience of cognitive effort (the sense of strain, of working hard to understand) is motivationally ambiguous: it can be interpreted as evidence of incompetence ("this is painful because I'm not equipped for it") or as evidence of productive learning ("this feels effortful because I'm encountering genuinely new material"). The vocabulary available for interpreting cognitive strain determines which interpretation is activated.

### Mechanism 3: Goal Construction and Vocabulary Quality

[[goal-setting-theory]] (Locke & Latham) establishes that goal effectiveness depends on specificity and challenge level — vague, easy goals produce less effort and worse performance than specific, challenging goals. What the goal-setting literature has not sufficiently examined is the role of vocabulary in determining the specificity and challenge level of goals that learners actually construct.

In the context of academic reading, goal vocabulary operates hierarchically. At the superordinate level, there are goals about why one is reading at all — the SDT-relevant regulatory vocabulary ("I'm reading this because I have to" vs. "I'm reading this because I want to understand X"). At the intermediate level, there are comprehension goals — what counts as successful understanding of this text ("I want to be able to explain the main argument" vs. "I want to get through this section"). At the subordinate level, there are strategic process goals — how one will engage with specific passages, arguments, or unfamiliar concepts.

> [!analytical-insight] **Hierarchical Goal Vocabulary and the Cascade Problem**
> Vocabulary deficits at the superordinate level of goal construction cascade downward through the hierarchy. A reader who can only articulate superordinate goals in controlled regulatory vocabulary ("I should understand this") lacks the motivational architecture to construct specific and challenging intermediate goals, because the energy driving goal construction is controlled rather than autonomous. Autonomous regulatory vocabulary at the superordinate level — "I'm genuinely trying to understand how this argument connects to what I already know about this domain" — generates a motivational pressure toward more specific and more challenging intermediate goals. The cascade implication is that vocabulary development at the superordinate level (the regulatory register) has multiplicative effects on goal quality throughout the hierarchy.

### Mechanism 4: The Cross-Phase Vocabulary Cascade in the SRL Cycle

[[Zimmerman's-Cyclical-SRL-Model]] describes a three-phase cycle: Forethought → Performance → Self-Reflection. Each phase has characteristic cognitive and motivational processes, and each generates content that feeds into the next phase. The vocabulary analysis reveals a previously under-theorized mechanism: the vocabulary deployed in each phase becomes the lexical input for the next phase's processes.

In the [[forethought-phase]], goal-setting vocabulary and strategic planning vocabulary determine what the monitoring system will be tracking. In the performance phase, monitoring vocabulary (the words used to notice and label comprehension states) determines what gets flagged for repair. In the [[self-reflection-phase]], attributional vocabulary determines what causal explanation is constructed for the performance experience, which then seeds the next cycle's forethought vocabulary. The cascade means that a vocabulary deficit in any phase propagates through the entire cycle — and that a vocabulary intervention in any phase has effects throughout the cycle.

> [!what-the-evidence-suggests] **Reflection Phase Vocabulary Is the Highest-Leverage Intervention Point**
> The cross-phase cascade analysis suggests that the self-reflection phase — where attributional vocabulary determines the causal explanation that seeds the next forethought cycle — is the highest-leverage point for vocabulary intervention. This is because reflection vocabulary affects both the retrospective interpretation (efficacy updating for the current session) and the prospective seed (goal vocabulary available for the next session). An intervention targeting reflection-phase attributional vocabulary would therefore have double the reach of an equivalent intervention targeting performance-phase monitoring vocabulary. This points toward a specific practical recommendation: developing a rich, dimensionally precise attributional vocabulary for the self-reflection phase should be the primary vocabulary development priority.

*The four mechanisms form an integrated architecture. The Attributional Vocabulary Loop establishes the self-amplifying nature of vocabulary effects across multiple reading sessions. The Self-Efficacy Calibration Mechanism shows how vocabulary operates in real-time within a single reading session. ## Phase V: Implications, Applications & Practical Tools

### The Practical Architecture

The mechanisms analyzed in Phase IV converge on a clear practical implication: vocabulary development in each motivational register is a trainable metacognitive skill with measurable benefits. This phase translates that implication into specific tools.

> [!best-practice] **Tool 1: The Attribution Reframing Vocabulary Table**
> The following vocabulary table provides direct attributional substitutes along the stability and controllability dimensions — the two dimensions most consequential for motivational outcomes (expectancy and effort mobilization, respectively).
>
> | Destructive Pattern | Dimension Encoded | Constructive Substitute | Dimension Encoded |
> |--------------------|--------------------|------------------------|-------------------|
> | "I can't do this" | Internal, stable, uncontrollable | "I don't have the right approach yet" | Internal, unstable, controllable |
> | "I'm just not good at this kind of reading" | Internal, stable, uncontrollable | "My strategy for this text type needs development" | Internal, unstable, controllable |
> | "This is too hard for me" | Internal, stable, uncontrollable | "This is at the edge of my current comprehension — exactly where growth happens" | Internal, unstable, controllable |
> | "I don't have the background for this" | External/Internal, stable, uncontrollable | "I need to develop specific background — here's what's missing" | Internal, unstable, controllable |
> | "I understand this easily" (ability praise) | Internal, stable, uncontrollable | "My current approach is working well for this material" | Internal, unstable, controllable |
>
> Note on the table: the constructive substitutes are not merely positive reframings — they are dimensional reassignments. The critical move is always toward unstable and controllable, not simply toward internal or external.

> [!best-practice] **Tool 2: SDT Regulatory Vocabulary Upgrade Chart**
> This chart translates controlled regulatory inner speech into progressively autonomous alternatives, following the internalization continuum.
>
> | Controlled Register | Introjected Register | Identified Register | Integrated Register |
> |--------------------|---------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
> | "I have to read this" | "I'll feel guilty if I don't" | "I'm reading this because it matters for my goal" | "I'm reading this because I genuinely want to understand this domain" |
> | "I should focus" | "I'd be embarrassed to not understand this" | "Focusing here serves my real objectives" | "Deep engagement with this is exactly what I'm committed to" |
> | "I must finish this section" | "I'll be disappointed in myself if I don't" | "Finishing this section advances what I'm actually trying to accomplish" | "Working through this fully is consistent with who I am as a learner" |
>
> Practice instruction: Begin by identifying your natural regulatory register for different reading contexts. The target is not to jump immediately to integrated regulation — progression through identified regulation is a more realistic and sustainable pathway. The vocabulary shift from introjected to identified ("because I'll feel guilty" → "because it serves my goal") is often the most consequential single step.

> [!best-practice] **Tool 3: The Five Questions Reading Protocol**
> A structured inner speech template for use at the beginning, middle, and end of any significant reading session, designed to engage all three motivational registers.
>
> **Before reading (Forethought Phase):**
> 1. *Goal vocabulary*: "What specifically do I want to understand from this reading?" (Mastery orientation; specific and challenging)
> 2. *Regulatory vocabulary*: "Why does this matter to me personally?" (Autonomous regulation activation)
>
> **During reading (Performance Phase):**
> 3. *Monitoring vocabulary*: "Am I following the argument / understanding the concept / or do I need to re-engage?" (Comprehension-cue monitoring, not completion-cue monitoring)
>
> **After reading (Self-Reflection Phase):**
> 4. *Attributional vocabulary*: "What explains my understanding or confusion — specifically, what approach, strategy, or background knowledge was relevant here?" (Strategy/approach attribution; avoid ability vocabulary)
> 5. *Forward vocabulary*: "What specific next step follows from this reading?" (Seeds the next forethought phase with strategy vocabulary)

> [!best-practice] **Tool 4: Goal Construction Vocabulary Template for Hierarchical Goals**
> The following template structures goal vocabulary at three hierarchical levels, integrating SDT regulatory language at the superordinate level and AGT mastery orientation at the intermediate level.
>
> **Superordinate Goal (Why am I engaging with this domain?):**
> "I am developing understanding of [domain] because [personally meaningful reason connected to genuine values or interests]."
> *Vocabulary principle: autonomous regulation language; first-person ownership; values-connection*
>
> **Intermediate Goal (What do I want from this reading session?):**
> "By the end of this session, I want to be able to [specific generative capability: explain, apply, critique, connect, evaluate] [specific content]."
> *Vocabulary principle: mastery orientation; specific and generative (not "understand" — "explain," "apply," "critique")*
>
> **Subordinate Goal (How will I engage with this text?):**
> "For [specific section/argument/concept], I will [specific strategy: map the argument structure, identify the key claims and evidence, locate the tension with what I already know, construct a counterargument]."
> *Vocabulary principle: strategy vocabulary; process-focused; specific enough to generate monitoring checkpoints*
>
> Note: The vocabulary "understand" is specifically flagged as insufficient for intermediate goal setting, because it fails to generate a monitoring checkpoint — there is no moment when you know whether you "understand" something, whereas there is a clear moment when you can or cannot "explain it in your own words" or "identify the key claims."

> [!warning] **The Vocabulary Substitution Trap**
> Mechanical vocabulary substitution without genuine engagement produces exactly what the SDT authenticity tension warned against: a kind of motivated self-deception that temporarily elevates mood without changing underlying motivational architecture. The tools above work when used as invitations to genuine reflection, not as scripts to perform. The criterion for genuine use: the vocabulary should surface something true that you did not previously have words for — not dress up a state you're aware of in better clothes.

### Limitations of the Vocabulary Approach

Three significant limitations bound this analysis. First, working memory constraints are real: constructing elaborate inner speech commentary during reading requires cognitive resources that compete with comprehension itself. The tools above are designed to be brief precisely because of this constraint — but the competition is real, and the practical recommendation is to develop vocabulary prompts *before* reading (in the forethought phase) and *after* reading (in the reflection phase) rather than during, until the vocabulary becomes sufficiently automatized to require minimal working memory overhead. Second, the evidence base is largely derived from educational psychology laboratory settings with student populations — generalizability to self-directed adult learning of complex academic texts requires careful application. Third, the vocabulary-motivation relationship is likely bidirectional and not simply unidirectional: motivational states also shape vocabulary availability, creating the possibility that low motivation periods produce vocabulary contraction that then deepens low motivation. This bidirectionality is exactly why early intervention matters more than later intervention.

*The practical tools above are grounded in the mechanism analysis of Phase IV: Tool 1 targets Mechanism 1 (Attributional Vocabulary Loop) specifically in the reflection phase. Tool 2 targets Mechanism 3 (Goal Construction) at the superordinate regulatory level. Tool 3 targets the Cross-Phase Cascade (Mechanism 4) by providing vocabulary scaffolding across all three SRL phases. Tool 4 targets Mechanism 3 at all three hierarchical levels. Together they constitute a coordinated vocabulary development system, not a collection of independent tips.*

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

The four mechanisms analyzed in Phase IV, integrated with the evidence of Phase III and the practical architecture of Phase V, converge on a synthesis that this analysis articulates as the **Motivational Lexicon** — a concept that names something real but previously unnamed in the self-regulated learning literature.

### The Motivational Lexicon

Every domain of expertise has a domain vocabulary — the technical terms, distinctions, and conceptual structures that enable experts to perceive, think, and communicate in ways unavailable to novices. Expert reading comprehension, critical thinking, and argumentation have their domain vocabularies: propositional structure, inferential coherence, claim-evidence relationships, implicit assumptions. The [[Vocabulary-Acquisition-for-Reading-Automaticity]] literature addresses this domain vocabulary.

What has not been adequately theorized is that self-regulated learning also has a domain vocabulary — a vocabulary not for understanding the text's content, but for understanding and directing one's own motivational and cognitive engagement with the text. This is the **Motivational Lexicon**: the structured vocabulary repertoire that constitutes the inner speech architecture of advanced self-regulated learning.

> [!original-synthesis] **The Motivational Lexicon: Inner Speech as Cognitive-Motivational Infrastructure**
> The Motivational Lexicon is the set of vocabulary items a learner has available for narrating, evaluating, directing, and reconstructing their own motivational and cognitive experience during academic learning. It differs from general emotional vocabulary (which describes feeling states) and from metacognitive strategy vocabulary (which names cognitive operations) by being specifically concerned with the attributional, regulatory, and goal-construction registers that determine motivational dynamics. A rich Motivational Lexicon enables what might be called *motivational metacognition* — the capacity to not just monitor cognitive states but to monitor and deliberately intervene in motivational states through the vocabulary used to narrate them. The claim this synthesis makes is that the Motivational Lexicon is as much a cognitive resource as working memory capacity or prior domain knowledge — it enables motivational regulation that is impossible without it, in the same way that scientific vocabulary enables scientific reasoning that is impossible without it. Developing this lexicon is therefore not a soft motivational supplement to "real" cognitive skill development; it is a form of cognitive skill development with equal standing.

This synthesis has several important implications. It reframes vocabulary development as a metacognitive skill, not a remediation activity. It implies that the gap between effective and ineffective self-regulated learners may be partly — perhaps substantially — a vocabulary gap that is systematically addressable. It suggests that the reports in this knowledge base, by providing technical vocabulary for motivational constructs (attribution dimensions, regulatory styles, goal orientations), are not just building declarative knowledge of psychology — they are building Motivational Lexicon resources that can be deployed in inner speech.

### The Central Question Revisited

How does the vocabulary deployed in inner speech during silent academic reading shape attributional patterns, self-efficacy beliefs, goal construction quality, and the depth of self-regulated learning?

The answer this analysis has built: through four interlocking mechanisms that form a coherent architecture — the Attributional Vocabulary Loop, the Self-Efficacy Calibration Mechanism, the Goal Construction Cascade, and the Cross-Phase Vocabulary Cascade. These mechanisms operate simultaneously and interact: goal vocabulary in forethought shapes monitoring sensitivity in performance, which shapes the quality of outcome experiences available for attribution in reflection, which shapes the attributional vocabulary that seeds the next forethought cycle. The vocabulary deployed at any point in this system reverberates through the entire cycle and across multiple sessions. This is why vocabulary development is not a single intervention but a sustained practice — it is building an architecture, not installing a feature.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Learning-Through-Report-Reading Loop**
> There is a specific and underappreciated way that the reports in this knowledge base function as Motivational Lexicon development tools. When you read a report on [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]] that gives you precise vocabulary for the three dimensions — locus, stability, controllability — and the vocabulary to distinguish "effort" attributions from "strategy" attributions from "ability" attributions, you are not only acquiring declarative knowledge of attribution theory. You are acquiring new vocabulary items for your Motivational Lexicon. The next time you finish a difficult reading session, "I didn't have the right strategy for that kind of argument" is now available where previously only "I didn't understand it" was. The reports are building the very inner speech vocabulary that enables better engagement with subsequent reports. This creates a genuine positive feedback loop between PKB development and SRL capability — one that compounds with each new conceptual node added to the knowledge base.

### Unresolved Questions

Three open questions mark the frontier of this analysis. First, the *automaticity threshold*: at what point does deliberate vocabulary practice become internalized to the level where it operates without conscious intention? The evidence suggests the threshold exists but provides little guidance on the timeline or conditions. Second, the *individual difference problem*: the evidence base largely treats vocabulary effects as homogeneous across learners, but it is plausible that learners with greater linguistic working memory capacity, richer prior emotional vocabulary, or different cultural relationship to self-analysis will show meaningfully different response patterns. Third, the *content-motivation interface*: the analysis has treated motivational inner speech and content-processing inner speech as distinct; a more complete theory would address how they interact when the content being processed is itself motivationally relevant (as when reading about motivation theory with the intent of applying it).

*The synthesis of the Motivational Lexicon names something that was already operating in effective self-regulated learners, but operating implicitly. Making it explicit — giving it a name, a structure, and a development pathway — is the specific contribution of this analysis. The reports in this knowledge base have been building the content vocabulary of motivation science; this analysis argues that building the motivational vocabulary of self-regulated reading is an equally important and closely related project.*

### Abstract Principle 1: Vocabulary Encodes Modifiability

The analysis revealed a concrete finding: in academic reading, ability vocabulary encodes causes as fixed and uncontrollable, while strategy vocabulary encodes causes as malleable and controllable. At an abstract structural level, this instantiates a domain-independent principle: **the vocabulary used to name a cause encodes a claim about that cause's modifiability**. This principle operates whenever agents explain outcomes to themselves or others, and wherever those explanations carry consequences for future effort allocation.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Academic Reading → Clinical Therapeutic Dialogue**
> This structural principle appears with striking clarity in the therapeutic effectiveness literature. The transition from problem-saturated descriptions ("I am an anxious person") to process-descriptions ("I experience anxiety in these specific contexts") is a core mechanism in narrative therapy and cognitive-behavioral frameworks. Both encode the same structural shift: from trait-stable-uncontrollable to contextual-unstable-potentially-controllable. The [[Attribution-Retraining-Interventions]] evidence and the cognitive-behavioral evidence are structural analogues — different domains, same dimensional reassignment principle. The transfer insight: any domain where self-explanation language is used (therapy, coaching, performance review, peer feedback) is subject to the same vocabulary-modifiability principle.

**Transfer Encoding**: When you encounter language that names causes in terms of *what someone is* rather than *what they did or how they approached it*, this is the trigger pattern. The diagnostic question: "Does this vocabulary close down or open up the space for modification?" If it closes down (it names a fixed property), the attribution is in the destructive configuration. The transferable prediction: language that opens up modification space will be associated with greater persistence and improvement trajectories, regardless of domain.

### Abstract Principle 2: Regulatory Language Is Motivational Architecture

The SDT finding that controlled versus autonomous regulatory vocabulary produces different motivational states is, at an abstract level, an instance of a general principle: **the vocabulary in which you frame your relationship to an activity partially instantiates your motivational relationship to that activity**. This principle operates wherever agents describe their reasons for engaging in activities, particularly activities that involve sustained effort.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Academic Reading → Deliberate Practice and Habit Formation**
> The [[goal-intention]] literature (Gollwitzer) and the habit formation literature ([[habit-formation]]) both find that the specific linguistic framing of behavior commitments predicts follow-through. "If I am in situation X, I will do Y" dramatically outperforms "I will try to do Y." The structural analogy to SDT regulatory language: implementation intentions work partly by converting vague autonomous motivation into a specific linguistic structure that does not require motivational retrieval at the moment of action. The two bodies of research converge on the same structural insight: specific, autonomous, first-person regulatory language creates behavioral commitment structures that vaguer or more controlled language cannot.

**Application Bridge**: In your SRL practice, whenever you are constructing goals or commitments for reading sessions, the vocabulary template should be: "When [specific trigger: I open this text / I begin this report], I will [specific action: read to the end of the first argument and pause to identify its structure] because [genuine personal reason]." This is the implementation intention format + autonomous regulatory language — a structural combination that integrates both bodies of evidence.

### Abstract Principle 3: Metacognitive Vocabulary Is a Metacognitive Resource

The analysis found that having technical vocabulary for attribution dimensions, regulatory styles, and goal orientations enables motivational self-regulation that is literally impossible without that vocabulary. At an abstract structural level: **vocabulary for cognitive and motivational states is itself a cognitive resource, not just a labeling system**. This principle operates in any domain where self-monitoring and self-regulation of internal states is advantageous.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Motivational Vocabulary → Emotional Intelligence Frameworks**
> The [[emotional-intelligence]] research tradition (Mayer, Salovey; Goleman) finds that emotional vocabulary richness (the number of distinct terms available for emotional states) predicts both the accuracy of emotional perception and the effectiveness of emotion regulation. The structural analogy is exact: in the emotional domain, vocabulary richness enables regulation precision; in the motivational domain, vocabulary richness enables regulatory precision. The same structural principle — vocabulary as resource, not just label — operates across both domains. The practical implication is the same in both: vocabulary development is regulation development.

**Meta-Transfer Reflection**: These three principles share a deeper structural commonality. In each case, vocabulary functions not as a passive description of pre-existing states but as an active cognitive-motivational structure that shapes what can be perceived, distinguished, and acted upon. This reflects a broader principle at work throughout cognitive science: the categories available in any representational system constrain what can be represented and therefore what can be regulated. Building vocabulary is building representational capacity, which is building regulatory capacity.

## Phase IX: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Motivational Lexicon** (Original Synthesis — this report)**
> **Definition:** The structured vocabulary repertoire that constitutes a learner's inner speech architecture for self-regulated academic engagement — specifically the vocabulary available for attributional narration, regulatory self-direction, self-efficacy evaluation, and goal construction. Distinct from domain vocabulary (vocabulary for content understanding) and strategy vocabulary (vocabulary for cognitive operations). The Motivational Lexicon is the set of words available for narrating, evaluating, and directing the motivational experience of learning itself.

> [!definition] **Attributional Vocabulary Loop** (Original Synthesis — this report)

> [!definition] **Regulatory Self-Talk Register** (SDT-derived)
> **Definition:** The portion of inner speech that carries regulatory content — the "why am I doing this" and "how am I directing this effort" layer of motivational inner speech. > [!definition] **Self-Efficacy Calibration** (Bandura-derived, extended)

> [!definition] **Goal Construction Vocabulary** (AGT/Goal-Setting-derived)
> **Definition:** The vocabulary used to construct and articulate goals across the three hierarchical levels of SRL goal architecture: superordinate (why am I engaging with this domain — regulatory vocabulary), intermediate (what do I want from this reading — mastery orientation vocabulary), and subordinate (how will I engage with this passage — strategy vocabulary). The quality of goal vocabulary at each level cascades downward through the hierarchy, with superordinate regulatory vocabulary being the highest-leverage point for vocabulary intervention.

> [!definition] **Attributional Vocabulary Dimensions** (Weiner)
> **Definition:** The three dimensions along which causal attributions vary and to which vocabulary items commit: *locus* (internal vs. external — whether the cause is within or outside the learner), *stability* (stable vs. unstable — whether the cause persists over time), and *controllability* (controllable vs. uncontrollable — whether the learner can modify the cause). Vocabulary pre-loads dimensional commitments: ability vocabulary encodes internal-stable-uncontrollable; strategy vocabulary encodes internal-unstable-controllable.
>
> **Cross-References:** [[Attribution (Heider, 1958)]], [[bernard-weiner]], [[attribution-style]], [[attribution-retraining]]

> [!definition] **Monitoring Vocabulary Sensitization**

Mastery-oriented goal vocabulary sensitizes monitoring to understanding cues (noticing non-comprehension, noticing inferential gaps); performance/completion-oriented vocabulary sensitizes monitoring to completion cues (progress through the text, time elapsed). The type of cue the monitoring system tracks determines what gets flagged for repair and what gets passed over silently.

> **Cross-References:** [[achievement-goal-theory]], [[metacognitive-monitoring]], [[metacognitive-regulation]], [[situation-model]]

> [!definition] **Cross-Phase Vocabulary Cascade**
> **Definition:** The mechanism by which the vocabulary deployed in one phase of Zimmerman's SRL cycle becomes the lexical input for the next phase's characteristic processes. Vocabulary in reflection seeds vocabulary available in forethought; vocabulary in forethought shapes vocabulary available for monitoring; monitoring vocabulary determines the vocabulary of outcome interpretation. The cascade means that vocabulary deficits in any phase propagate through the entire cycle, and vocabulary interventions in any phase have effects throughout the cycle.

### C. Conceptual Tensions & Open Questions

> [!tension] **Vocabulary Authenticity vs. Vocabulary Practice: Can You Bootstrap Autonomous Motivation?**
> **Position A (SDT orthodoxy):** Authentic autonomous motivation cannot be manufactured through vocabulary substitution. The move from introjected to identified regulation requires genuine value alignment — not performative language change. Changing vocabulary without changing underlying values produces self-deception, not internalization.
>
> **Position B (Internalization process view):** The internalization process itself involves initially adopting the regulatory language of a more autonomous style before that style is genuinely one's own — exactly as children adopt the language of social norms before full internalization. Vocabulary practice can initiate the internalization process rather than merely describing it.
>
> **Current Evidence State:** The evidence supports a conditional version of Position B: vocabulary practice produces real motivational change when accompanied by genuine reflection on personal values connection, but not when performed mechanically. The threshold between genuine and performative vocabulary practice is not clearly operationalized in the research.
>
> **Why This Matters:** The practical tools in Phase V are designed on Position B assumptions. If Position A's stronger claim is correct, they will produce a kind of motivated self-deception rather than genuine motivational development.
>
> **This Report's Stance:** The analysis accepts the conditional Position B — vocabulary practice as an initiator of internalization rather than a substitute for it — while flagging the authenticity tension as a genuine practical risk requiring reflective engagement, not mechanical vocabulary substitution.

> [!open-question] **The Automaticity Threshold: When Does Vocabulary Become Infrastructure?**
> At what point does deliberate vocabulary practice become sufficiently internalized that it operates without conscious intention during reading — functioning as genuine motivational infrastructure rather than a metacognitive tax on working memory? The evidence suggests the threshold exists, but neither the timeline nor the conditions for reaching it are well-characterized. This is the most practically consequential open question for implementation of the Motivational Lexicon framework.

### G. Practical Application Protocols

> [!protocol] **The Motivational Vocabulary Session Protocol**
> **Context:** Use at the beginning and end of each significant reading session to build Motivational Lexicon through deliberate vocabulary practice.
>
> **Before Reading (5 minutes):**
> 1. *Superordinate Goal*: Write one sentence connecting this reading to a genuine personal value or interest ("I'm reading this because..."). Avoid obligation language.
> 2. *Intermediate Goal*: Write what specific capability you want to have after reading ("By the end, I want to be able to..."). Use a generative verb (explain, apply, critique, connect — not "understand").
> 3. *Subordinate Goal*: Identify the one most challenging section and write what specific strategy you will apply to it.
>
> **After Reading (5 minutes):**
> 4. *Monitoring review*: What did you notice about your comprehension during reading? Describe in terms of specific passages and specific gaps, not overall impressions.
> 5. *Attribution vocabulary*: Complete this sentence: "Where I struggled, the most accurate explanation is..." — use strategy, approach, or background vocabulary; consciously avoid ability vocabulary.
> 6. *Forward vocabulary*: Write one specific next step that follows from today's reading.
>
> **Success Criteria:** The protocol is working when the before-reading goal vocabulary becomes increasingly specific and challenging, and the after-reading attribution vocabulary becomes increasingly nuanced and strategy-focused.
>
> **Common Pitfall:** The most common failure mode is mechanical completion — writing something in each slot without genuine reflection. The criterion for genuine completion: each response should surface something true that you did not previously have words for.
