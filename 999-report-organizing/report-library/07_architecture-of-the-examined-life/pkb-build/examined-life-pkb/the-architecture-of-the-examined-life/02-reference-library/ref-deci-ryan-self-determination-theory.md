# 📚 Deci & Ryan: Self-Determination Theory

> [!abstract] Quick Reference
> **Authors:** Edward L. Deci & Richard M. Ryan — psychologists at the University of Rochester who developed Self-Determination Theory over four decades of research on human motivation, personality development, and well-being
> **Work:** "Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being" (2000) — comprehensive theoretical statement published in *American Psychologist* synthesizing SDT's framework and empirical support
> **Domain:** Psychology, Motivation Theory, Personality Development
> **Accessibility:** Academic — written for academic psychologists; assumes familiarity with motivation research and empirical methodology
> **Cited in:** [[report-03-the-motivation-architecture|Report 03]], [[report-08-the-examined-life-in-practice|Report 08]], [[report-09-the-autonomy-paradox|Report 09]], [[report-10-the-social-turn|Report 10]], [[report-12-character-and-habituation|Report 12]]

## What This Work Contributes

**Self-Determination Theory (SDT)** is a comprehensive framework for understanding human motivation, personality development, and psychological well-being. The theory's central claim is that humans have three **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Basic Psychological Needs|basic psychological needs]]** whose satisfaction is essential for flourishing: **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Autonomy|autonomy]]** (experiencing behavior as self-endorsed and volitional), **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Competence|competence]]** (experiencing effectiveness in producing desired outcomes), and **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Relatedness|relatedness]]** (experiencing meaningful connection with others).

SDT distinguishes **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Intrinsic Motivation|intrinsic motivation]]** (engaging in activity for its inherent satisfaction) from **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Extrinsic Motivation|extrinsic motivation]]** (engaging in activity as means to separable outcome). Critically, extrinsic motivation is **not monolithic** — SDT proposes a continuum of **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Internalization|internalization]]** along which externally regulated behaviors can become increasingly self-determined through integration with one's values and sense of self.

The internalization continuum ranges from **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#External Regulation|external regulation]]** (behavior controlled by rewards/punishments) through **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Introjected Regulation|introjected regulation]]** (behavior driven by internal pressure, guilt, or ego-involvement) and **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Identified Regulation|identified regulation]]** (behavior aligned with personal values) to **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Integrated Regulation|integrated regulation]]** (behavior fully assimilated into coherent sense of self). Even fully integrated extrinsic motivation remains distinct from intrinsic motivation — the former is valued *instrumentally* (as means to valued end), while the latter is valued *for itself*.

A critical SDT finding is that **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Autonomy Support|autonomy-supportive]]** social contexts (which acknowledge perspectives, provide choice, and minimize controlling language) facilitate both intrinsic motivation and internalization of extrinsic motivation, while **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Controlling Contexts|controlling contexts]]** (which pressure compliance through rewards, punishments, or surveillance) undermine autonomous motivation and well-being, even when they successfully produce behavioral compliance.

The theory distinguishes **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Autonomous Motivation|autonomous motivation]]** (intrinsic motivation plus well-internalized extrinsic motivation) from **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Controlled Motivation|controlled motivation]]** (external regulation plus introjected regulation). This distinction predicts important outcomes: autonomous motivation is associated with persistence, creativity, deep learning, psychological well-being, and performance quality, while controlled motivation produces compliance, surface learning, reduced creativity, and psychological costs even when it achieves behavioral endpoints.

SDT introduces **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Organismic Integration Theory|Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)]]** as a sub-theory explaining how people internalize and integrate social values and regulations. OIT predicts that internalization occurs when social contexts support the three basic needs — autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these needs are satisfied, people naturally move toward integration; when thwarted, development arrests at less autonomous regulatory styles.

## Role in the Examined Life Series

Self-Determination Theory provides the **motivational architecture** for understanding why people engage (or fail to engage) in the effortful practice of self-examination, appearing in five reports and establishing that the examined life requires autonomy-supportive conditions and intrinsic or well-integrated motivation.

### Report 03: The Motivation Architecture
This report positions SDT as the primary framework for understanding **what motivates the examined life**. The examined life cannot be sustained through external reward/punishment (external regulation) or guilt/shame (introjected regulation) without psychological cost. It requires either intrinsic motivation (finding inquiry inherently satisfying) or well-internalized extrinsic motivation (recognizing inquiry as essential to living well and integrating this value into identity).

The synthesis move: Socrates's *elenchus* can undermine introjected pseudo-virtue (behavior motivated by social approval or guilt) while facilitating movement toward integrated regulation (behavior aligned with examined understanding of what's worth caring about). The examined life, properly understood, is **autonomously motivated** — people examine their lives because they genuinely value doing so, not because external pressure demands it.

### Report 08: The Examined Life in Practice
SDT's autonomy-support research provides **practical guidance** for creating conditions that facilitate the examined life. Educational environments, workplaces, and social relationships can be structured to support the three basic needs, making self-examination more likely to emerge and persist. The report synthesizes SDT with **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Scaffolding|scaffolding]]** theory ([[ref-vygotsky-mind-in-society|Vygotsky]]) to show how autonomy-supportive scaffolding enables development of autonomous motivation.

The practical synthesis: Building a sustainable examined life requires (1) autonomy-supportive social contexts that provide choice and acknowledge perspective, (2) competence-supportive scaffolding that enables mastery of inquiry practices, (3) relatedness-supportive communities that value thoughtful reflection, and (4) conditions facilitating internalization so examination becomes integrated into identity.

### Report 09: The Autonomy Paradox
This report addresses a tension in SDT: If autonomy means self-determination, how can we *encourage* someone to be autonomous without being controlling? The report synthesizes SDT's autonomy-support research with **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Libertarian Paternalism|libertarian paternalism]]** and **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Choice Architecture|choice architecture]]** to show how environments can be structured to facilitate autonomy without controlling it.

The synthesis: Autonomy support is not non-intervention (leaving people alone) but **active scaffolding of self-determination** — providing information, acknowledging feelings, offering choices, and explaining rationales while minimizing controlling language and pressure. This is how Socrates operates in the *Euthyphro* — he doesn't tell Euthyphro what to think but creates conditions facilitating Euthyphro's autonomous reasoning.

### Report 10: The Social Turn
SDT's emphasis on **relatedness** as a basic psychological need reinforces that **autonomy is not independence**. People develop and express autonomy within social contexts of meaningful connection. The examined life, from this perspective, is not solitary introspection but socially embedded autonomous inquiry — people examine their lives in dialogue with others who support their self-determination.

The synthesis integrates SDT with Vygotsky's sociocultural theory to show that autonomous motivation develops through internalization of social practices in autonomy-supportive contexts. The examined life is **socially cultivated autonomy** — independence achieved through (not despite) interdependence.

### Report 12: Character and Habituation *(Integration as Virtue)*
This report connects SDT's internalization continuum with Aristotelian **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Habituation|habituation]]**. Virtue development, from an SDT perspective, is the process through which initially externally regulated behaviors (following moral rules to gain approval) become progressively internalized until they are experienced as autonomous expression of integrated values. The virtuous person is not controlled by external rules or internal guilt but acts from integrated commitment to what they genuinely value.

The synthesis: Aristotle's account of virtue as "second nature" developed through habituation describes the endpoint of successful internalization. SDT provides the motivational mechanism: repeated practice in autonomy-supportive contexts facilitates need satisfaction and progressive integration. Character development requires both practice (Aristotle) and need-supportive conditions (SDT).

## Key Concepts from This Work

- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Self-Determination Theory|Self-Determination Theory (SDT)]]** — Comprehensive framework for human motivation based on satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Basic Psychological Needs|Basic Psychological Needs]]** — Universal requirements for well-being: autonomy (self-endorsement), competence (effectiveness), relatedness (connection)
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Autonomy|Autonomy]]** — Experiencing one's behavior as volitional and self-endorsed; regulating behavior according to integrated values and authentic interests (distinct from independence)
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Competence|Competence]]** — Experiencing effectiveness in producing desired outcomes and mastering challenges; feeling capable and confident
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Relatedness|Relatedness]]** — Experiencing meaningful connection, care, and belonging with others; feeling socially integrated
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Intrinsic Motivation|Intrinsic Motivation]]** — Engaging in activity for its inherent satisfaction, interest, or enjoyment; behavior valued for itself rather than as means to separable outcome
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Extrinsic Motivation|Extrinsic Motivation]]** — Engaging in activity as means to separable outcome; behavior instrumental to achieving something else
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Internalization|Internalization]]** — The process through which external regulations and values are progressively transformed into personally endorsed values integrated with sense of self
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Autonomous Motivation|Autonomous Motivation]]** — Motivation experienced as volitional and self-endorsed; includes intrinsic motivation and well-integrated extrinsic motivation
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Controlled Motivation|Controlled Motivation]]** — Motivation experienced as pressured by external contingencies or internal compulsions; includes external regulation and introjected regulation
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#External Regulation|External Regulation]]** — Behavior controlled by rewards, punishments, or external contingencies; least autonomous form of extrinsic motivation
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Introjected Regulation|Introjected Regulation]]** — Behavior driven by internal pressure, guilt, shame, or ego-involvement; partially internalized but not integrated with self
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Identified Regulation|Identified Regulation]]** — Behavior aligned with consciously valued personal goals; more autonomous but still instrumental
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Integrated Regulation|Integrated Regulation]]** — Behavior fully assimilated into coherent sense of self; most autonomous form of extrinsic motivation
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Autonomy Support|Autonomy Support]]** — Social context that acknowledges perspectives, provides meaningful choice, encourages initiative, and minimizes controlling language
- **[[glossary-examined-life-key-terms#Controlling Contexts|Controlling Contexts]]** — Social environments that pressure compliance through rewards, punishments, deadlines, surveillance, or evaluative monitoring

## Full Citation

Deci, Edward L., & Ryan, Richard M. (2000). "The 'What' and 'Why' of Goal Pursuits: Human Needs and the Self-Determination of Behavior." *Psychological Inquiry*, 11(4), 227–268.

**Alternative foundational citation:**
- Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). "Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being." *American Psychologist*, 55(1), 68–78.

**Standard citation format:**
- Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (2000). The "what" and "why" of goal pursuits: Human needs and the self-determination of behavior. *Psychological Inquiry*, 11(4), 227–268. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327965PLI1104_01

**Citation note:**
Both the *Psychological Inquiry* (2000) and *American Psychologist* (2000) articles are frequently cited as canonical SDT statements. The *American Psychologist* version is more widely accessible and cited; the *Psychological Inquiry* version includes extended peer commentary and authors' responses.

**Available in:**
- Both articles widely available through academic databases (PsycINFO, Academic Search Complete, JSTOR)
- Included in many SDT research collections and anthologies
- Heavily cited in motivation research (25,000+ citations for *American Psychologist* version)
- PDF versions available through ResearchGate and university repositories

**Theory development:**
SDT has been developed continuously since the 1970s. Key earlier works include:
- Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior*. Plenum Press.
- The 2000 articles synthesize decades of empirical research into mature theoretical framework
- Ongoing research continues refining and extending the theory through six mini-theories: Cognitive Evaluation Theory, Organismic Integration Theory, Causality Orientations Theory, Basic Needs Theory, Goal Contents Theory, and Relationships Motivation Theory

**Impact and recognition:**
- One of the most influential motivation theories in contemporary psychology
- Applied across education, health, work, sports, parenting, psychotherapy, and organizational behavior
- Extensive cross-cultural research supporting universality of basic needs
- Foundation for practical interventions in education (autonomy-supportive teaching) and health (motivational interviewing)
