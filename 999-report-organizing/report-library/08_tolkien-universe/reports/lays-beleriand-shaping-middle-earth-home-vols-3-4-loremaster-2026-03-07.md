# Verse, Cosmos, and the Craft of Mythology: The Lays of Beleriand and The Shaping of Middle-earth (HoME Volumes 3–4)

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Thematic Focus

Sometime in the early 1920s, [[J.R.R. Tolkien]] sat down and began to write a poem. Not the kind of verse-fragment that professional academics produce for private amusement — an elaborate joke, a bit of occasional verse — but something altogether more ambitious and, in its way, more desperate. He had been carrying a mythology in his head for nearly a decade. He had versions of the tales of [[Beren]] and [[Lúthien]], of [[Túrin Turambar]], of the [[Fall of Gondolin]] — narratives that had already grown through multiple prose forms without finding the shape that satisfied him. And now he turned to verse.

The poem he began was the [[Lay of Leithian]]. Over the following years he would write more than four thousand lines of it, in rhyming octosyllabic couplets, reaching the climax of Beren and Lúthien's quest but never finishing. He would set it aside and return to it. He would share it with [[C.S. Lewis]], whose annotated response constitutes one of the most vivid records of the [[Inklings]] workshop process ever preserved. He would revise several hundred lines of it in the 1950s, when he was famous and old and the prose *[[Silmarillion]]* was still unfinished on his desk. And he would never complete it.

Alongside this he wrote the [[alliterative Lay of the Children of Húrin]] — roughly two thousand lines in the ancient mode of [[Beowulf]], the poetry of the Anglo-Saxon tradition he had spent his professional career studying and translating. This poem too was abandoned before its conclusion.

Why did Tolkien, a prose writer of supreme accomplishment, spend so much of his creative energy on verse projects he could not finish? What was he reaching for, and why did verse seem to him the appropriate vehicle? And what relationship does this sustained verse project bear to the other materials in *[[The Shaping of Middle-earth]]* — the compressed mythological prose of the *[[Quenta Noldorinwa]]*, the extraordinary cosmological diagrams of the *[[Ambarkanta]]*, the first systematic *[[Annals of Valinor]]* and *[[Annals of Beleriand]]*? What do *[[The Lays of Beleriand]]* (HoME Volume 3) and *The Shaping of Middle-earth* (HoME Volume 4) tell us about Tolkien's theory of how mythology must be constructed — and why they remain among the most revealing documents in the entire legendarium for anyone who wants to understand not just *what* Tolkien built, but *how* and *why*?

**This report examines these two volumes as windows into Tolkien's methodology.** The central argument is that the lays and the cosmological documents of this period reveal a thinker who understood, with the clarity of a professional philologist, that a mythology is not simply a collection of stories — it is a *textual tradition*, requiring multiple genres, multiple modes of presentation, multiple registers of language, and a cosmological architecture as systematic as any classical or Norse model. The unfinished verse projects, the cosmological diagrams, and the dry annalistic records are not failures or supplementary notes. They are different instruments in a single extraordinary endeavor.

**Scope**: This report treats [[HoME Vol. 3]] (*The Lays of Beleriand*, 1985) and [[HoME Vol. 4]] (*The Shaping of Middle-earth*, 1986) as its primary texts, drawing comparisons with the [[Book of Lost Tales]] (HoME Vols. 1–2) and the later *[[Beren and Lúthien]]* (2017) where relevant. Narrative accounts of the stories themselves — the quest for the Silmaril, the tragedy of Túrin — are covered more fully in companion reports on the [[Quenta Silmarillion]] and the [[Wars of Beleriand]]. This report focuses on *form*, *methodology*, *cosmology*, and *creative process*.

**Roadmap**: Phase II establishes the conceptual tools — the verse forms, the textual context, Tolkien's stated and implied theory of mythological form. Phases III and IV present the evidence from each volume: the lays and Lewis's commentary (Vol. 3), then the prose materials and cosmological documents (Vol. 4). Phase V performs the deep analytical work. Phases VI and VII synthesize and draw conclusions. Phase IX provides a full lexicon and expansion topics.

## Phase II: Historical & Textual Framework

### Where These Volumes Fit in the Creative History

> [!definition] **[[The History of Middle-earth]]** (abbreviated HoME)
> A twelve-volume series edited by [[Christopher Tolkien]], published between 1983 and 1996, presenting the full creative archaeology of the legendarium: drafts, revisions, abandoned versions, editorial commentary, and the complete evolution of the mythology from Tolkien's earliest notebooks (c. 1916–17) to his late theological essays. The volumes do not form a narrative in themselves — they are an editorial excavation of a creative process. For the reader of the published *[[Silmarillion]]*, they reveal the extraordinary complexity that lies behind every paragraph.

> [!definition] **HoME Vol. 3: *[[The Lays of Beleriand]]*** (published 1985)
> Contains the two major verse works from the late 1910s through the 1930s: the *[[Lay of Leithian]]* (the story of Beren and Lúthien in rhyming octosyllabic couplets, 4,223 lines, unfinished) and the *[[Lay of the Children of Húrin]]* (the opening of Túrin's tragedy in Old English alliterative meter, approximately 2,000 lines, unfinished). Also contains C.S. Lewis's remarkable marginal commentary on the Leithian typescript. Christopher Tolkien's editorial apparatus contextualizes both poems within the composition history and traces their relationship to the prose versions.

> [!definition] **HoME Vol. 4: *[[The Shaping of Middle-earth]]*** (published 1986)
> Contains four distinct document types from the late 1920s and 1930s: (1) the "[[Sketch of the Mythology]]" (c. 1926), the first compressed prose synopsis of the legendarium; (2) the *[[Quenta Noldorinwa]]* (c. 1930), a more elaborate prose retelling; (3) the *[[Ambarkanta]]* ("Shape of the World"), Tolkien's cosmological diagrams with accompanying text; and (4) the *[[Annals of Valinor]]* and *[[Annals of Beleriand]]*, the first attempts at systematic chronological frameworks. Together these documents represent the transition from the elaborate frame-narrative of the *Book of Lost Tales* to the compressed mythological style of what would eventually become *The Silmarillion*.

### The Book of Lost Tales: The Baseline

To understand what Tolkien was moving away from in these volumes, one must understand the [[Book of Lost Tales]] (HoME Vols. 1–2). Written primarily between 1916 and 1920, the Lost Tales are framed as stories told to a mortal mariner named [[Eriol]] (later [[Ælfwine]]) who has reached the island of [[Tol Eressëa]], where Elves tell him the ancient history of the world. The frame is elaborate, intricate, and deeply romantic in the nineteenth-century sense — the tales arrive through a nested narrative of fireside telling, with named Elvish storytellers and rich descriptions of Elvish domestic life. Each tale is long, detailed, and idiosyncratic.

The problem was obvious: this form was unwieldy and unpublishable in its existing state. The frame kept expanding; the individual tales were inconsistent with each other; and the project resisted completion by its very architecture. Beginning in the mid-1920s, Tolkien began searching for a different form — something that could contain the whole mythology in a single, coherent, authoritative presentation.

> [!key-claim] **The Formal Problem at the Heart of the Legendarium**
> Tolkien's legendarium faced a persistent challenge that no other mythological project of the twentieth century quite paralleled: he was trying to create, almost single-handedly, the equivalent of a complete national mythology — something with the authority of the Norse *Eddas*, the comprehensiveness of the Greek mythological tradition, and the literary quality of Virgil — while simultaneously making it original, internally consistent, and emotionally convincing. The form question (how should such a mythology be presented?) was not peripheral but central. Every formal choice carried implications for what the mythology could *be*.

### The Two Verse Traditions

> [!definition] **[[Octosyllabic Rhyming Couplets]]** (the meter of the *Lay of Leithian*)
> Lines of eight syllables (iambic tetrameter) organized in pairs that rhyme: *da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM / da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM*. This was the dominant meter of medieval English and French romance — Chaucer used it in the *House of Fame*, and it appears throughout the medieval tradition that most directly shaped Tolkien's literary sensibility. It is a relatively rapid, forward-moving meter, well-suited to narrative, with a certain sing-song quality that distinguishes it from the graver weight of blank verse or the severe rhythms of alliterative poetry. In Tolkien's hands it carries echoes of Chaucer and of the medieval Arthurian romances he loved, while reaching toward the elevation of epic.

> [!definition] **[[Old English Alliterative Verse]]** (the meter of the *Lay of the Children of Húrin*)
> The metrical system of Anglo-Saxon poetry, including *[[Beowulf]]*, the *Battle of Maldon*, and the works of [[Cynewulf]]. Each line is divided into two half-lines (each containing two stressed syllables), linked by alliteration — at least one stressed syllable in the first half-line alliterates with the first stressed syllable of the second. The effect is muscular, incantatory, and heavy with the weight of an entire literary tradition. The system was also used in Middle English poems like *[[Sir Gawain and the Green Knight]]* and *[[Pearl]]* — texts Tolkien edited professionally. Writing in this tradition was, for Tolkien, not mere imitation but a homecoming.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Conceptual Checkpoint**
> Before reading the textual evidence, clarify this distinction: the octosyllabic couplets of the *Lay of Leithian* and the alliterative verse of the *Lay of the Children of Húrin* are not interchangeable choices. They represent different relationships to literary tradition and different emotional registers. What does it tell you that Tolkien chose medieval romance meter for Beren and Lúthien, and Old English heroic meter for Túrin? What does each story require that the other does not?

### Tolkien's Theory of Mythological Form

Tolkien never wrote a sustained theoretical treatise on mythology's relationship to verse — but the evidence of his practice, supplemented by his letters and his essay "On Fairy-Stories," is sufficient to reconstruct his position. The great mythologies he admired most all existed in verse: Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, Virgil's *Aeneid*, the [[Finnish Kalevala]] (the direct inspiration for the legendarium project), the [[Norse Poetic Edda]], *Beowulf*. Prose mythology, in his understanding, was secondary — a later retelling, a preservation of material that had originally achieved its authoritative form in verse. The dignity and weight of myth, its claim on the imagination, came in part from the formal discipline that verse imposed.

This conviction was not sentimental. It was grounded in Tolkien's professional understanding of how actual mythological traditions had developed. The [[Kalevala]] had been assembled by [[Elias Lönnrot]] from oral verse traditions; the Norse myths had survived partly in Snorri Sturluson's prose Eddas, but the older and more authoritative [[Poetic Edda]] was verse. Tolkien knew, as a philologist, that the prestige of the verse versions was not accidental — it reflected the mnemonic, ritual, and aesthetic functions that verse had always served in pre-literate and semi-literate cultures. A mythology in verse had a kind of authority that prose could never fully replicate.

## Phase III: Evidence from the Texts — *The Lays of Beleriand* (HoME Vol. 3)

### The Lay of Leithian: "Release from Bondage"

The *[[Lay of Leithian]]* begins around 1925, though some scholars suggest fragments may be earlier. Its title means "Release from Bondage" in Tolkien's invented Elvish — a translation he himself provided, and a title that operates at multiple levels simultaneously. The most immediate referent is Beren's release from the imprisonment of Thû (the figure who would become [[Sauron]]) and from the hopeless task of winning a Silmaril from Morgoth's crown. But "release from bondage" also names what verse itself represents in Tolkien's practice: a disciplined formal constraint that paradoxically liberates the narrative by forcing precise word-choice, memorable phrasing, and a pace governed by aesthetic necessity rather than expositional convenience.

The poem opens with a characteristic flourish — a self-conscious announcement of its subject in the medieval epic tradition:

> *The leave of tree and shining grass,*
> *and winds that touch the sea and pass*
> *were present at the telling...*

These lines (in Christopher Tolkien's reconstruction) establish immediately the mode: this is verse meant to be heard, verse that reaches back to the medieval romance while driving toward something new. What follows is the story familiar to any reader of the published *Silmarillion* — the love of the mortal man Beren for the Elvish princess [[Lúthien Tinúviel]], the impossible task set by her father [[Thingol]], the imprisonment by Sauron, the rescue by Lúthien, the descent into Angband, the theft of a Silmaril from Morgoth's crown, Beren's death, Lúthien's plea before Mandos, and their return to a mortal life together.

But the verse experience of this story is fundamentally different from the prose experience. The famous passage in which Lúthien calls to Beren in the pits of [[Sauron]]'s isle — her singing that breaks his captivity — arrives in the poem with a weight of accumulated rhythmic expectation that no prose equivalent can generate. The couplets have been building, rhyme linking to rhyme, meaning accumulating in a kind of sonic inevitability, until the moment of release. The verse form *enacts* what it describes.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Poem Enacts Its Title: Form as Argument in the *Lay of Leithian***
> The title "Release from Bondage" applies not only to the story's plot but to the poem's formal logic. Rhyming couplets are, paradoxically, both a constraint and a generative engine: the requirement to find a rhyme forces the poet toward word-choices that pure prose narration would never reach, and the anticipation of the rhyme creates forward momentum that becomes, in moments of climax, a kind of inevitability. When Lúthien's singing releases Beren from imprisonment, the formal structure of the poem itself performs a release — the rhyme arrives, and it feels earned. Tolkien's choice of this particular verse form for this particular story is not arbitrary. The meter and the narrative are in structural sympathy.

The poem extends through twelve cantos in its primary form, reaching as far as the escape from Angband with the Silmaril, before breaking off. Christopher Tolkien's editorial work in *Beren and Lúthien* (2017) shows clearly that Tolkien returned to the poem in the 1950s, revising approximately the first six cantos extensively — at a time when the *[[Lord of the Rings]]* had just been published and the *Silmarillion* was still years from any publishable form. That return, more than thirty years after the poem's inception, reveals how central the verse project remained to Tolkien's sense of what the legendarium required.

The incompleteness of the poem is itself significant. It is not that Tolkien did not know how the story ended — he knew perfectly well, and the prose versions make this clear. What he could not do was *finish it in verse*, verse that met his own standards of quality. The gap between the story he carried in his imagination and the poem he could write on the page was the permanent condition of the lays project.

> [!textual-note] **Tolkien's Own Assessment: The Gap Between Vision and Execution**
> In a draft letter to his publisher, Tolkien wrote of his mythology that it was "primarily linguistic in inspiration" — but the verse projects reveal an additional pressure: the gap between what the mythology demanded formally and what his verse could deliver. The surviving manuscripts show extensive revision, false starts, and passages that clearly dissatisfied him. This is not the work of a casual versifier. It is the work of a craftsman who knew precisely how far short his execution fell of his conception, and who could not stop trying.

### The Alliterative Lay of the Children of Húrin

The *[[Lay of the Children of Húrin]]* represents a different formal ambition altogether. Where the *Lay of Leithian* reaches toward the medieval romance, the alliterative poem reaches back behind the Norman Conquest — to the Old English of [[Beowulf]], to the Germanic heroic tradition that Tolkien believed expressed something essential about the Northern imagination.

The choice of alliterative verse for the [[Túrin Turambar]] story is not merely literary antiquarianism. Tolkien understood, as the professional scholar of *Beowulf* who would eventually argue in "[[The Monsters and the Critics]]" that the poem was a supreme artistic achievement rather than a flawed historical document, that alliterative verse carried within it a particular moral and emotional register. It was the verse of men facing doom without hope of reprieve — the verse of *Beowulf* going into the dragon's barrow knowing he will not return, of the warriors at [[Maldon]] fighting on after all hope of victory was gone. This was the precise emotional register that Túrin's story required.

The poem's opening lines (which Tolkien composed in modern English alliterative verse following the ancient prosodic rules meticulously) establish this register immediately — the heavy, forward-leaning rhythm of stressed syllables linked by alliteration, the syntactic inversions of Old English verse style, the grave and purposeful tone. Christopher Tolkien notes that this poem is more technically accomplished in its prosody than the *Lay of Leithian* — Tolkien's command of the alliterative tradition, developed through decades of professional scholarship, gave him greater mastery over this mode than over the rhyming couplets.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **The Alliterative Lay ↔ Beowulf and the Ethics of Northern Heroism**
> Tolkien's famous argument in "The Monsters and the Critics" was that *Beowulf* is not merely an adventure story but a meditation on mortality: the hero who knows he faces death and faces it anyway, not because he expects salvation but because courage in the face of doom is the supreme virtue the Northern tradition recognized. The alliterative *Lay of the Children of Húrin* places Túrin within exactly this framework — but with a crucial theological complication. Túrin is not heroic precisely in the *Beowulf* sense, because his doom is not merely external (dragon, mortality) but internal (pride, wrath, the operating curse of Morgoth). The alliterative form summons the tradition of heroic doom, while the story subverts it: this is what Northern heroism looks like when its virtues become vices, when the determination that should be courage becomes the pride that destroys. The verse form creates the expectation; the story fulfills it with devastating irony.

The poem breaks off after approximately two thousand lines, covering Húrin and [[Morwen]]'s early history and Túrin's childhood — a substantial opening, but nowhere near the story's full arc. Unlike the *Lay of Leithian*, there is no evidence of Tolkien's return to this poem in later life. The alliterative mode, it seems, was eventually conceded to the two great professional projects that consumed it: the *Beowulf* translation and the verse *Fall of Arthur*, both of which appeared posthumously.

### C.S. Lewis's Commentary: The Inklings at Work

The most extraordinary document in *The Lays of Beleriand* is neither of the lays themselves but a set of marginal notes that [[C.S. Lewis]] made on a typescript of the *Lay of Leithian*, almost certainly in 1929–30 during the earliest period of the Tolkien-Lewis friendship. Christopher Tolkien transcribes and discusses these notes in the editorial apparatus of HoME Vol. 3, and they are among the most vivid records of the [[Inklings]] workshop process that exist.

Lewis's approach to the typescript is, characteristically, theatrical. He writes in the persona of a fictitious medieval scholiast — a learned commentator providing annotations in mock-archaic academic style, praising the poet's achievements with elaborate rhetoric and criticizing weak passages with caustic precision. The performance is brilliant and revealing in equal measure.

What Lewis loved in the poem: the freshness and conviction of the mythology, the specific imagery (he singled out several passages for particular praise, including the description of Lúthien's first appearance), and the general narrative momentum. He clearly found the legendarium compelling — not as a curiosity but as a genuine literary achievement. His notes convey the experience of a sophisticated reader encountering a world he did not know existed, and finding it far better than he had expected.

What Lewis criticized: occasional padding to fill out the couplet requirement, awkward rhymes that sacrificed natural word order for metrical convenience, and passages where the verse felt mechanical rather than inspired. These are precisely the criticisms a skilled versifier would make — Lewis was himself a competent and occasionally distinguished poet, and he read the *Lay of Leithian* with a craftsman's eye.

> [!authorial-intent] **Tolkien's Response to Lewis's Critique**
> The correspondence and memoirs surrounding the Tolkien-Lewis friendship make clear that Lewis's annotated typescript was exactly the kind of response Tolkien was seeking — detailed, technically informed, and genuinely engaged with the mythology as a literary project rather than a private indulgence. Tolkien's own surviving letters to Lewis suggest that this exchange was one of the catalysts for his sustained friendship with Lewis throughout the 1930s. The creative isolation that had characterized the *Book of Lost Tales* period — Tolkien writing essentially for himself — was giving way to something more like a genuine literary workshop. The *Lay of Leithian* was the first major document of that workshop.

> [!analytical-insight] **What the Lewis Commentary Reveals About the Inklings' Method**
> The mock-scholiast framing of Lewis's commentary is more than playful evasion of direct criticism — it is a mode of engagement that takes the mythology seriously as literary achievement. By writing as if commenting on an ancient text, Lewis implicitly grants the *Lay of Leithian* the status of something that might *be* an ancient text — something with the weight and authority of the traditions Tolkien was drawing on. This is intellectual generosity of a very high order, and it explains something about the relationship: Lewis could read the mythology with the imagination of a reader who temporarily inhabits the secondary world, while simultaneously offering the criticism of a craftsman who inhabits the primary world. Tolkien needed both.

## Phase IV: Evidence from the Texts — *The Shaping of Middle-earth* (HoME Vol. 4)

### The Sketch and the Quenta: Toward Compressed Mythology

*[[The Shaping of Middle-earth]]* opens with the "[[Sketch of the Mythology]]" (c. 1926), a document that represents a decisive formal departure from everything that preceded it. Where the *Book of Lost Tales* had expanded the mythology through elaborate narrative framing, the Sketch compresses it — stripping away the Eriol/Ælfwine framework entirely and presenting the mythology as direct, authoritative summary. At roughly thirty pages, it covers material that took hundreds of pages in the Lost Tales. The compression is deliberate and theoretically significant: this is Tolkien testing whether the mythology can stand without its scaffolding.

The *[[Quenta Noldorinwa]]* (c. 1930, "The History of the Noldor") expands and refines the Sketch into something closer to the published *Silmarillion* in style, though considerably shorter. It is written in a measured, elevated prose that consciously echoes the register of medieval historical chronicles — not the intimate fireside prose of the Lost Tales, but something graver, more remote, and more authoritative. This is the prose style that Christopher Tolkien would eventually recognize as the distinctive voice of the *Silmarillion* — the prose of a mythology telling its own history from a great distance.

> [!literary-insight] **The Prose of the *Quenta Noldorinwa* as Mythological Register**
> The prose style Tolkien develops in the *Quenta Noldorinwa* is a deliberately archaic construction — it draws on the Authorized Bible's cadences, on medieval chronicle prose, and on the grave simplicity of the Norse sagas in translation. Sentences are relatively short; passive constructions create distance; events are reported rather than experienced. "And it came to pass that..." "And thus it was..." This is the register of events that happened long ago and far away, the register of myth rather than of narrative fiction. When the published *Silmarillion* reports the Kinslaying at Alqualondë in a single measured paragraph, the horror is intensified precisely by the prose's emotional restraint. The *Quenta Noldorinwa* is the laboratory where Tolkien discovers this technique.

### The Ambarkanta: Diagrams of a Cosmos

Among the most extraordinary documents in the entire *History of Middle-earth* series is the *[[Ambarkanta]]* ("Shape of the World"), which appears in HoME Vol. 4. It is not a narrative but a cosmological treatise — a description of the physical architecture of [[Arda]] as a three-dimensional structure, accompanied by five diagrams that Tolkien himself drew.

The diagrams show a cross-section of Tolkien's world. At the center is [[Ambar]], the solid earth of Arda itself, set like a plate or disc within concentric spheres of air and space. Above and around the world extends [[Vista]], the breathable atmosphere. Beyond Vista lies [[Ilmen]], the pure upper air through which the stars move — distinct from the breathable atmosphere below it, a region accessible only to the Maiar and the greatest Elven spirits. Encircling everything is [[Vaiya]], the outer sea/atmosphere that wraps the world on all sides, including above and below. And beyond Vaiya, separated from the living world by the *[[Ekkaia]]* (the Encircling Sea), lies the absolute exterior — the Void, the formless nothing outside Eä.

The diagrams also show [[Valinor]] on the western edge of the world, accessible across the Great Sea (and, after the Second Age's [[Change of the World]], only via the [[Straight Road]] that curves through the bent sphere back to [[Aman]]). The [[Door of Night]] in the east allows the Moon's passage beneath the world. The [[Gates of Morning]] in the west release the Sun each day.

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: The Ambarkanta and the Problem of the Sacred Visible**
> Tolkien's cosmological diagrams are simultaneously a cartographic achievement and a theological statement. The division of the world's atmosphere into Vista (breathable, human), Ilmen (pure, accessible to the higher powers), and Vaiya (the outer deep that wraps creation) maps onto a hierarchy of being — what humans can inhabit, what the Maiar inhabit, and the surrounding mystery. The architecture is not random: it expresses the Tolkienian conviction that the sacred and the physical are not separate but layered — that the same world contains different ontological levels accessible to different kinds of being. This is a visual embodiment of the Boethian understanding of creation as a structured hierarchy from material to spiritual, each level sustained by the level above it.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **The Ambarkanta ↔ Norse Cosmology and Its Divergences**
> Norse cosmology organized the world as a great tree ([[Yggdrasil]]) with nine realms arranged vertically (Ásgarðr above, Miðgarðr in the middle, Niðavellir and Hel below). Tolkien clearly knew this model intimately. But the Ambarkanta's concentric sphere architecture is fundamentally different: it is not a hierarchical tree but a nested set of envelopes, with the sacred not above the world but *around* it on all sides. The Elvish perspective in the legendarium — looking *west* toward [[Aman]] rather than *up* toward Ásgarðr — is built into this cosmological architecture. Valinor is in the world, accessible in principle across the sea, not in a separate spiritual realm above the clouds. This theological geography generates the legendarium's unique quality of immanence: the divine is not remote but historically present in the world's physical geography. The Ambarkanta's diagrams make this visible.

What makes the Ambarkanta remarkable as a document is not only its content but its existence. Tolkien could have described his cosmological architecture in prose; instead, he drew diagrams. This choice reveals a mind that was thinking three-dimensionally about the world's physical structure with the precision of a geographer, not merely a storyteller. The diagrams are working documents — they helped Tolkien keep his cosmological thinking consistent across the legendarium's different narratives. They are also, in their way, a form of world-building that goes beyond anything conventional fantasy creation has demanded: an attempt to make the secondary world's physics as internally coherent as a real planetary system.

> [!key-insight] **Linguistic Observation: The Name *Ambarkanta* and Tolkien's Self-Referential Etymology**
> The title *Ambarkanta* is Quenya: *Ambar* (world, earth, fate — the word encompasses all three meanings in Tolkien's construction) + *kanta* (shape, form). The word *ambar* also relates to the Elvish word for doom or fate (*umbar* in Sindarin), creating a semantic field in which "the shape of the world" carries within it the sense of the world's ordained destiny. The Ambarkanta is not merely a physical description but a fate-map — the shape of the world as Eru intended it to be, before the Marring. This embedded etymology is characteristic of Tolkien's practice throughout the legendarium: he rarely names anything without encoding meaning in the name itself, so that the language of the mythology is always simultaneously a carrier of its theology.

### The Annals: Chronology as World-Building Method

The *[[Annals of Valinor]]* and *[[Annals of Beleriand]]* represent yet a third formal experiment within HoME Vol. 4. Where the *Quenta Noldorinwa* narrates the mythology, and the *Ambarkanta* diagrams its cosmological architecture, the Annals record it as dry historical chronicle — year by year, event by event, in the format of the medieval ecclesiastical annals that Tolkien knew from his scholarly work.

The entries are deliberately laconic. "Year 1: Valar enter Arda." "Year 1050: Melkor restless." "Year 1080: First war of Valar and Melkor." This flatness is not laziness — it is a precise formal decision. By presenting the mythological history in the same format as a real historical chronicle, Tolkien imports into the secondary world the authority of historical documentation. The Annals feel like *records*, like something consulted rather than performed. They create the impression that there is a scribe behind them — an Elf or a learned Man who was there, or who learned from those who were — recording events with the selective brevity of someone who knows that what they write will outlast them.

> [!textual-note] **The Multi-Genre Mythology: Why One Form is Never Enough**
> What *The Shaping of Middle-earth* reveals, taken as a whole, is that Tolkien understood his mythology to require multiple genres operating simultaneously. The narrative prose of the *Quenta Noldorinwa* gives the mythology its story. The cosmological diagrams of the *Ambarkanta* give it its physical world-architecture. The Annals give it its historical authority. The verse lays (in the companion volume) give it its elevation and emotional intensity. No single document is the mythology — the mythology is the totality of all these documents in relationship. This is how real textual traditions work: Homer and Hesiod and the historians and the dramatists and the philosophers collectively constitute "Greek mythology," not any single text.

## Phase V: Deep Analysis & Interpretation

### The Theory Behind the Verse Project

Tolkien's sustained effort to render the mythology in verse was, at its deepest level, an attempt to give the legendarium the same kind of authority that the mythologies he most admired possessed. The [[Finnish Kalevala]] — the direct inspiration for the whole project, as Tolkien acknowledged in Letter 131 to Milton Waldman — existed in verse. The [[Poetic Edda]], the repository of Norse myth, existed in verse. *Beowulf*, the poem Tolkien loved and spent his professional life defending and translating, existed in verse. For Tolkien, the question was not whether mythology should be in verse but which verse tradition was appropriate for his mythology.

His two choices — medieval romance couplets for the Beren and Lúthien story, Old English alliterative verse for the Túrin story — reveal a sophisticated understanding of how form and content interact. The love story required the forward-moving romance tradition, with its connotations of knight-errantry, courtly love elevated by the heroic, and the interweaving of adventure with devotion. The tragedy required the doom-laden gravity of Old English heroic poetry, with its fatalist weight and its echoes of a world without Christian consolation.

> [!literary-insight] **Two Stories, Two Traditions, One Theory**
> Tolkien's decision to use different verse forms for the two major lays is itself a statement of mythological method: he understood that different aspects of the legendarium embodied different aspects of the literary inheritance he was drawing on. The Beren and Lúthien story reaches toward the romance tradition — toward Chaucer and the medieval lai and the Celtic tales of mortal men in Faërie. The Túrin story reaches toward the heroic tragedy of the Northern world — toward the *Völsunga Saga*, the *Njarl's Saga*, and ultimately *Beowulf*. To use the same verse form for both would be to flatten the mythology, to obscure the different emotional registers that make the First Age so rich. The form is part of the meaning.

Yet the verse projects remained unfinished, and this incompleteness deserves direct examination. The *Lay of Leithian* breaks off; the alliterative *Children of Húrin* breaks off; even the verse *[[Fall of Arthur]]* (a non-legendarium project) breaks off. This is not a coincidence of circumstance — it is a pattern. What was it about verse that Tolkien could not complete?

The answer is suggested by a remark in one of Tolkien's letters, where he speaks of his perfectionism as "the besetting sin of those who in early life discover they can do something well, and then spend the rest of their lives trying to do it perfectly." Verse, unlike prose, exerts a constant visible discipline. Every line is measured. Every rhyme or alliterative link is either right or wrong. The gap between what a poem could be and what it currently is is always visible, because the form itself is a standard of judgment. The prose *Silmarillion* could be revised indefinitely, sections added and removed, the style adjusted — it was always *closer to finished* than a verse text of equal ambition, precisely because the prose form did not make its own incompleteness visible in the same way. Verse was the more honest medium, and therefore the more tormenting one.

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: Incompleteness as a Feature of Subcreation**
> There is a broader theological dimension to the unfinished verse lays that Tolkien himself would have recognized. In his essay "On Fairy-Stories," he argues that the human impulse to create Secondary Worlds is a reflection of the imago Dei — the image of God the Creator in which humans were made. But the human sub-creator is always finite, always limited, always reaching toward a perfection that only the Primary Creator can achieve. The unfinished lays are not simply failures of execution; they are, in a Tolkienian framework, the inevitable condition of any subcreative project of sufficient ambition. The mythology is always larger than the work that contains it. The Loremaster who reads these fragments must hold in mind both what they are and what they were reaching toward.

### The Ambarkanta as Unique Achievement

The *Ambarkanta* deserves to be considered one of the most original achievements in *The Shaping of Middle-earth*, and indeed in Tolkien's entire creative life. It has no real parallel in any of the mythological traditions Tolkien drew on. The Norse cosmological scheme (Yggdrasil, the nine worlds) is a mythological diagram rather than a physical-cosmological one — it describes how the cosmos is organized spiritually and mythologically, not physically. The classical geographical tradition (the *orbis terrarum* with its encircling Ocean) is geographical rather than cosmological. Tolkien's Ambarkanta is something genuinely different: a three-dimensional physical model of the world's structure, drawn to scale, with labeled layers and specific physical properties.

What makes this significant is not merely its originality but what it implies about Tolkien's conception of secondary-world building. He was not content to evoke; he needed to understand. The layers of atmosphere, the relationships between physical regions and ontological levels, the specific geography of how the Sun and Moon travel through the sky — these were not incidental decorations but structural features of the world he was building. The Ambarkanta was the engineering drawing that ensured the poetry was physically coherent.

> [!original-synthesis] **The Ambarkanta and the Poetics of Structural Coherence**
> There is a profound connection between the Ambarkanta's cosmological precision and the legendary quality that readers most consistently identify in Tolkien's best work: the sense that the world continues beyond the edges of the narrative, that there are consistent rules governing what cannot be shown as well as what can. The Ambarkanta does not appear in *The Lord of the Rings* or even prominently in *The Silmarillion* — yet the world of those books feels physically real in a way that depends on exactly the kind of structural coherence the Ambarkanta provides. The reader cannot see the cosmological diagrams, but they can feel their effects: a world where the sky is not merely scenery but a layered architecture with ontological significance, where the stars were placed by a deliberate hand and move through a specific region of the atmosphere, where the Blessed Realm is not "heaven" in any vague sense but a physical location accessible across a physical sea. The Ambarkanta is the invisible skeleton of the visible body.

### The Significance of Annalistic Method

The Annals in HoME Vol. 4 — the *Annals of Valinor* and *Annals of Beleriand* — represent a formal insight that would become increasingly important as the legendarium developed. By writing the mythology's chronology in annalistic form (and later developing the more elaborate *Grey Annals* in HoME Vol. 11 and the *Númenórean Annals* for the Second Age), Tolkien was simultaneously solving a practical problem and pursuing a theoretical conviction.

The practical problem was consistency. The Lost Tales were full of chronological imprecision — events happened in a general sequence, but dates were vague and sometimes contradictory. The Annals imposed discipline by forcing Tolkien to assign specific years to every event and to maintain those assignments consistently across different parts of the mythology. When a character appears in both the Beleriand narrative and a Valinor narrative, their ages and the timeline of their relationship must be consistent. The Annals were the tool that made this consistency achievable.

The theoretical conviction was that convincing secondary worlds require the impression of a *historical record*. The mythology needed to feel as if it had been preserved through a chain of transmission — as if there were scribes and scholars who cared about getting the dates right. The annalistic format automatically implies this chain: someone compiled these records, from sources, with the scholarly intention of accuracy. This implication is independent of any explicit claim — the form itself generates the impression.

> [!key-insight] **Linguistic Observation: Tolkien's Use of Valian Years and Sun Years**
> The Annals of Valinor use Valian Years (each approximately ten solar years in later reckoning) for events before the Sun's creation, switching to solar years at the First Age's beginning. This calendrical precision is not merely pedantic — it encodes a theological argument. Valian Years are longer because time itself moved differently in the age before the Sun, when the world was lit by the Two Trees and the Ainur walked more openly in Arda. The shift to solar years at the First Age marks a cosmological change: the world becomes measurably more mortal, its time more like the time of Men, from the moment the Sun first rises. The Annals' calendar system is a form of theological statement embedded in chronological format.

## Phase VI: Themes, Significance & Limitations

### Why These Volumes Matter for Understanding Tolkien

*The Lays of Beleriand* and *The Shaping of Middle-earth* are among the most important volumes in the *History of Middle-earth* series for a specific reason: they capture Tolkien at the moment of greatest formal experimentation, before he had settled on the mythological voice and structural approach that would eventually characterize the published *Silmarillion*. Reading these volumes alongside the later *Silmarillion* gives one the experience of watching a great sculptor at work — seeing the forms he tried and set aside, and thereby understanding why the final form is as it is.

For any reader who has found the published *Silmarillion* austere, remote, or emotionally distant, the lays offer a completely different experience of the same material. The *Lay of Leithian* brings Beren and Lúthien alive in a way that the compressed prose cannot — the verse creates space for their emotional reality to breathe. The alliterative *Lay of the Children of Húrin* conveys the weight of [[Túrin]]'s doom through its very metrical heaviness in a way the prose account cannot quite replicate. These verse fragments are not inferior to the prose versions — they are different, offering different aspects of the same mythological jewel.

> [!important] **The Incompleteness Problem: How to Read Unfinished Works**
> The most significant limitation of HoME Vols. 3–4 as reading experiences is their fragmentary nature. The lays both break off before their conclusions; the *Quenta Noldorinwa* is a draft superseded by later versions; the *Ambarkanta*'s cosmological scheme was subsequently modified. A reader coming to these volumes hoping for resolved narratives will be disappointed. The appropriate approach is that of the archaeologist rather than the novel-reader: these are fragments of a creative excavation, not finished artifacts. Their value lies in what they reveal about the process that eventually produced the finished legendarium — and that value is enormous for anyone who wants to understand Tolkien's achievement in depth.

### The Inklings Context

The Lewis commentary in HoME Vol. 3 makes these volumes essential reading for anyone interested in the [[Inklings]] as a cultural phenomenon. The Inklings are sometimes romanticized as a golden circle of Christian literary excellence — Tolkien, Lewis, [[Charles Williams]], [[Owen Barfield]], and others meeting in the [[Eagle and Child]] pub and at Lewis's rooms at Magdalen College. The Lewis commentary on the *Lay of Leithian* allows a different and more specific view: two writers at the beginning of a friendship, one sharing a work of enormous personal significance, the other responding with the care and precision that genuine literary friendship requires.

What the commentary shows is that the Inklings' workshop practice was real and rigorous. Lewis was not simply encouraging — he was critical, technically specific, and honest about where the poem fell short. And the evidence suggests that Tolkien took this criticism seriously: the 1950s revisions to the *Lay of Leithian* address several of the specific weaknesses Lewis had identified two decades earlier. This is a creative relationship of genuine intellectual depth and mutual respect.

> [!reflection] **Knowledge State — After**
> Return to your earlier answer about whether the shift from verse to prose represents failure, maturation, or something else. How has your view changed? The most complete answer will acknowledge that it was neither failure nor mere maturation but a theoretically sophisticated decision: Tolkien recognized that the mythology was too large and too complex to be rendered entirely in verse, that the verse lays served certain functions (elevation, emotional intensity, authority) that the compressed mythological prose could not fully replace, and that the solution was ultimately the multi-genre mythology — verse for certain tales, prose for the mythological overview, annals for chronological authority, diagrams for cosmological coherence — that HoME Vols. 3–4 together represent the experimental development of.

### Limitations and Open Questions

The textual situation of both volumes presents genuine challenges. The dating of compositions within these years is often approximate; Christopher Tolkien's editorial notes are the primary guide, and some of his attributions of date have been questioned by subsequent Tolkien scholars. The relationship between the versions in these volumes and Tolkien's intentions for what might eventually have been published is genuinely uncertain — Tolkien never prepared any of these documents for publication himself.

The question of what the *Lay of Leithian* would have looked like completed is genuinely unanswerable. Based on the existing cantos and the 1950s revisions, one can extrapolate — but the poem's completion was always reaching toward something Tolkien could not quite achieve. Whether this represents a limitation of his verse-craft or a limitation of the verse form for material of this particular kind is a question that admits no definitive answer.

> [!warning] **Common Misconception: These Volumes as "Raw Material" Only**
> A significant error in approaching HoME Vols. 3–4 is treating them merely as drafts from which the published *Silmarillion* was refined — as if the lays and the Ambarkanta were stepping stones to a more important destination. This misunderstands their nature. The *Lay of Leithian* is not an inferior version of the prose story in *The Silmarillion* — it is a different and in some respects richer artistic object. The *Ambarkanta*'s diagrams convey things about Tolkien's cosmological thinking that no prose description can replace. These works have value in themselves, not only as evidence for a creative history. Reading them *as* their own objects, rather than as stages in the evolution of something else, is essential to understanding what Tolkien was doing.

## Phase VII: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

### The Multi-Genre Mythology: A Synthesizing Framework

*[[The Lays of Beleriand]]* and *[[The Shaping of Middle-earth]]* together constitute the fullest evidence we have for a claim that the Loremaster would argue is the central insight about Tolkien's creative method: **he understood that a convincing mythology cannot be any single genre of text.** It must be a *textual tradition* — a multiplicity of documents in different formal registers, each contributing something the others cannot, together creating the impression of a real cultural and historical body of lore.

Homer and Hesiod are both in the tradition; neither alone is Greek mythology. The Norse tradition encompasses the Poetic Edda (verse), the Prose Edda (prose mythological synthesis), the sagas (prose historical narratives), and the skaldic poetry (verse praise and narrative). Each of these contributes something different to the cultural totality. Tolkien was reconstructing — or rather, constructing de novo — a comparable textual tradition. The verse lays were his Poetic Edda: elevated, emotionally intense, incomplete in the way that oral traditions are always incomplete. The *Quenta Noldorinwa* and later the *Silmarillion* were his Prose Edda: the synthesizing mythological prose that organizes the tradition into a coherent whole. The Annals were his medieval chronicles: the historiographic documentation that makes the mythology feel like history. The Ambarkanta was something with no real parallel: a cosmological working document that systematized the world's physical architecture with a precision no traditional mythology quite achieved.

> [!original-synthesis] **The Loremaster's Contribution: Tolkien's Theory of Mythological Completeness**
> There is an implicit theory of mythological completeness in HoME Vols. 3–4 that Tolkien never articulated explicitly but that his practice embodies with remarkable consistency. A mythology achieves the quality of *realness* — the quality that makes readers feel they are encountering a tradition that existed before the text they are reading, and will continue to exist after they finish reading — not through any single perfect text but through the accumulation of multiple imperfect, partial, genre-diverse documents. The incompleteness of the lays is not a flaw in this framework but a feature: incomplete texts suggest a tradition larger than what has been preserved, and that suggestion is exactly the effect a mythology needs to create. The very fragments that frustrate readers looking for completed narratives are precisely the documents that make the legendarium feel historically real. Tolkien's "failure" to finish the lays was, in the deepest sense, a formal success.

### The Question Revisited: Why Verse?

The central question of this report — why did Tolkien pursue verse so persistently, and what does its eventual displacement by prose mean? — admits now of a synthesizing answer.

Verse was necessary because the mythology's claim to mythological authority could not be made in any other way. A world that contained only prose, however well-written, would feel like an invented world. Verse does something that prose cannot: it creates the impression of *oral tradition*, of a body of narrative that has been memorized and transmitted across generations, shaped by the discipline of formal recitation. When [[Aragorn]] recites a fragment of the *Lay of Leithian* at Weathertop in *The Lord of the Rings* — "A Elbereth Gilthoniel" and the alliterative lines about Lúthien Tinúviel — the effect is of a *real oral tradition* surfacing in a modern prose narrative. That effect depends on the verse existing, somewhere, as a completed thing. The prose *Silmarillion* could not produce it.

But verse, ultimately, was not sufficient by itself to accomplish everything the legendarium required. It was too slow, too demanding of sustained technical excellence that Tolkien could not always maintain, and too restricting of the mythology's scope. The solution was not to abandon verse but to deploy it strategically — to let the prose carry the mythological overview, the annals carry the chronological authority, and the verse (in both the lays and in the verse fragments embedded throughout *The Lord of the Rings* itself) carry the elevation and authority that only verse can provide.

HoME Vols. 3–4 are the laboratory where this solution was developed. They are not the record of failed projects; they are the record of a mythology being invented.

## Phase IX: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **[[Lay of Leithian]]** (Quenya: *Leithian* = "release from bondage")
> Tolkien's long verse narrative (4,223+ lines, unfinished) telling the story of [[Beren]] and [[Lúthien]] in rhyming octosyllabic couplets. Written from approximately 1925 onward, with major revisions in the 1950s. The most sustained single verse work in the legendarium, and the primary evidence for what the mythology would have been had Tolkien succeeded in rendering it in verse. Appears in *The Lays of Beleriand* (HoME Vol. 3) and in comparative form in *Beren and Lúthien* (2017).

> [!definition] **[[Lay of the Children of Húrin]]** (also "Lay of Húrin and Morwen")
> Tolkien's alliterative verse treatment of the opening of [[Túrin Turambar]]'s tragedy, written in the mode of Old English heroic poetry (following the prosodic rules of *Beowulf*). Approximately 2,000 lines, covering events up to Húrin's departure from Dor-lómin. The most technically accomplished piece of alliterative verse Tolkien produced outside his professional translations. Appears in *The Lays of Beleriand* (HoME Vol. 3).

> [!definition] **[[Ambarkanta]]** (Quenya: *ambar* = "world/fate" + *kanta* = "shape/form")
> Tolkien's cosmological treatise and diagram series presenting the physical architecture of [[Arda]] as a system of concentric spheres: [[Ambar]] (the solid earth), [[Vista]] (breathable atmosphere), [[Ilmen]] (the pure upper air of stars and Maiar), [[Vaiya]] (the outer envelope surrounding the world), and [[Ekkaia]] (the Encircling Sea beyond which is the Void). Five diagrams drawn by Tolkien himself. Appears in *The Shaping of Middle-earth* (HoME Vol. 4). Unique among the legendarium's documents as a visual-cosmological working document.

> [!definition] **[[Quenta Noldorinwa]]** (Quenya: "History of the Noldor")
> A compressed prose mythology of the First Age, written approximately 1930, representing the first version of the *Silmarillion* narrative to use the elevated, chronicle-like prose style that characterizes the published volume. Shorter and less polished than the final *Silmarillion*, but immediately recognizable as its direct ancestor in style and structure. Appears in *The Shaping of Middle-earth* (HoME Vol. 4).

> [!definition] **[[Annals of Valinor]]** and **[[Annals of Beleriand]]**
> The first systematic chronological frameworks for the pre-First Age and First Age history of the legendarium, written in the format of medieval ecclesiastical annals: brief entries dated by year, recording events in laconic documentary style. The Annals create the impression of *historical record* for the mythology, contributing to the sense of textual tradition that Tolkien regarded as essential to convincing subcreation. Later developed into the more elaborate Grey Annals (HoME Vol. 11). Appear in *The Shaping of Middle-earth* (HoME Vol. 4).

> [!definition] **[[Octosyllabic Couplets]]** (also "rhyming octosyllables" or "rhyming tetrameters")
> Lines of eight syllables, typically iambic, organized in rhyming pairs. The dominant meter of medieval French and English romance — used by Chaucer in the *House of Fame* and throughout the medieval narrative tradition Tolkien drew on for the *Lay of Leithian*. Produces a rapid, forward-moving rhythm suited to narrative, with rhymes that create both formal pleasure and inevitability at moments of climax. Historically associated with the chivalric romance tradition.

> [!definition] **[[Alliterative Verse]]** (Old English: *fornyrðislag*, the "meter of ancient words")
> The metrical system of Anglo-Saxon and Middle English heroic poetry, including *Beowulf*, *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*, and *Pearl*. Each full line divides into two half-lines, each containing two primary stresses; at least one stressed syllable in the first half-line alliterates (begins with the same consonant or vowel sound) with the first stressed syllable of the second half-line. Tolkien was a professional scholar of this tradition and used it for the *Lay of the Children of Húrin*, the verse *Fall of Arthur*, and other creative works. The form carries connotations of Northern heroism, gravity, and doom.

> [!definition] **[[Ilmen]]** (Quenya: from *ilm-*, related to "gleam, whiteness")
> In the *Ambarkanta*'s cosmological scheme, the second layer of the world's atmosphere above [[Vista]] — the pure, rarefied air through which the stars move, accessible to the [[Maiar]] and the greatest Elven spirits but not to mortals in their normal condition. When the Sun and Moon were created, they were set to travel through Ilmen. The distinction between Vista (mortal air) and Ilmen (sacred air) is a cosmological encoding of the ontological hierarchy between mortal and immortal kinds of being.

> [!definition] **[[Vaiya]]** (Quenya: related to *vaita*, "to enfold, wrap")
> In the *Ambarkanta*, the outermost sphere of the created world — the "Outer Sea" or "Wrapping Air" that surrounds Arda on all sides (above, below, and around). Not physically accessible by mortal means. The ships of the Elves who are permitted to return to Valinor sail through Vaiya on the Straight Road after the Change of the World. Vaiya is the cosmological boundary between Arda and the Void outside Eä.

> [!definition] **[[Sketch of the Mythology]]** (sometimes "Sketch")
> The first compressed prose synopsis of the entire legendarium, written approximately 1926, which inaugurated the shift from the elaborate frame-narrative of the *Book of Lost Tales* to the compressed mythological prose of the *Silmarillion* tradition. Approximately 30 pages covering material that took hundreds of pages in the Lost Tales. The Sketch demonstrates that the mythology could stand without the Eriol/Ælfwine frame — that it had sufficient authority on its own terms to be presented as direct mythological narrative. Appears in *The Shaping of Middle-earth* (HoME Vol. 4).

> [!definition] **[[Ekkaia]]** (Quenya: "the Encircling Sea")
> Also called the Outer Sea — in the *Ambarkanta*'s cosmological scheme, the absolute boundary of the created world: the outermost barrier of [[Vaiya]], beyond which lies the [[Void]] (the Timeless Halls, the formless exterior of Eä). Not truly a sea in any physical sense but the boundary condition of existence. The [[Walls of Night]] encircle the Ekkaia. The cosmological role of the Ekkaia is to make Arda a finite, bounded world within an infinite formlessness — a theological statement about the nature of created reality.
