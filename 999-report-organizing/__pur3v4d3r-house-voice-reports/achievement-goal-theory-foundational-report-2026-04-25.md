# Achievement Goal Theory: The Architecture of Why Learners Pursue Competence

## Abstract

Achievement Goal Theory is one of the most consequential frameworks in modern motivation science, and its consequence rests not on the obvious observation that learners pursue different aims but on the far more interesting claim that the *purpose* a learner adopts in an achievement situation organizes nearly everything that follows — the strategies they deploy, the emotions they generate, the attributions they make, the difficulties they seek or avoid, and the trajectory of their competence development across months and years. This report traces the theory from its originating insight in the developmental work of John Nicholls and the social-cognitive research of Carol Dweck, through the bifurcation that established mastery and performance goals as qualitatively distinct orientations toward competence, through Andrew Elliot's approach-avoidance refinement that doubled the conceptual space, through the 2×2 framework that has become the dominant operationalization, and into the climates and contexts that shape which goals learners are likely to adopt in the first place. The argument has three load-bearing threads. The first is that achievement goals are not preferences for outcomes but conceptions of what competence *is* — a claim that distinguishes the framework from goal-setting traditions that take the meaning of success as given. The second is that the mastery-performance distinction has proven analytically robust across three decades because it tracks a genuine architectural difference in how the cognitive system handles errors, effort, and other people's performance. The third is that the framework's most important contribution to educational practice is not its taxonomy of goals but its account of the perceived classroom environments — the [[mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate]] distinction — that systematically channel learners toward one orientation or the other. The report closes by examining the framework's tensions with adjacent theories, the productive disagreements at its current frontier, and the cross-domain transfer of its central insight to expertise development, organizational learning, and the design of personal knowledge environments.

> [!schema-activation] **Activating Prior Knowledge — A Bridge Into the Report**
> Before reading further, consider what you already know from related territory in the PKB. You have likely encountered [[motivation-science]] as the broader landscape, [[goal-setting-theory]] as the cousin tradition focused on goal *content* and *difficulty*, and [[self-determination-theory]] as the rival tradition focused on the experiential quality of motivation rather than its directional aim. You have probably also met [[implicit-theory-of-intelligence]] — the [[fixed-mindset]] versus [[growth-mindset]] distinction — which Carol Dweck developed in deep conversation with the achievement goal tradition and which functions as both an antecedent and a sibling construct to the goals discussed here.
>
> The reader who arrives with these frameworks already in place will find that Achievement Goal Theory occupies a distinctive position among them. It does not ask, as goal-setting theory does, *how specific or difficult* the target should be; it asks instead *what kind of competence* the learner is pursuing. It does not ask, as self-determination theory does, *how autonomous or controlled* the regulation feels; it asks instead *against what standard* the learner is evaluating success. And it does not ask, as attribution theory does, *how the learner explains an outcome after the fact*; it asks instead *what the learner was trying to accomplish in the first place*, on the wager that the going-in purpose powerfully shapes the explanation that arrives at the end.
>
> **A guiding question to hold throughout:** *Why has the simple distinction between "trying to learn" and "trying to look smart" generated thirty years of productive theoretical refinement, and what does the answer reveal about the architecture of human achievement motivation?*

## 1. Foundational Definitions and the Core Question

What makes Achievement Goal Theory worth treating as a foundational framework rather than as one more taxonomy of motivational tendencies is the particular question it places at the center of investigation — a question that earlier traditions in the psychology of motivation either took for granted or treated as already settled. The question is not whether learners are motivated, nor how strongly, nor with what expected probability of success; it is, instead, *what learners take success itself to mean* in the moment they engage with an achievement task. When one frames the question this way, the theoretical landscape reorganizes around a single insight that the founders of the framework recognized in the late 1970s and early 1980s: two learners can pursue the same observable activity with the same energy and the same apparent commitment, and yet be doing entirely different things internally because the standards by which they would judge themselves successful are not the same.

> [!definition] **Achievement Goal**
> An [[achievement-goal]] is a cognitive-affective representation of *the purpose for engaging in a competence-relevant activity*, where that purpose specifies the standard against which the actor will evaluate their own performance and the meaning that will be assigned to outcomes. It is not a target outcome (which would be a goal in the goal-setting sense), nor a desired affective state (which would be an aim in the well-being sense); it is the *interpretive frame* through which the actor renders the situation evaluatively legible.
>
> **Boundary:** An achievement goal is not the same as an intention, a wish, or a value, although it interacts with all three. Intentions specify *what* one will do; achievement goals specify *what would count as having done it well*. A learner can hold a perfectly clear intention to study for a chemistry exam while remaining ambiguous about whether success means understanding the material deeply (mastery) or outperforming peers (performance) — and which of those two interpretive frames is dominant will reorganize nearly everything else about the studying.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This definition is the conceptual hinge on which the entire framework turns; if achievement goals are nothing more than outcome targets, then the framework reduces to a special case of goal-setting theory and the thirty years of differentiated empirical findings become difficult to explain.
>
> **See also:** [[goal-orientation]], [[goal-setting]], [[motivation-science]], [[goal-orientation-framework]]

This way of framing the construct has a consequence that becomes visible only when one traces it carefully. Because the goal is an interpretive frame rather than a target outcome, the same external behavior can be governed by different goals at different moments, and the same goal can issue in different external behaviors depending on what the situation affords. A student preparing for a class presentation under a mastery frame will solicit feedback that exposes weaknesses, will rehearse the parts that feel shakiest, and will treat a difficult question from the audience as an opportunity to refine understanding; the same student under a performance frame will avoid feedback that might be discouraging, will rehearse the parts that already look polished, and will treat a difficult question as a threat to be deflected. The behaviors look superficially similar — both students are preparing, both are rehearsing, both are presenting — but the cognitive and affective machinery is operating on different objectives, with different criteria for what would count as a good outcome and therefore different selection pressures on what to attend to and what to ignore.

> [!key-claim] **The Central Thesis**
> Achievement Goal Theory's central and durable claim is that *the purpose adopted in an achievement situation organizes a coherent set of cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses*, such that knowing a learner's goal allows one to predict not only what they will do but also how they will feel, what they will attend to, what they will remember, what they will attribute outcomes to, and what they will choose to do next. This claim — what one might call the [[the-conceptual-pivot-of-the-theory|conceptual pivot of the theory]] — is what justifies treating goals as architectural rather than merely descriptive features of motivation.

To see why this thesis is non-trivial, contrast it with two adjacent claims that it might be confused with but is not. The first is the goal-setting claim, associated with Locke and Latham, that *specific and difficult goals produce higher performance than vague or easy goals* — a thoroughly validated proposition but one that takes the meaning of "performance" as an unproblematic given and concerns itself with target characteristics rather than purpose structure. Achievement Goal Theory does not contradict this finding; it operates underneath it, asking what the goal *means to the learner* once it has been adopted. The second is the expectancy-value claim, associated with [[expectancy-value-theory]], that *motivation is the product of expected probability of success and the value attached to success* — a powerful framework that, again, takes the meaning of "value" as already specified and asks how its magnitude shapes choice. Achievement Goal Theory operates underneath this as well, asking what *kind* of value the learner is pursuing in the first place. Neither comparison reduces to an opposition; the frameworks coexist productively in contemporary motivation science. But the comparison clarifies what is distinctive about the goals tradition: it places the *interpretive purpose* of the activity at the center of the explanatory architecture.

> [!claude-insight] **Claude's Perspective on Why This Question Took So Long to Surface**
> One of the historically interesting features of Achievement Goal Theory is how late it arrived in the psychology of motivation, given how natural the question seems in retrospect. Behaviorist traditions had no use for it because internal interpretive frames were methodologically off-limits. Drive-reduction theories dissolved the question into homeostatic mechanisms. Even early cognitive theories of motivation, focused on expectancies and incentive values, treated the meaning of an outcome as something the experimenter could specify rather than something the learner constructed. What changed in the late 1970s was the convergence of three currents: developmental research showing that children's *understanding of ability* shifted across childhood, attribution research showing that learners constructed *causal explanations* of success and failure, and educational concern about students who appeared to give up in the face of difficulty despite manifest capability. These three currents made it natural to ask not just whether students were motivated but *toward what conception of competence* their motivation was directed — and the answer to that question turned out to be far more consequential than anyone had anticipated.

The methodological commitment that follows from this conceptual stance is also worth noting at the outset. Because achievement goals are interpretive frames rather than observable behaviors, they cannot be measured by watching what learners do; they must be inferred either from self-report instruments that ask learners how they think about achievement situations, or from experimental manipulations that prime particular framings, or from the situational structures (climates) that make particular goals more or less salient. The dominant measurement tradition has been self-report, with instruments such as the [[motivated-strategies-for-learning-questionnaire-mslq]], the Patterns of Adaptive Learning Survey developed by Midgley and colleagues, and the Achievement Goal Questionnaire developed by Elliot and McGregor. Each of these instruments encodes specific theoretical commitments about how many goals exist, how they should be distinguished, and what items most validly capture them — which means that decisions made in the construction of measurement tools have, throughout the history of the field, been simultaneously decisions about what the theory itself claims. We will return to this entanglement in the section on tensions and critiques.

> [!section-summary] **Section 1 — Key Takeaways**
> Achievement Goal Theory begins with a question that earlier motivational frameworks did not foreground: *what does the learner take success itself to mean?* An achievement goal is the interpretive frame through which a competence-relevant activity becomes evaluatively legible — neither a target outcome nor a desired feeling, but the standard by which the actor will judge their own performance. The framework's central thesis is that this interpretive purpose organizes a coherent constellation of cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses, which is why knowing a learner's goal allows broad prediction across multiple downstream variables. The construct must be carefully distinguished from goal-setting theory, which concerns target characteristics, and from expectancy-value theory, which concerns the magnitude rather than the kind of value pursued.

> [!reflection] **Reflective Questions for Section 1**
> 1. Consider a recent learning situation in your own life. Was the dominant interpretive frame oriented toward developing competence, demonstrating competence, or avoiding evidence of incompetence? How did this frame shape what you attended to and what you avoided?
> 2. The framework claims that goals are not behaviors but interpretive frames. What methodological consequences does this entail, and what risks does it create for the validity of self-report measurement?
> 3. If two students prepare identically for an exam but hold different achievement goals, in what specific moment would the difference become visible to an outside observer?

> [!situation-model] **Situation Model — Updated Through Section 1**
> **Key Entities:** Learner; achievement situation; achievement goal (interpretive frame); standard of evaluation; downstream cognitive-affective-behavioral responses.
> **Causal Map:** Achievement situation → adoption of an achievement goal (the interpretive frame) → standard of evaluation activated → cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses organized to track that standard.
> **Structural Overview:** The framework is positioned underneath goal-setting theory and expectancy-value theory, asking what success *means* to the learner before asking how specific the target is or how much it is valued.
> **Evolution This Section:** Established the foundational definition; distinguished achievement goals from intentions, target outcomes, and value magnitudes; introduced the methodological consequence that goals must be inferred rather than observed.

## 2. The Originating Insight: The Developmental Differentiation of Ability Conceptions

The conceptual machinery introduced in the previous section requires an antecedent that the framework itself did not initially supply but that John Nicholls developed in parallel and that became, in the hands of subsequent theorists, the deep ground on which the entire mastery-performance distinction rests. The antecedent is a developmental observation about how children's understanding of *ability itself* changes between roughly ages five and twelve — an observation that, when one traces its consequences carefully, turns out to explain why the mastery-performance bifurcation is not an arbitrary taxonomic choice but a reflection of two genuinely different cognitive architectures that the human mind is capable of running.

> [!definition] **Conception of Ability (Nicholls)**
> A [[conception-of-ability]] is the implicit theory a person holds about *what ability is* and *how it can be detected*. Two distinct conceptions are available to mature reasoners. Under the **undifferentiated** or **task-involved** conception, ability is essentially indistinguishable from learning and effort: to become more competent is to demonstrate more ability, and effort is the visible expression of ability rather than its compensation. Under the **differentiated** or **ego-involved** conception, ability is conceptualized as a *capacity* distinct from effort, such that demonstrating high ability requires high performance achieved with *low* effort, and the same performance achieved through high effort signals lower underlying capacity.
>
> **Boundary:** The two conceptions are not the same as the [[fixed-mindset]]/[[growth-mindset]] distinction, although they are closely related and often co-active. Mindsets concern beliefs about *malleability* of ability; conceptions of ability concern the *structural relationship* between ability and effort. A learner can hold a growth mindset (ability is malleable) while still operating under a differentiated conception in a given moment (effort signals lower current capacity), which is precisely why the two frameworks have remained productively distinct.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This distinction is the developmental and conceptual taproot of the entire mastery-performance bifurcation. Without it, the bifurcation looks like a simple preference for different outcomes; with it, the bifurcation reveals itself as a deep difference in *what evidence the cognitive system is treating as diagnostic of competence*.
>
> **See also:** [[the-developmental-differentiation-of-effort-and-ability]], [[ego-involvement]], [[implicit-theory-of-intelligence]], [[entity-theory-of-intelligence]], [[incremental-theory-of-intelligence]]

The developmental work that supports this distinction is itself worth tracing because it shows where the cognitive architecture of achievement motivation actually comes from rather than treating that architecture as a brute fact about adult learners. Nicholls' studies, conducted across the 1970s and 1980s and extended in collaboration with Susan Miller and Terri Patashnick, presented children of different ages with scenarios in which two characters performed equally well on a task but exerted different amounts of effort, and asked the children which character had more ability. Younger children (typically before age seven) treated ability and effort as equivalent or saw effort as causing ability; somewhat older children (roughly seven to nine) recognized that effort and ability could each contribute to outcomes but treated their relationship inconsistently; older children (typically eleven and beyond) reliably recognized the *compensatory* relationship — that equal performance achieved with unequal effort implies unequal underlying capacity, with the lower-effort actor inferred to have higher ability. The progression unfolds across years of cognitive development, but its endpoint matters more than its timing for present purposes: by adolescence, learners have available *both* conceptions of ability and can shift between them depending on situational cues and personal disposition.

> [!key-claim] **Why the Two Conceptions Matter**
> The availability of two distinct conceptions of ability creates the architectural conditions under which two distinct achievement goals become possible. A learner operating under the *undifferentiated* conception has no way to construct a performance goal in the technical sense, because the question "did I perform better than others while expending less effort?" is not a question their cognitive architecture can ask; success simply consists of having learned more than before. A learner operating under the *differentiated* conception, by contrast, has access to a question that the undifferentiated conception cannot pose: "did my performance signal high or low underlying capacity?" — and that question, once available, can become the dominant organizing principle of the entire engagement.

What this means for the achievement goal framework is more consequential than it might first appear. The mastery goal is not an alternative to the performance goal in the sense that a strawberry is an alternative to a pear — two equally legitimate options on a flat menu. It is, instead, a goal that *operates within* the undifferentiated conception of ability, while the performance goal operates within the differentiated conception, with the result that the two goals are recruiting two architecturally different ways of thinking about what competence even is. Success under a mastery goal is success-as-improvement: the relevant comparison is the learner's prior state, the relevant evidence is observable change, and effort is the visible mechanism by which ability is being constructed. Success under a performance goal is success-as-demonstration: the relevant comparison is other people's performance (or normative standards that stand in for other people), the relevant evidence is the inference about underlying capacity that observers will draw, and effort is a problematic signal because high effort under a differentiated conception suggests lower underlying capacity rather than greater commitment.

> [!claude-insight] **The Hidden Architecture That Makes the Bifurcation Real**
> When one examines why the mastery-performance distinction has proven so empirically productive across thirty years and dozens of laboratories, the answer that emerges from a careful reading of Nicholls' developmental work is that the distinction tracks a real architectural difference in how the human cognitive system can handle competence-relevant information. Under the undifferentiated conception, errors are diagnostic of *what to learn next* and are therefore informationally valuable; under the differentiated conception, errors are diagnostic of *underlying capacity* and are therefore threatening. Under the undifferentiated conception, effort is the operating mechanism of competence development and is therefore something to be deployed liberally; under the differentiated conception, effort is a signal that observers can use to make ability inferences, which means that high effort visible to others becomes evidence one might prefer to suppress. The reason a single underlying construct — the conception of ability — can produce two such different patterns of cognition, affect, and behavior is that the construct specifies *what counts as evidence* for the cognitive system, and changing the evidence-criteria changes everything that follows.

This architectural framing also explains a finding that was puzzling in the early years of the framework but becomes intelligible once the conception-of-ability ground is in place: students operating under performance goals frequently disengage from challenging tasks even when they are perfectly capable of succeeding, while students operating under mastery goals frequently *seek* challenging tasks even when they expect to fail. The behavior looks paradoxical only if one assumes that both students share the same evaluative architecture and are simply rolling different cost-benefit calculations against it. Once one recognizes that performance-goal students are running an architecture in which difficulty is a threat to ability inferences and mastery-goal students are running an architecture in which difficulty is the very substrate of competence development, the apparent paradox dissolves into the predictable behavior of two different cognitive systems pursuing two different evidentiary criteria. The choice to take on a hard problem feels different — really, *is* different — depending on which conception of ability is currently active.

> [!warning] **A Common Misconception About Conceptions of Ability**
> A frequent misreading of Nicholls' work treats the undifferentiated conception as developmentally inferior — a childlike confusion that mature thinkers should leave behind. This misreading inverts what the framework actually claims. Mature learners possess *both* conceptions and can deploy either one; the question is which conception is operative in a given situation, and the empirical evidence consistently favors the *undifferentiated* (task-involved) conception as the more adaptive choice in most learning contexts. The developmental story is not a story of progress from inferior to superior but a story of *expansion of available frames*, with mature learners gaining access to a differentiated frame that is sometimes useful (in genuinely competitive evaluative contexts) but frequently corrosive when imported into contexts where learning rather than evaluation is the actual stake.

> [!section-summary] **Section 2 — Key Takeaways**
> Achievement Goal Theory's mastery-performance bifurcation rests on a developmental observation about ability conceptions — specifically, that mature learners have access to both an undifferentiated (task-involved) conception, in which ability and effort are not sharply distinguished, and a differentiated (ego-involved) conception, in which ability is a capacity distinct from effort and signaled by performance achieved with low effort. The mastery goal operates within the undifferentiated conception; the performance goal operates within the differentiated conception. This is not a difference of preference but of *evidentiary architecture*: the two goals treat different things as diagnostic of competence, which is why they organize different patterns of attention, emotion, and behavior. The seemingly paradoxical behaviors of the two orientations — performance-goal disengagement from difficulty, mastery-goal pursuit of difficulty — become intelligible once one sees that they are running on different architectures, not different settings of the same architecture.

> [!reflection] **Reflective Questions for Section 2**
> 1. Consider a moment when you noticed yourself reluctant to expend visible effort on a task. Was that reluctance better explained by the cost of the effort itself, or by a concern that visible effort would be read as a signal of low underlying ability?
> 2. If both conceptions of ability are available to mature learners, what situational features tend to make the differentiated conception salient, and what features tend to make the undifferentiated conception salient?
> 3. The framework claims that errors are *diagnostic* under the undifferentiated conception and *threatening* under the differentiated conception. What practical implications follow for how feedback should be designed if a teacher wants to keep students operating in the undifferentiated frame?

> [!situation-model] **Situation Model — Updated Through Section 2**
> **Key Entities:** All from §1, plus — Conception of ability (undifferentiated/task-involved vs differentiated/ego-involved); developmental progression in childhood; effort-ability relationship.
> **Causal Map:** Developmental availability of two ability conceptions → conception activated in a situation → evidentiary criteria for "what counts as competence" set → achievement goal made possible (mastery requires undifferentiated; performance requires differentiated) → downstream cognitive-affective-behavioral responses.
> **Structural Overview:** The mastery-performance distinction rests on a deeper structural distinction in how the cognitive system can construct ability evidence. The two goals are not flat alternatives but goals operating within different evidentiary architectures.

## 3. The Dichotomous Architecture: Mastery and Performance Goals

The first generation of Achievement Goal Theory, developed across the 1980s by John Nicholls, Carol Dweck, Carole Ames, and Martin Maehr — working in distinct laboratories that converged with surprising rapidity on a common conceptual structure — formalized the bifurcation introduced in the previous section into the dichotomous model that became the framework's first stable expression. The model is simple to state but, when one traces what it actually claims, considerably more consequential than its simplicity suggests.

> [!definition] **Mastery Goal Orientation**
> A [[mastery-goal-orientation]] is the disposition to define competence in terms of *task-referenced or self-referenced standards* — that is, in terms of whether one has understood the material, mastered the skill, or improved relative to one's prior level of performance. Synonyms in the literature include *learning goal* (Dweck), *task-involved goal* (Nicholls), and simply *task goal* (Ames). The standard of evaluation is intrinsic to the activity itself: success is success because the activity has been done well, the skill genuinely acquired, the understanding genuinely deepened.
>
> **Boundary:** A mastery goal is not a synonym for "intrinsic motivation" in the [[self-determination-theory]] sense, although the two are correlated. Intrinsic motivation concerns whether an activity is engaged in for its own sake; a mastery goal concerns the standard by which the engagement is being evaluated. A learner can hold a mastery goal toward an activity they find tedious (because they need to acquire the skill) and an intrinsic motivation toward an activity they evaluate by performance standards (because the comparison itself is enjoyable). The constructs are kin, not identical.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is one of the two foundational categories from which all subsequent refinements have proceeded; understanding it precisely is prerequisite to understanding the 2×2 framework, the goal climate literature, and the connections to mindset and self-regulation.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-goal-orientation]], [[mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation]], [[mastery-vs-performance-goals]], [[performance-goal-vs-learning-goal]]

> [!definition] **Performance Goal Orientation**
> A [[performance-goal-orientation]] is the disposition to define competence in terms of *normative or other-referenced standards* — that is, in terms of whether one has performed better (or at least no worse) than relevant comparison others, or whether one has met an external standard that signals favorable ability. Synonyms include *ego-involved goal* (Nicholls), *ability goal* (Ames), and *judgment goal* in some early formulations. The standard of evaluation is extrinsic to the task content: success is success because the comparison has been favorably resolved or the public judgment has been favorably secured.
>
> **Boundary:** A performance goal is not the same as a desire to do well, nor a synonym for ambition or drive. A learner can pursue excellence under a mastery goal (defining excellence as deep mastery of the material) or under a performance goal (defining excellence as outranking peers). The two are differentiated not by intensity of pursuit but by what counts as the criterion against which excellence is measured.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Together with the mastery goal, this category constitutes the foundational dichotomy from which the framework proceeds; the entire empirical literature on achievement goal consequences turns on the differential predictions these two categories generate.
>
> **See also:** [[performance-goal-orientation]], [[ego-involvement]], [[mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation]], [[social-comparison-theory]]

The substantive payoff of the dichotomy is the differential prediction it generates across nearly every major outcome variable that motivation researchers care about — and the consistency of these predictions across thirty years of empirical work is what gives the framework its scientific traction. When one traces the comparison carefully, the pattern that emerges is what Carol Ames in her 1992 review called the *mastery advantage*: across cognitive, affective, and behavioral indicators, mastery goals are associated with adaptive outcomes while performance goals are associated, in the dichotomous model, with a more mixed and frequently maladaptive pattern. Mastery-goal learners use deeper processing strategies, persist longer in the face of difficulty, prefer challenging tasks, attribute outcomes to controllable factors such as effort and strategy, experience higher intrinsic interest, exhibit better emotional regulation in the face of setbacks, and demonstrate stronger long-term retention of what they have learned. Performance-goal learners, in the dichotomous model, show the opposite tendencies on most of these dimensions: shallower processing, shorter persistence, preference for tasks that allow safe demonstration of competence, attribution of failure to uncontrollable factors such as fixed ability, lower intrinsic interest, more disruptive negative affect under setback, and weaker long-term retention.

> [!key-claim] **The Mastery Advantage Pattern**
> The dichotomous model's central empirical claim — sometimes called the *mastery advantage* or *mastery hypothesis* — is that mastery goals produce a coherent constellation of adaptive learning outcomes that performance goals fail to produce or actively undermine. This claim is not merely a description of average effects; it is a claim about the *systematic coupling* between an interpretive frame and the cognitive-affective-behavioral architecture that frame organizes. The coherence of the pattern is itself the most important finding, because coherence is what justifies treating the goal as an architectural rather than a descriptive variable.

> [!example] **The Mastery Advantage in a Single Vignette**
> Consider two students, Maya and Chen, both preparing for a calculus midterm. Both score 78 on the exam. When asked what they will do differently next time, Maya (mastery-oriented) reviews her exam carefully, identifies that she misunderstood the chain rule, asks the instructor for clarification, and adjusts her study strategy to include more conceptual practice problems for the next unit. Chen (performance-oriented) avoids looking at the exam, focuses on whether 78 was above or below the class median, and decides either to study harder for the next exam (if the score was below median) or to coast (if it was above). Both students received identical information; the difference in what they *do* with that information is the difference between an interpretive frame that treats the exam as diagnostic of what to learn next and an interpretive frame that treats the exam as diagnostic of comparative standing.

But the dichotomous model also generated a complication that its founders did not initially recognize and that became, in the early 1990s, the source of considerable empirical tension. The complication was that performance goals did not always produce maladaptive outcomes; in certain contexts, particularly when learners had high prior competence and the evaluative environment was supportive, performance goals were associated with strong achievement, high effort, and even occasionally with deep processing. These findings were difficult to reconcile with the dichotomous model's claim that performance goals were globally maladaptive, and they generated a wave of theoretical work in the mid-1990s asking whether the performance goal category was perhaps too coarse — whether, in particular, it was conflating two genuinely distinct orientations that happened to share a comparative frame of reference but that pulled in different motivational directions. The answer that emerged from this work, primarily in Andrew Elliot's laboratory, was that the dichotomous model needed to be refined along a second axis — the axis of approach-avoidance valence — and that doing so would resolve much of the empirical inconsistency that had accumulated.

> [!claude-insight] **Why the Dichotomous Model Was Right About the Architecture but Coarse About the Goals**
> Reading the dichotomous-model literature in light of what came later, what one notices is that the founders had correctly identified the *architectural distinction* (undifferentiated vs differentiated conceptions of ability, mastery vs performance evaluative standards) but had not yet recognized that the differentiated conception itself splits into two motivationally different operating modes — one in which the learner approaches the goal of demonstrating high ability and one in which the learner approaches the goal of avoiding the demonstration of low ability. The empirical anomalies that troubled the dichotomous model were not anomalies in the architecture but in the *resolution* at which the architecture had been described. This is a recurring pattern in the history of psychological theory: the original distinction is correct in spirit but coarse in execution, and refinement consists not in abandoning the original but in subdividing its categories along a second dimension that the original framework had implicitly held constant.

> [!section-summary] **Section 3 — Key Takeaways**
> The dichotomous model formalized the mastery-performance bifurcation as the framework's first stable expression. Mastery goals define competence in task- or self-referenced terms; performance goals define competence in normative or other-referenced terms. The empirical work of the 1980s and early 1990s generated the *mastery advantage* finding — that mastery goals were associated with a coherent constellation of adaptive cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes that performance goals failed to produce or actively undermined. But the dichotomous model also generated empirical anomalies that it could not easily explain — particularly cases in which performance goals were associated with strong achievement and effort — and these anomalies pointed toward the need for a refinement along a second axis: the axis of approach-avoidance valence.

## 4. The Approach-Avoidance Refinement: Doubling the Conceptual Space

The most consequential refinement of the dichotomous model came from Andrew Elliot's laboratory in the mid-1990s and is best understood as the recognition that the *direction* of motivation — toward a positive possibility versus away from a negative possibility — is a dimension that the dichotomous model had implicitly held constant for both goal types but that could in fact vary independently within each type. The result was the *trichotomous model* (mastery, performance-approach, performance-avoidance) introduced in Elliot's 1997 work and refined into the *2×2 model* (mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, performance-avoidance) in Elliot and McGregor's 2001 paper, which has since become the dominant operationalization of Achievement Goal Theory in contemporary research.

> [!definition] **Approach-Avoidance Valence (in Achievement Motivation)**
> [[approach-avoidance-motivation]] is the dimension along which a goal can be characterized as oriented toward the *attainment of a positive possibility* (approach) or toward *avoidance of a negative possibility* (avoidance). The distinction is not a matter of whether the actor wants the outcome — both approach and avoidance goals are wanted — but of whether the cognitive-affective machinery is *pulled toward the desired endpoint* or *pushed from the feared endpoint*, with the difference producing systematically different patterns of attention, emotion, and persistence.
>
> **Boundary:** Approach-avoidance is not the same as success-failure orientation. An approach goal can fail (the desired positive does not materialize), and an avoidance goal can succeed (the feared negative does not materialize). The distinction concerns the *anchor point of motivation*, not the actual outcome.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This dimension, when crossed with the mastery-performance distinction, produces the 2×2 framework that organizes the contemporary research literature and that has resolved most of the empirical anomalies that troubled the dichotomous model.
>
> **See also:** [[approach-avoidance-motivation]], [[regulatory-focus-theory]], [[performance-approach-goal]], [[performance-avoidance-goal]]

The empirical motivation for introducing this distinction was the observation, accumulated across the early 1990s, that the performance goal category was producing inconsistent results across studies — sometimes associated with strong achievement and effort, sometimes with shallow processing and disengagement. Elliot's hypothesis, vindicated by a series of careful studies in the late 1990s, was that the inconsistency reflected an unrecognized heterogeneity within the performance goal category: some learners coded as "performance-oriented" were oriented toward *demonstrating high ability* relative to others, while other learners coded as "performance-oriented" were oriented toward *avoiding the demonstration of low ability* relative to others. These two orientations share a common feature — both define competence in normative-comparative terms — but they pull in motivationally different directions, with the first generating high effort, sustained engagement, and willingness to compete openly, and the second generating self-handicapping, withdrawal from competition, and shallow processing as a way of preserving the option to attribute failure to insufficient effort rather than insufficient ability.

> [!definition] **Performance-Approach Goal**
> A [[performance-approach-goal]] is an achievement goal in which the learner is oriented toward *demonstrating high ability relative to others* (or relative to a normative standard). The standard of evaluation is comparative; the directional anchor is the desired positive possibility of favorable comparison; the cognitive-affective machinery is pulled toward the prospect of being seen (by self or others) as more capable than relevant peers.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** Self-report items such as "It is important to me to do better than other students," "I want to do well compared to others in this class," and "My goal is to get a better grade than most of the students."
>
> **See also:** [[performance-approach-goal]], [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]]

> [!definition] **Performance-Avoidance Goal**
> A [[performance-avoidance-goal]] is an achievement goal in which the learner is oriented toward *avoiding the demonstration of low ability relative to others*. The standard of evaluation is comparative; the directional anchor is the feared negative possibility of unfavorable comparison; the cognitive-affective machinery is pushed away from the prospect of being seen as less capable than relevant peers.
>
> **Operational Indicator:** Self-report items such as "I just want to avoid doing poorly compared to others," "My goal is to avoid performing poorly compared to others," and "I am often concerned that I may not learn all that there is to learn in this class."
>
> **Boundary:** The performance-avoidance goal is not a weak or low-magnitude version of the performance-approach goal; it is a qualitatively different orientation with its own characteristic pattern of antecedents, processes, and consequences. The two share only the comparative evaluative standard.
>
> **See also:** [[performance-avoidance-goal]], [[self-handicapping]], [[learned-helplessness]]

> [!key-claim] **The Asymmetric Pattern Within the Performance Goal Category**
> The introduction of the approach-avoidance distinction within the performance goal category resolved most of the empirical anomalies that had troubled the dichotomous model. Performance-approach goals are associated with high effort, strong achievement (especially in evaluative contexts), and a generally adaptive — though more brittle — pattern of engagement; performance-avoidance goals are associated with self-handicapping, disengagement, shallow processing, anxiety, and the consistently maladaptive pattern that the dichotomous model had attributed to performance goals as a whole. The performance goal category, in other words, was hiding two motivationally different goals that the dichotomous model had been averaging together with predictably noisy results.

The refinement also opened a question that the trichotomous model could not fully address but that the subsequent 2×2 model made unavoidable: if approach and avoidance constitute a meaningful dimension within the performance goal category, do they also constitute a meaningful dimension within the *mastery* goal category? The answer, developed by Elliot and McGregor in 2001, is yes — although the mastery-avoidance category proved harder to construct convincingly than the performance-avoidance category and remains the most contested cell of the 2×2 framework even today. We will examine this complication in the next section, where the full 2×2 architecture is introduced. For present purposes, the key point is that the approach-avoidance refinement transformed the framework from a flat dichotomy into a multi-dimensional space in which goals can be located by their evaluative standard (mastery vs performance) and their directional anchor (approach vs avoidance), with the four resulting cells exhibiting distinct patterns that the dichotomous model could only describe by averaging across them.

> [!warning] **The Asymmetry That the 2×2 Framework Inherits**
> One subtlety that becomes important in subsequent sections is that the four cells of the 2×2 framework are not symmetrical in their empirical robustness. Performance-approach, performance-avoidance, and mastery-approach are all well-attested in the empirical literature with stable measurement instruments and consistent predictive validity. Mastery-avoidance, by contrast, has proven harder to operationalize and harder to distinguish empirically from related constructs, and a substantial fraction of the contemporary debate about the framework's structure concerns whether mastery-avoidance is a genuine fourth category or a methodological artifact of the symmetry assumption that motivated its introduction.

> [!section-summary] **Section 4 — Key Takeaways**
> The approach-avoidance refinement, introduced by Andrew Elliot in the mid-1990s, doubled the framework's conceptual space by recognizing that the directional anchor of motivation — toward a positive possibility or away from a negative possibility — varies independently of the evaluative standard. Within the performance goal category, the refinement distinguished performance-approach (oriented toward demonstrating high ability) from performance-avoidance (oriented toward avoiding the demonstration of low ability), and this distinction resolved most of the empirical anomalies that had troubled the dichotomous model: performance-approach goals show a generally adaptive pattern, while performance-avoidance goals show the consistently maladaptive pattern previously attributed to performance goals as a whole. The refinement also raised the question of whether mastery goals admit the same approach-avoidance subdivision — a question taken up by the 2×2 framework discussed in the next section.

> 2. Why does the distinction between approach and avoidance produce different cognitive-affective architectures even when the evaluative standard is the same? Trace the mechanism: how does directional anchor reshape attention, emotion, and behavior?
> 3. If performance-avoidance goals are reliably maladaptive, why do learners adopt them at all? What does the persistence of this orientation suggest about the antecedent conditions that make it functionally rational from the learner's standpoint?

## 5. The 2×2 Framework and Its Extensions

The 2×2 achievement goal framework, formalized by Andrew Elliot and Holly McGregor in 2001, is the operationalization of Achievement Goal Theory that has dominated the empirical literature for the past two decades and that most contemporary researchers treat as the canonical structural description of the achievement goal space. Its construction is straightforward in principle: cross the evaluative standard (mastery vs performance) with the directional anchor (approach vs avoidance), and the result is a four-cell taxonomy in which every achievement goal can in principle be located. Its substantive payoff is more interesting, because the four cells turn out to occupy genuinely different positions in the motivational landscape, with distinct antecedents, distinct mediating processes, and distinct consequences for learning and well-being.

> [!definition] **The 2×2 Achievement Goal Framework**
> The [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework|2×2 achievement goal framework]] is the structural model in which achievement goals are organized along two crossed dimensions — evaluative standard (mastery: task/self-referenced; performance: normative/other-referenced) and directional anchor (approach: toward positive possibility; avoidance: away from negative possibility) — yielding four cells: mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, performance-avoidance. The framework is not a list of empirically observed goals but a *combinatorial space* derived from the crossing of two theoretically motivated dimensions, with each cell predicted to organize a distinct motivational architecture.
>
> **Boundary:** The 2×2 framework is not a claim that learners adopt only one goal at a time; multiple goal pursuit is well-documented and is the topic of an active research program on *goal complexes* and *goal profiles*. The framework specifies the structural space within which goals can be located, not the constraint that only one location can be occupied at a time.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** This is the structural model that organizes the contemporary empirical literature; familiarity with the four cells, their labels, and their characteristic patterns is prerequisite to reading current research in achievement motivation.
>
> **See also:** [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]], [[2x2-framework]], [[the-four-by-four-matrix]], [[mastery-approach-goal]], [[mastery-avoidance-goal]]

The four cells, briefly characterized:

> [!definition] **Mastery-Approach Goal**
> A [[mastery-approach-goal]] is the orientation toward *attaining task-referenced or self-referenced competence* — toward learning, understanding, mastering, improving. It combines the undifferentiated evaluative standard with the approach directional anchor and is the cell most consistently associated with the full mastery advantage pattern: deep processing, sustained engagement, willingness to seek challenge, adaptive attribution patterns, intrinsic interest, and resilient affect under setback.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-approach-goal]], [[deep-processing]], [[intrinsic-motivation]]

> [!definition] **Mastery-Avoidance Goal**
> A [[mastery-avoidance-goal]] is the orientation toward *avoiding task-referenced or self-referenced incompetence* — toward not failing to learn, not losing skills one has already acquired, not falling short of one's own prior level of mastery. It combines the undifferentiated evaluative standard with the avoidance directional anchor and is the most theoretically novel and most empirically contested cell of the framework.
>
> **Boundary:** The mastery-avoidance goal is not the same as anxiety about learning, nor is it the same as a fear of failure that happens to occur in a learning context. It is the specific orientation in which the *positive possibility* of mastery has been replaced by the *feared possibility* of falling short of one's own mastery standard, with the directional anchor reversed but the evaluative standard preserved.
>
> **Operational Examples:** Older adults concerned about cognitive decline ("I just want to not lose what I've learned"); experts concerned about skill atrophy ("I don't want to fall below my best"); perfectionists who frame learning in terms of avoiding self-disappointing performance.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-avoidance-goal]], [[the-functional-priority-of-unexpected-and-negative-outcomes]]

> [!claude-insight] **Why Mastery-Avoidance Has Proven Hard to Pin Down**
> The mastery-avoidance cell is the most theoretically interesting of the four because it is the cell that the symmetry of the 2×2 framework *requires* to exist but that empirical research has had the most difficulty operationalizing convincingly. Part of the difficulty is that the natural exemplars of the orientation — older adults worried about cognitive decline, experts worried about skill atrophy, perfectionists worried about self-disappointment — represent populations and conditions that the achievement motivation literature, traditionally focused on student samples and skill acquisition, did not historically study. Part of the difficulty is that the construct is psychologically harder to inhabit than its peers: most learners can readily report whether they want to demonstrate or avoid demonstrating ability relative to others, but reporting whether they want to attain or avoid not-attaining their own mastery standard requires a kind of self-referential reflexivity that ordinary students rarely deploy. And part of the difficulty is genuinely structural: the avoidance variant of a self-referential goal may simply be a less stable psychological configuration than its approach counterpart, more vulnerable to drift toward either mastery-approach (when the threat recedes) or performance-avoidance (when the standard becomes implicitly normative). The cell is real, in the sense that it captures motivational configurations one can clearly identify in certain populations, but it is the cell most likely to be revised or absorbed in future structural extensions of the framework.

The 2×2 framework's payoff in the empirical literature has been substantial. Each of the four cells has been associated with a distinct profile of antecedents (the situational and dispositional factors that make the goal more likely to be adopted), mediators (the cognitive, affective, and behavioral processes through which the goal exerts its effects), and consequences (the outcomes the goal produces). Mastery-approach goals are the most consistently adaptive across virtually all domains studied. Performance-approach goals are adaptive in some respects (achievement, effort) and less adaptive in others (intrinsic interest, deep processing), with the balance depending on the evaluative context. Performance-avoidance goals are consistently maladaptive across virtually all outcome variables, including achievement itself. Mastery-avoidance goals fall somewhere between mastery-approach and performance-avoidance, with most studies showing intermediate patterns that are difficult to interpret cleanly because of the operationalization difficulties noted above.

> [!example] **Mapping a Single Learner's Goal Profile**
> Consider a graduate student preparing for comprehensive exams. They might be simultaneously oriented toward (a) deepening their understanding of the field's central theoretical debates [mastery-approach], (b) avoiding the embarrassment of forgetting key authors and concepts they once knew [mastery-avoidance], (c) demonstrating to their committee that they are among the strongest students in their cohort [performance-approach], and (d) avoiding the specific failure mode of being asked a question they cannot even begin to address [performance-avoidance]. All four orientations may be active simultaneously; their relative dominance will shift across moments and contexts; and the *goal complex* — the configuration of all four — may predict outcomes better than any single goal in isolation. Contemporary research increasingly investigates these multi-goal complexes rather than treating goals as mutually exclusive categories.

The framework has also been extended in subsequent years, with two extensions worth noting briefly. The first is the **3×2 framework** developed by Elliot, Murayama, and Pekrun in 2011, which subdivides the mastery cell into *task-based* and *self-based* components — the recognition that "task-referenced" (am I performing the task well by intrinsic standards?) and "self-referenced" (am I performing better than I previously did?) are conceptually distinct and may produce somewhat different patterns. Crossing this three-category evaluative standard with the two-category directional anchor yields six cells: task-approach, task-avoidance, self-approach, self-avoidance, other-approach, other-avoidance. The 3×2 framework has accumulated empirical support but has not displaced the 2×2 as the dominant operationalization, in part because the additional resolution does not always pay off in differentiated predictions and in part because the 2×2 has thirty years of accumulated findings that the 3×2 cannot easily inherit.

The second extension is **goal complexes**, the recognition that learners often hold multiple goals simultaneously and that the *configuration* of goals matters more than any single goal. This program has produced findings about specific configurations such as the "multiple-goal hypothesis" — that simultaneously high mastery-approach and performance-approach goals can produce strong outcomes — and about goal profiles, latent classes of learners characterized by particular combinations of all four 2×2 goals. The goal complex literature is contemporary and active, and it points toward what may be the next major structural shift in the framework: away from treating goals as discrete categories that learners "have" and toward treating goals as *configural states* that learners assemble dynamically in response to specific tasks and contexts.

> [!warning] **A Note on the Literature's Vocabulary**
> Readers entering the contemporary literature should be alert to a small but persistent vocabulary problem. *Performance goal* in dichotomous-model writing usually means what later writers would call *performance-approach* (since the dichotomous model conflated the approach and avoidance variants). *Mastery goal* in dichotomous-model writing usually means *mastery-approach* for the same reason. When reading older studies, the absence of approach-avoidance language does not mean the constructs are missing — it means they have been folded into a coarser category that the study's design could not separate.

> [!section-summary] **Section 5 — Key Takeaways**
> The 2×2 framework crosses the mastery-performance evaluative standard with the approach-avoidance directional anchor to produce four cells: mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, performance-avoidance. Each cell occupies a distinct position in the motivational landscape with distinct antecedents, mediators, and consequences. Mastery-approach is most consistently adaptive; performance-avoidance is most consistently maladaptive; performance-approach and mastery-avoidance show context-dependent intermediate patterns. Subsequent extensions include the 3×2 framework (subdividing mastery into task-based and self-based components) and the goal complex literature (which examines configurations of multiple simultaneously-held goals). The framework continues to evolve, with current debate focused on whether goals should be treated as discrete categories that learners possess or as configural states that learners assemble dynamically.

## 6. Climates, Antecedents, and the TARGET Framework

A theoretical framework that specified only the structure of achievement goals would describe the motivational landscape without explaining how learners come to occupy particular positions within it — and Achievement Goal Theory's most consequential contribution to educational practice is precisely the account it offers of the situational conditions that channel learners toward one orientation or another. The account is built around the concept of the *goal climate* (sometimes called the *goal structure* or *perceived classroom goal orientation*), which is the perceived evaluative architecture of an achievement environment — the set of cues, practices, and norms through which the environment communicates what *it* takes success to mean.

> [!definition] **Goal Climate (Mastery vs Performance)**
> A [[mastery-climate]] is an evaluative environment that emphasizes effort, improvement, deep understanding, and intrinsic interest in the activity, and that communicates these emphases through grading practices, public recognition patterns, task design, and the way authority figures talk about success and failure. A *performance climate* is an evaluative environment that emphasizes normative comparison, public ranking, ability differentiation, and the demonstration of high performance, communicating these emphases through the same channels. The contrast is encoded in the wiki-link [[mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate]].
>
> **Boundary:** A goal climate is not the same as a teacher's stated philosophy. A teacher who explicitly endorses mastery values can simultaneously create a performance climate through grading on a curve, posting class rankings, or singling out high-scoring students for praise — and the *climate students perceive* will track the actual practices, not the stated philosophy. Climates are constructed by what the environment *does*, not by what its authority figures *say*.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The climate concept is the framework's primary point of practical leverage; intervention work has consistently shown that climates can be shifted through targeted changes in evaluative practice, with downstream effects on the goals learners adopt and the outcomes those goals produce.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-climate]], [[mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate]], [[autonomy-supportive-teaching]], [[target-framework]]

The empirical evidence on goal climates is substantial and consistent. Learners who perceive their classroom as emphasizing mastery are more likely to adopt mastery-approach goals, exhibit deeper processing, persist longer in the face of difficulty, attribute outcomes to controllable factors, and report higher intrinsic interest in the subject matter. Learners who perceive their classroom as emphasizing performance are more likely to adopt performance-approach or performance-avoidance goals (with the avoidance variant becoming more likely when learners doubt their ability to compete favorably), exhibit shallower processing, withdraw effort from challenging tasks, and engage in self-handicapping behaviors that protect ability inferences at the cost of actual learning. The effect of climate on goal adoption is not deterministic — individual differences in dispositional goal orientation, mindset, and prior achievement all moderate the relationship — but it is reliable and robust, and it is the basis on which the framework's intervention literature is built.

> [!key-claim] **Climates Channel Goals; Goals Channel Outcomes**
> The strongest pragmatic claim of Achievement Goal Theory is that *the perceived evaluative architecture of an achievement environment systematically channels learners toward particular goal orientations, which in turn channel them toward particular cognitive, affective, and behavioral patterns*. This two-stage causal chain — climate → goal → outcome — is what makes the framework actionable: educational practitioners cannot directly install achievement goals in learners' heads, but they can manipulate climates, and climate manipulations reliably produce goal shifts which reliably produce outcome shifts.

The most influential operational specification of how to construct a mastery climate is the **TARGET framework**, developed by Carole Ames in the early 1990s as a synthesis of climate research and a practical tool for educational design.

> [!definition] **The TARGET Framework**
> The [[target-framework]] is an acronym for six dimensions of classroom practice that, taken together, constitute the perceived goal climate: **T**ask (the design of activities — challenging, meaningful, varied), **A**uthority (the distribution of decision-making — student involvement vs teacher control), **R**ecognition (the basis on which students are publicly acknowledged — effort and improvement vs normative ranking), **G**rouping (how students are organized — heterogeneous and cooperative vs homogeneous and competitive), **E**valuation (how performance is judged — criterion-referenced vs norm-referenced; private vs public), **T**ime (how the pace of learning is structured — flexible to allow mastery vs uniform to allow comparison).
>
> **Operational Use:** A teacher seeking to construct a mastery climate would design tasks for meaningfulness rather than gradeability, share authority with students, recognize effort and improvement publicly, group students heterogeneously and cooperatively, evaluate against criterion standards privately, and allow flexible pacing toward mastery. A teacher constructing a performance climate (often unintentionally) would do the opposite on each dimension.
>
> **Boundary:** TARGET is not a complete theory of classroom climate; it is a practical synthesis of climate research as it stood in the early 1990s and remains the dominant framework for translating goal-climate findings into design recommendations. Subsequent work has added dimensions and refined the recommendations, but the six original categories continue to organize most intervention research.
>
> **See also:** [[target-framework]], [[autonomy-supportive-teaching]], [[autonomy-support]], [[feedback-design-for-autonomy-and-mastery]]

What gives the TARGET framework its enduring usefulness is that each of its six dimensions corresponds to a specific channel through which the evaluative architecture is communicated to learners — and each channel can be modified independently of the others, allowing practitioners to make targeted interventions without overhauling the entire instructional system. A teacher who suspects that posted grades are pushing students toward performance-avoidance goals can shift the *evaluation* dimension by moving to private criterion-referenced feedback without changing how tasks are designed, how groups are formed, or how time is paced. The framework's modularity, in other words, makes it operationally useful in a way that more global climate descriptions are not.

> [!claude-insight] **The Climate Concept's Hidden Implication**
> One implication of the climate concept that is not always made explicit in the practical literature is that *climate is a perception, not an objective property of the environment*. Two students in the same classroom can perceive different climates — one perceiving mastery emphasis where another perceives performance emphasis — depending on which cues they attend to, what their prior expectations are, and what their own dispositional goal orientations lead them to look for. This means that climate interventions cannot be evaluated solely by what the teacher does; they must be evaluated by what students *perceive* the teacher to be doing, which sometimes diverges sharply from the teacher's intentions. The implication for practice is that climate construction is fundamentally a communicative act: the teacher must not only enact mastery-supporting practices but also ensure that students *recognize* those practices for what they are, which often requires explicit discussion of the evaluative architecture rather than reliance on students to infer it from behavior alone.

The climate concept also points toward a broader point about the framework's relationship to other motivational theories. The dimensions that constitute a mastery climate are not unique to Achievement Goal Theory; they overlap substantially with the conditions that [[self-determination-theory]] identifies as supporting [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], [[competence-need|competence]], and [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] — the three [[basic-psychological-needs]] whose satisfaction SDT identifies as the foundation of intrinsic motivation. This overlap is not coincidental; it reflects the fact that multiple theoretical traditions have converged on a similar account of *what kinds of educational environments support adaptive motivation*, even when they disagree about the underlying psychological mechanisms. The climate concept is, in this sense, a point of cross-theoretical synthesis where Achievement Goal Theory and Self-Determination Theory speak a common language — a point we will return to in Section 8.

> [!section-summary] **Section 6 — Key Takeaways**
> Goal climates are the perceived evaluative architectures of achievement environments — the cues, practices, and norms through which environments communicate what counts as success. A mastery climate emphasizes effort, improvement, and deep understanding; a performance climate emphasizes normative comparison, ranking, and ability differentiation. Climates reliably channel learners toward particular goal orientations, which in turn channel them toward particular outcomes — making climate the framework's primary point of practical leverage. The TARGET framework operationalizes climate construction across six dimensions (Task, Authority, Recognition, Grouping, Evaluation, Time), each modifiable independently. Climates are *perceptions* rather than objective properties, which means climate construction is fundamentally a communicative act and not merely a behavioral one.

## 7. Consequences: The Differential Outcomes of Achievement Goals

The empirical payoff of Achievement Goal Theory rests on its capacity to predict differentiated outcomes — to specify not just that learners with different goals will look different but to characterize *how* they will differ across cognition, affect, behavior, and longer-term developmental trajectories. The literature on these consequences is now extensive enough to support meta-analytic synthesis, and the pattern that emerges is both more nuanced than the early dichotomous-model predictions and more robust than skeptics have sometimes claimed. The framework's predictions are confirmed for most of the major outcome variables motivation researchers care about; where the predictions fail or weaken, the failures are themselves theoretically informative because they typically point to moderators (climate features, individual differences, task characteristics) that the framework has progressively incorporated into its account.

Consider first the cognitive consequences. Mastery-approach goals are reliably associated with deeper levels of processing — what cognitive psychologists call [[deep-processing]] or [[elaboration|elaborative]] strategies — including [[self-explanation]], [[elaborative-interrogation]], integration of new material with prior knowledge, and the construction of [[mental-model|mental models]] that organize the material conceptually rather than as a list of facts to be remembered. Performance-approach goals show a more mixed pattern: in some studies they are associated with adequate or even strong cognitive engagement, but the engagement tends to be more strategically targeted at what will be evaluated and less oriented toward depth for its own sake. Performance-avoidance goals are reliably associated with shallow processing — surface-level rehearsal, reliance on [[maintenance-rehearsal]] rather than elaborative encoding, avoidance of [[desirable-difficulties]] that would expose weaknesses, and what one might call evidence-suppression strategies that protect ability inferences at the cost of actual understanding. The cognitive consequences, in short, are not just downstream effects of the goals; they are *constitutive* of what the goals are doing — the deep-processing patterns of mastery-approach learners and the surface-processing patterns of performance-avoidance learners are, at the level of cognitive operations, what the goal orientations *consist of*.

> [!key-claim] **Goals Are Architectures, Not Just Targets — A Restatement**
> The cognitive-consequence literature provides perhaps the strongest support for the framework's central architectural claim. If achievement goals were merely *targets* that learners pursued, we would expect them to predict effort and outcome but not the *strategies* used to pursue those targets. The fact that goals predict the choice of cognitive strategy (deep vs surface processing, elaboration vs rehearsal, error-as-information vs error-as-threat handling) at a level that closely matches the theoretical predictions is what justifies treating the goals as architectures that organize the cognitive system, not as preferences that operate above it.

The affective consequences follow a similar but distinct pattern. The recently developed framework of [[control-value-theory]], pioneered by Reinhard Pekrun, has integrated achievement-goal predictions about emotion with a broader theory of [[academic-emotions|achievement emotions]] and has produced a set of refined predictions that the contemporary literature largely supports. Mastery-approach goals are associated with positive activating emotions during learning (interest, enjoyment, hope), with constructive responses to setback (frustration that motivates renewed effort rather than shame that motivates withdrawal), and with relatively stable emotional regulation across the learning trajectory. Performance-approach goals are associated with positive emotions when performance is going well but with sharper negative emotions when performance falters, producing a more volatile emotional trajectory. Performance-avoidance goals are associated with anxiety as the dominant emotion of engagement, with shame as the dominant emotion of failure, and with relief (rather than satisfaction) as the dominant emotion of success — because under an avoidance frame, success consists of not having failed, which generates relief but not the positive activating affect that would sustain continued engagement.

> [!example] **The Affective Consequence in a Single Episode**
> A student receives a difficult question they cannot immediately answer. Under a mastery-approach frame, the predominant affect is curiosity (what is this question revealing about what I don't yet understand?) shading into productive frustration if the question proves resistant. Under a performance-approach frame, the affect is initially confidence shading into anxiety as difficulty mounts, with the cognitive system increasingly devoting resources to image-management rather than problem-solving. Under a performance-avoidance frame, the affect is anxiety from the outset, with the dominant cognitive activity being the search for face-saving exits — a guess that sounds plausible, a deflection to a related topic, an admission of uncertainty calibrated to minimize ability inferences. Three students, the same external situation, three architecturally different emotional and cognitive responses.

The behavioral consequences are perhaps the most consistently documented in the literature. Mastery-approach goals predict longer persistence on difficult tasks, greater willingness to seek help when help would be informative, more frequent self-initiated study, and greater resilience following failure. Performance-approach goals predict adequate persistence in evaluative contexts but inconsistent persistence in non-evaluated learning, strategic help-seeking calibrated to image-management rather than understanding, and a vulnerability to disengagement when comparative standing becomes unfavorable. Performance-avoidance goals predict short persistence, [[self-handicapping]] behaviors (deliberate withdrawal of effort that creates an attribution-protecting alibi for poor performance), avoidance of help-seeking (which would signal incompetence), and a high probability of disengagement following any signal of impending failure.

The longer-term developmental trajectories are perhaps the most consequential outcomes from an educational standpoint. Across multi-year studies, learners with consistently mastery-approach goal profiles show stronger expertise development, more coherent conceptual understanding, better long-term retention, and more robust transfer of skills to novel contexts. Learners with consistently performance-avoidance profiles show the opposite trajectory: weaker long-term retention, fragmented understanding that does not transfer well, and a documented tendency to abandon domains where competition is high or evaluation is salient. The mastery-advantage pattern, in other words, is not just a difference in the moment of engagement; it accumulates into divergent expertise trajectories that compound across years.

> [!claude-insight] **The Developmental Compound Interest of Goal Orientations**
> One of the most important but underappreciated implications of the consequences literature is what one might call the *compound interest* of goal orientations. The differences in cognitive engagement between mastery-approach and performance-avoidance learners in any single learning episode may be modest; the differences accumulate, however, across thousands of learning episodes over years of education, and the result is a divergent expertise trajectory that becomes nearly impossible to close once it has opened. A learner who consistently engages each new concept with deep elaboration is building both content knowledge and meta-cognitive skill at every step; a learner who consistently engages with surface rehearsal is building neither, and is moreover constructing increasingly fragmented prior knowledge that makes future deep engagement progressively harder. This is why early intervention to shift goal orientations and climates is so consequential: the intervention is not just changing how the learner engages today; it is changing the cognitive architecture that will be doing the engaging tomorrow.

The consequences literature also documents what might be called *transfer effects* — patterns in which the goal orientation a learner adopts in one domain influences how they engage with competence-relevant tasks in adjacent domains. A learner whose dominant orientation in academic settings is performance-avoidance frequently exhibits the same orientation in athletic, social, and even creative domains, suggesting that achievement goals are not entirely domain-specific but are partially organized as dispositional traits that travel with the learner across contexts. This dispositional dimension complicates the simple climate → goal → outcome causal chain by introducing a stable individual-difference factor that interacts with situational climates rather than being entirely determined by them — a point we will return to in the next section's discussion of the framework's tensions with strict situationist accounts.

> [!warning] **A Caveat About the Strength of the Mastery Advantage**
> The mastery-advantage pattern is robust but not unconditional. There are documented contexts — particularly competitive evaluative environments where ability differentiation is the actual stake (academic admissions, professional licensing, athletic selection) — in which performance-approach goals produce achievement effects equal to or greater than mastery-approach goals. The framework does not claim that mastery goals are universally superior; it claims that they are superior across most learning contexts that emphasize skill development, conceptual understanding, and long-term retention. Where the actual stake is normative ranking against competitors, performance-approach goals are well-matched to the situation in a way that mastery goals are not.

> [!section-summary] **Section 7 — Key Takeaways**
> The empirical literature on achievement goal consequences confirms differentiated patterns across cognitive, affective, behavioral, and developmental outcomes. Mastery-approach goals predict deep processing, positive activating emotions, sustained persistence, and accumulating expertise advantages over years. Performance-approach goals predict adequate engagement in evaluative contexts but more volatile emotional trajectories and inconsistent persistence in non-evaluated learning. Performance-avoidance goals predict shallow processing, anxiety, self-handicapping, and divergent developmental trajectories that compound across years into substantial expertise gaps. The cognitive consequences are particularly important because they are constitutive rather than merely downstream — the strategies a learner uses are part of what the goal *is*, not just an effect of what the goal predicts. The mastery advantage is robust but conditional: in genuinely competitive evaluative contexts, performance-approach goals can be well-matched to the situation in a way that mastery goals are not.

> [!reflection] **Reflective Questions for Section 7**

What does this imply about the timing of motivational interventions, and why might early interventions be qualitatively different in their leverage from later ones?
> 2. If achievement goals are partially dispositional (traveling with the learner across domains) and partially situational (responsive to climate), what does this dual character imply about the appropriate target of intervention — should we be modifying climates, modifying dispositions, or both?
> 3. The mastery advantage is conditional rather than universal. What feature of an evaluative context determines whether mastery-approach or performance-approach goals will produce stronger achievement, and what does this conditionality reveal about the relationship between goal and environment?

## 8. Tensions, Critiques, and Adjacent Frameworks

A foundational report on a mature theoretical framework is incomplete if it presents only the framework's positive content without examining the points where the framework is contested, the adjacent theories with which it productively disagrees, and the unresolved debates that constitute its current frontier. Achievement Goal Theory is a mature framework with three decades of empirical work behind it, and its maturity is partly evident in the sophistication of the disagreements that surround it — disagreements that have largely moved past whether the framework is *correct* and into the more interesting territory of what it *gets right*, what it *misses*, and how it relates to other ways of asking similar questions.

The first major tension is internal: the *structural debate* about how many goals exist and how they should be organized. The dichotomous, trichotomous, 2×2, and 3×2 models are not merely sequential refinements; they are partially competing structural claims about the goal space, and a substantial fraction of the contemporary literature consists of careful empirical comparisons attempting to determine which structure best fits the data. The most sustained critique within this debate has come from researchers who argue that the *mastery-avoidance* cell, while theoretically required by the symmetry of the 2×2 framework, lacks the empirical robustness of the other three cells and may be partially absorbed by adjacent constructs (perfectionism, fear of failure, regulatory focus). A second strand of structural critique has questioned whether the framework's reliance on self-report measurement systematically inflates the empirical separation between cells that may be more correlated in actual cognitive operation than they appear in survey responses. Both critiques are genuine and ongoing; neither has produced a consensus alternative to the 2×2 framework, but both have introduced productive cautions about treating the four-cell structure as the final word on the goal space.

> [!tension] **Trait vs State: The Disposition-Situation Debate**
> A persistent tension within the achievement goal literature concerns whether achievement goals are best conceptualized as *traits* (stable individual differences that learners carry across situations) or as *states* (dynamic configurations that learners construct in response to specific situational features). The trait position emphasizes the cross-situational consistency of goal orientations and their correlations with stable individual differences such as mindset, self-esteem, and personality. The state position emphasizes the responsiveness of goals to climate manipulations and the within-person variability of goals across contexts. The current consensus, supported by hierarchical-modeling studies, is that both positions capture genuine variance: goals are partially dispositional (with cross-situational consistency above chance) and partially situational (with within-person variability across contexts that climate features systematically explain). The debate has shifted from "trait or state?" to "what are the relative magnitudes of trait and state variance, and how do they interact?"

> [!debate] **The Performance-Approach Paradox**
> A second major debate concerns the *adaptive value of performance-approach goals*. The early dichotomous-model literature characterized performance goals as globally maladaptive; the introduction of the approach-avoidance distinction substantially rehabilitated performance-approach goals, with empirical work showing strong achievement effects in many contexts. But a subsequent wave of research, most prominently associated with Carol Midgley and her collaborators, has argued that performance-approach goals are still *long-term* maladaptive even when they are *short-term* effective — that the achievement gains they produce come at hidden costs to intrinsic interest, deep processing, and developmental trajectory that only become visible across multi-year studies. The opposing position, associated with Andrew Elliot and others, holds that performance-approach goals are *contextually* adaptive and that the long-term costs documented by critics are overestimated, attributable to confounded climate effects, or specific to subpopulations rather than general. The debate is unresolved and is methodologically significant because it concerns what time-frame and what outcome variables should be privileged when evaluating motivational consequences.

The framework's relationship to **adjacent theories** is itself a productive site of tension and integration. Three adjacencies are particularly worth mapping.

The relationship to **[[self-determination-theory]]** is, as noted in §6, one of substantial overlap in practical recommendations (both frameworks recommend autonomy-supportive, mastery-emphasizing environments) combined with theoretical disagreement about underlying mechanisms. Achievement Goal Theory locates motivational quality in the *interpretive frame* the learner adopts; Self-Determination Theory locates it in the *experiential quality* of the regulation (autonomous vs controlled) and the satisfaction of [[basic-psychological-needs]]. The two frameworks are not contradictory and are increasingly being integrated, with researchers proposing that achievement goals are partially mediated by need satisfaction and that need satisfaction is partially channeled through goal adoption — a bidirectional integration that respects both frameworks' insights while avoiding the older zero-sum framing.

The relationship to **[[implicit-theory-of-intelligence|implicit theories of intelligence]]** (mindset theory) is even closer historically and theoretically. Carol Dweck's work on mindset developed in continuous dialogue with achievement goal research, and the [[fixed-mindset]]/[[growth-mindset]] distinction has been theorized as a developmental antecedent of goal orientation: learners holding entity (fixed) theories of intelligence tend to adopt performance goals because intelligence is conceptualized as a fixed capacity to be demonstrated, while learners holding incremental (growth) theories tend to adopt mastery goals because intelligence is conceptualized as a malleable capacity to be developed. The two frameworks remain distinct — mindsets concern *malleability beliefs*, goals concern *evaluative purposes* — but they are mutually reinforcing and are increasingly studied as components of an integrated motivational system.

The relationship to **[[goal-setting-theory]]** is one of complementary scope rather than competition. Goal-setting theory studies the effects of *target characteristics* (specificity, difficulty) on performance, treating the meaning of "performance" as given by the task structure. Achievement Goal Theory studies the effects of *interpretive purposes* on the cognitive-affective architecture of engagement, treating the meaning of "performance" as something the learner actively constructs. The two frameworks operate at different levels of analysis and answer different questions; both are needed for a complete account of how goals shape achievement, and contemporary work increasingly draws on both.

> [!claude-insight] **Where the Framework's Frontier Actually Is**
> Reading the contemporary debates carefully, what emerges as the most productive frontier for Achievement Goal Theory is not the resolution of the structural debate (whether 2×2 or 3×2 or some configural model is correct) but rather the integration of the framework with adjacent theories of motivation, cognition, and emotion. The framework's strongest scientific contributions in the past decade have come from such integrations — with control-value theory on academic emotions, with self-determination theory on need satisfaction, with mindset theory on developmental antecedents, with self-regulated learning on cognitive engagement strategies. The framework is mature enough that further refinement of its internal taxonomy is producing diminishing returns; its growing usefulness lies in its capacity to function as a *connector* between theoretical traditions, providing a precise account of the *interpretive frame* dimension that other frameworks have tended to treat implicitly. This integrative function may turn out to be the framework's most lasting contribution to motivation science.

> [!original-synthesis] **The Bifurcation-Refinement-Multiplication Progression**
> A pattern visible across the framework's thirty-year history but not, to our knowledge, named explicitly in the literature is what we will call the *bifurcation-refinement-multiplication* progression. Each stage of the framework's development has followed the same logical structure: an initial *bifurcation* (mastery vs performance) that captures a real architectural distinction; a subsequent *refinement* (approach vs avoidance) that subdivides one of the original categories along a previously implicit dimension; a further *multiplication* (2×2, then 3×2, then goal complexes) that extends the refinement across both original categories and into multidimensional configurations. This progression is not unique to Achievement Goal Theory — it can be observed in the history of attribution theory, of cognitive load theory, of self-regulation theory, of dual-process theory — and its recurrence suggests that it may be a general pattern in how empirically productive psychological frameworks evolve under sustained scrutiny. The pattern's recognition allows the prediction that achievement goal research is currently in a *multiplication* phase that will likely give way to a *configuration* phase in which the discrete-cell taxonomy is replaced by a dynamic multidimensional space within which learners construct context-specific goal complexes — a phase whose contours are already visible in the goal-complex literature but whose mature theoretical form has not yet been articulated.

> [!section-summary] **Section 8 — Key Takeaways**
> Achievement Goal Theory's maturity is evident in the sophistication of its current debates rather than in their absence. The structural debate (how many goals, how organized) remains active, with the mastery-avoidance cell most contested. The disposition-situation debate has resolved into a consensus that goals are partially trait-like and partially state-like, with both forms of variance interacting. The performance-approach paradox concerns whether short-term effective performance-approach goals are also long-term adaptive, and this debate remains unresolved. The framework's relationship to adjacent theories — Self-Determination Theory, mindset theory, goal-setting theory, control-value theory — has shifted from competition to integration, with the framework increasingly functioning as a connector between traditions. The most productive frontier is not internal refinement but cross-theoretical integration, and the bifurcation-refinement-multiplication progression suggests that the next major structural shift will likely be toward dynamic configural models that absorb the 2×2 taxonomy into a higher-dimensional space.

## Far Transfer: Applying These Insights Beyond the Classroom

The educational research from which Achievement Goal Theory emerged is the framework's natural home, but the architectural insight at its core — that *what one takes success to mean reorganizes the cognitive system pursuing it* — generalizes to any domain in which competence is being developed and evaluated. Research on [[transfer-of-learning]] consistently finds that [[far-transfer]] is harder than near transfer because the surface features of new domains obscure the structural principles that would license the analogy; the work of theorists like Diane Halpern, David Perkins, Gavriel Salomon, and Susan Barnett and Stephen Ceci has converged on the recommendation that successful far transfer requires explicit identification of the structural principle, deliberate mapping to the new domain, and recognition that the surface differences may be substantial even when the deep structure is preserved. With this caution in mind, three transfer domains for achievement goal insights are worth examining briefly.

> [!far-transfer] **Expertise Development in Professional Practice**
> The structural principle of Achievement Goal Theory transfers cleanly to the literature on [[deliberate-practice]] and [[expertise-development]], where it predicts that practitioners adopting a mastery-approach orientation toward their developing skill will engage more deeply with feedback that exposes weaknesses, will seek out the [[desirable-difficulties]] that drive expertise growth, and will maintain a longer developmental trajectory than colleagues operating under performance-avoidance frames that lead them to avoid challenging cases or hide errors. The application is particularly visible in medical training, where the difference between residents who treat diagnostic errors as informative (mastery-approach) and residents who treat them as threats to professional identity (performance-avoidance) compounds across the residency into measurable differences in diagnostic skill years later. The boundary condition is important: the framework predicts these effects most strongly in genuinely educative contexts where errors carry feedback value; in high-stakes contexts where errors carry catastrophic consequences (surgical operations, emergency response), the appropriate orientation may legitimately shift toward performance-approach, with the structural principle being not "mastery is always best" but "mastery should dominate when learning is the actual stake, and performance should dominate when demonstration is the actual stake." See also: [[expertise-development]], [[deliberate-practice]], [[adaptive-expertise]].

> [!far-transfer] **Organizational Learning and the Climate of Failure**
> The climate concept transfers powerfully to organizational contexts where the *psychological climate around failure* shapes whether teams learn from errors or hide them. Amy Edmondson's work on psychological safety and organizational learning runs structurally parallel to the goal-climate literature: organizations that create what is essentially a mastery climate around failure (errors are diagnostic information, effort and improvement are recognized, learning trajectories are evaluated criterion-referentially) develop more capable teams over time than organizations that create performance climates around failure (errors are signs of incompetence, normative ranking is publicized, individuals are recognized for outcomes rather than learning). The deep principle being transferred is that *the perceived evaluative architecture of an environment systematically channels engagement strategies*, and this principle holds at the team and organizational level much as it holds in the classroom. The boundary condition is that organizational climates are constructed by many actors with conflicting incentives, which makes climate construction more politically contested than in the simpler classroom case. See also: [[psychological-safety]], [[double-loop-learning]], [[learning-analytics]].

> [!far-transfer] **The Design of Personal Knowledge Environments**
> The framework transfers, perhaps surprisingly, to the design of [[personal-knowledge-base|personal knowledge bases]] and [[personal-knowledge-management|personal knowledge management]] systems — the very kind of system in which this report is being read. A PKB is, among other things, an evaluative environment in which the user constantly makes implicit judgments about whether their notes are *good enough*, and the goal climate of that internal evaluation shapes what the user does with notes that fall short. A PKB used under a mastery climate (notes are diagnostic of where understanding is incomplete; revising notes is recognized as the central act of learning; the criterion is criterion-referenced against the user's own developing understanding) will support deep engagement, willingness to revise extensively, and long-term knowledge-graph growth. A PKB used under an implicit performance climate (notes are evaluated for how impressive they would look to an imagined reader; revising is felt as evidence of original failure; the criterion is normative against an imagined standard of "what good notes should be") will support shallow engagement, reluctance to revise, and the proliferation of polished but inert notes that do not feed into actual understanding. The deep principle being transferred is that *evaluative architecture matters even when there is no external evaluator*, because the internalized evaluator the user constructs will channel engagement just as a teacher's evaluative practices channel student engagement. See also: [[personal-knowledge-base]], [[zettelkasten]], [[note-making-vs-note-taking]], [[the-pkb-as-constitutive-metacognitive-architecture]].

> [!reflection] **A Metacognitive Closing Prompt for the Far Transfer Section**
> Consider the achievement domains in which you are currently active — your professional work, a creative practice, a learning project, perhaps the operation of your own knowledge system. For each domain, ask: *What is the dominant evaluative architecture I am operating under, and is that architecture matched to the actual stakes of the domain?* Where the architecture and the stakes are mismatched — where you are operating under a performance frame in a context that is genuinely about learning, or under a mastery frame in a context that is genuinely about demonstration — the framework predicts predictable inefficiencies. The diagnostic question is the first step toward designing the architecture you actually need.

## Synthesis and Integration

The argument of this report has been that Achievement Goal Theory's claim on attention rests not on the surface observation that learners pursue different aims but on the deeper architectural claim that the *purpose* a learner adopts in an achievement situation organizes a coherent cognitive-affective-behavioral system, with downstream consequences that compound across the developmental trajectory. The argument has unfolded across eight sections that together construct a layered understanding: the foundational definition of achievement goals as interpretive frames (§1), the developmental ground of ability conceptions on which the mastery-performance distinction rests (§2), the dichotomous taxonomy of the framework's first generation (§3), the approach-avoidance refinement that doubled the conceptual space and resolved early empirical anomalies (§4), the 2×2 framework that has dominated the contemporary literature (§5), the climate-based account of antecedents with the TARGET framework as practical operationalization (§6), the differentiated empirical literature on consequences (§7), and the active debates and adjacent-framework integrations that constitute the framework's current frontier (§8).

Three threads have run through the entire argument and bear restating in light of the full account. The *first* is that achievement goals are architectures rather than preferences; they reorganize what counts as evidence, what counts as success, what attention should track, and what behavior should accomplish. Treating them as mere preferences misses what makes the framework explanatorily powerful and reduces it to a special case of taxonomic motivation research. The *second* is that the mastery-performance distinction has proven robust across thirty years because it tracks a real architectural difference in how the cognitive system handles errors, effort, and other people's performance — a difference grounded in the developmental availability of two distinct conceptions of ability. The framework's longevity is not an artifact of methodological convergence but a reflection of the genuineness of the underlying distinction. The *third* is that the framework's most consequential point of practical leverage is upstream of goals themselves: it lies in the *climates* that channel learners toward particular orientations, which means that interventions targeting climates have the broadest reach and that the TARGET framework's six dimensions remain the most actionable synthesis of climate research available.

Two contributions of this particular report deserve explicit acknowledgment as *original synthesis* rather than as restatements of established literature. The first is the framing of Achievement Goal Theory's history as a *bifurcation-refinement-multiplication progression* that mirrors the developmental pattern of other empirically productive psychological frameworks. This framing is not present in the literature in this form and is offered as a way of making sense of the framework's evolution and predicting its likely next phase (configural rather than categorical models). The second is the framing of the *climate concept as perceived evaluative architecture* — an interpretive synthesis that emphasizes the constructive and communicative nature of climate over the more behavioral framings that dominate the practical literature. Both contributions are well-motivated by the established research but are offered here as integrative interpretations rather than as confirmed findings, and readers should treat them with appropriate epistemic caution.

The report also acknowledges its limitations explicitly. The literature on achievement goals is vast and the report has been selective in its coverage; whole bodies of work — particularly the cross-cultural literature on goal orientation, the work on achievement goals in athletic and organizational contexts, and the substantial neuroscience literature on the neural substrates of approach and avoidance motivation — have been touched on only briefly or omitted entirely in service of depth on the framework's central architecture. The contested empirical questions, particularly the performance-approach paradox and the operationalization of mastery-avoidance, have been described but not adjudicated, because the evidence does not support a confident verdict at this time. And the integration with adjacent frameworks has been sketched rather than worked out in detail, because each integration is itself a substantial topic that warrants its own treatment.

The forward-looking question with which the report opened — *Why has the simple distinction between "trying to learn" and "trying to look smart" generated thirty years of productive theoretical refinement, and what does the answer reveal about the architecture of human achievement motivation?* — can now be answered with appropriate nuance. The distinction has generated thirty years of productive refinement because it tracks a genuine architectural difference in how the human cognitive system can construct competence-relevant evidence; because the architecture, once recognized, supports increasingly fine-grained distinctions (approach-avoidance, task-self-other, configural complexes); because the framework's predictions about consequences are robust across methods, populations, and time-frames; and because the climate concept provides a point of practical leverage that has supported a continuing program of intervention research. What this reveals about the architecture of human achievement motivation is that the cognitive system is not a passive registrar of incentives that the environment supplies but an active constructor of the very evaluative frame within which incentives become legible — and that the construction of this frame is the hinge on which the entire downstream motivational architecture turns. Achievement Goal Theory is, in the end, a theory of how *meaning* shapes *operation* in the pursuit of competence — and that is why it has earned its place as one of the foundational frameworks in motivation science.

### A.1 Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Achievement Goal (Nicholls; Dweck; Ames)**
> A cognitive-affective representation of the *purpose for engaging in a competence-relevant activity*, specifying the standard against which the actor will evaluate performance. It is an interpretive frame, not a target outcome.
>
> **Boundary:** Distinct from intentions (which specify what to do), targets (which specify what would count as completion), and values (which specify the magnitude of importance attached to outcomes).
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The conceptual hinge of the framework; everything else builds on this definition.
>
> **See also:** [[achievement-goal]], [[goal-orientation]], [[motivation-science]]

> [!definition] **Conception of Ability (Nicholls)**
> The implicit theory a person holds about *what ability is* and *how it can be detected*. The *undifferentiated* conception treats ability and effort as equivalent (effort signals and constructs ability); the *differentiated* conception treats ability as a capacity distinct from effort (high performance with low effort signals high ability; high performance with high effort signals lower underlying capacity).
>
> **Boundary:** Not the same as mindset; mindset concerns *malleability*, conception of ability concerns the *structural relationship* between ability and effort.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The developmental and conceptual taproot of the entire mastery-performance bifurcation.
>
> **See also:** [[conception-of-ability]], [[the-developmental-differentiation-of-effort-and-ability]], [[ego-involvement]]

> [!definition] **Mastery Goal (Nicholls; Ames; Dweck)**
> An achievement goal in which competence is defined in *task-referenced or self-referenced* terms — success as understanding, mastery, or improvement against one's prior level. Synonyms: learning goal (Dweck), task-involved goal (Nicholls), task goal (Ames).
>
> **Boundary:** Not synonymous with intrinsic motivation; the constructs are correlated but conceptually distinct (intrinsic motivation concerns *why* one engages, mastery goal concerns *how one evaluates* the engagement).
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Foundational category; combines with approach/avoidance valence to yield mastery-approach and mastery-avoidance.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-goal-orientation]], [[mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation]]

> [!definition] **Performance Goal (Nicholls; Ames; Dweck)**
> An achievement goal in which competence is defined in *normative or other-referenced* terms — success as outperforming relevant others or meeting external standards that signal favorable ability. Synonyms: ego-involved goal (Nicholls), ability goal (Ames).
>
> **Boundary:** Not synonymous with ambition or competitiveness; concerns the *standard of evaluation*, not the magnitude of pursuit.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** Foundational category; combines with approach/avoidance valence to yield performance-approach and performance-avoidance.
>
> **See also:** [[performance-goal-orientation]], [[ego-involvement]]

> [!definition] **Approach-Avoidance Valence (Elliot)**
> The dimension along which a goal is anchored toward *attainment of a positive possibility* (approach) or *avoidance of a negative possibility* (avoidance). The distinction concerns the directional anchor of the cognitive-affective machinery, not whether the actor wants the outcome.
>
> **Boundary:** Not the same as success-failure orientation; an approach goal can fail and an avoidance goal can succeed.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** When crossed with the mastery-performance distinction, produces the 2×2 framework that organizes contemporary research.
>
> **See also:** [[approach-avoidance-motivation]], [[regulatory-focus-theory]]

> [!definition] **Performance-Approach Goal (Elliot)**
> An achievement goal oriented toward *demonstrating high ability relative to others*. Combines normative evaluative standard with approach valence; typically associated with high effort, strong achievement in evaluative contexts, and a more brittle engagement pattern than mastery-approach goals.
>
> **Boundary:** Not maladaptive in the way performance-avoidance goals are; the distinction is empirically and theoretically critical.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** One of the four cells in the 2×2 framework; contested in the long-term-adaptive-or-not debate.
>
> **See also:** [[performance-approach-goal]], [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]]

> [!definition] **Performance-Avoidance Goal (Elliot)**
> An achievement goal oriented toward *avoiding the demonstration of low ability relative to others*. Combines normative evaluative standard with avoidance valence; consistently associated with self-handicapping, shallow processing, anxiety, and the maladaptive pattern previously attributed to performance goals as a whole in the dichotomous model.
>
> **Boundary:** Not a low-magnitude performance-approach goal; qualitatively distinct orientation with its own pattern of antecedents, processes, and consequences.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The most consistently maladaptive cell of the 2×2 framework.
>
> **See also:** [[performance-avoidance-goal]], [[self-handicapping]], [[learned-helplessness]]

> [!definition] **Mastery-Avoidance Goal (Elliot & McGregor)**
> An achievement goal oriented toward *avoiding task-referenced or self-referenced incompetence* — toward not failing to learn, not losing skills already acquired, not falling short of one's own prior mastery standard.
>
> **Boundary:** The most theoretically novel and empirically contested cell; required by the symmetry of the 2×2 framework but harder to operationalize than the other three; most natural in populations such as older adults concerned about cognitive decline and experts concerned about skill atrophy.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The structural symmetry-claim of the 2×2 framework rests on its viability; current debates about the framework's structure often hinge on whether this cell is genuinely distinct or partially absorbed by adjacent constructs.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-avoidance-goal]], [[the-functional-priority-of-unexpected-and-negative-outcomes]]

> [!definition] **Goal Climate (Ames)**
> The perceived evaluative architecture of an achievement environment — the cues, practices, and norms through which the environment communicates what counts as success. A *mastery climate* emphasizes effort, improvement, and deep understanding; a *performance climate* emphasizes normative comparison, ranking, and ability differentiation.
>
> **Boundary:** Climate is a *perception*, not an objective property of the environment; two students in the same classroom can perceive different climates depending on what cues they attend to.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The framework's primary point of practical leverage; intervention work has consistently shown climate manipulation produces goal shifts which produce outcome shifts.
>
> **See also:** [[mastery-climate]], [[mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate]], [[target-framework]]

> [!definition] **TARGET Framework (Ames)**
> An acronym for six dimensions of classroom practice that constitute the perceived goal climate: **T**ask, **A**uthority, **R**ecognition, **G**rouping, **E**valuation, **T**ime. Each dimension is a channel through which the evaluative architecture is communicated; each is independently modifiable, allowing targeted interventions.
>
> **Boundary:** Not a complete theory of classroom climate; a practical synthesis of climate research as it stood in the early 1990s, but enduringly useful for translating goal-climate findings into design recommendations.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The most widely-used operationalization of climate construction in the intervention literature.
>
> **See also:** [[target-framework]], [[autonomy-supportive-teaching]], [[feedback-design-for-autonomy-and-mastery]]

> [!definition] **Goal Complex (Contemporary)**
> A *configuration* of multiple simultaneously-held achievement goals, treated as a unit of analysis in contemporary research. The goal-complex literature investigates whether configurations (e.g., simultaneously high mastery-approach and performance-approach) predict outcomes better than any single goal in isolation, and whether learners can be classified into latent profiles based on their full goal configurations.
>
> **Boundary:** Distinct from the *2×2 framework* itself, which specifies the structural taxonomy; goal complexes investigate combinations within that space.
>
> **Report-Specific Significance:** The likely next phase of the framework's structural evolution, away from discrete categorical models toward dynamic configural ones.
>
> **See also:** [[goal-orientation-framework]], [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]]

### A.2 Key Figures and Intellectual Lineage

> [!person] **John Nicholls (1940–1994), University of Illinois**
> Developed the developmental work on conceptions of ability that grounded the entire mastery-performance distinction. His distinction between *task-involved* and *ego-involved* goals (1984, 1989) is the conceptual ancestor of the contemporary mastery-performance distinction. Key works: *The Competitive Ethos and Democratic Education* (1989); developmental studies of effort-ability conceptions across the 1970s and 1980s.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Nicholls' developmental ground informed Dweck's social-cognitive work and Ames' classroom-climate work; he is the framework's most theoretically deep originator.

> [!person] **Carol Dweck (b. 1946), Stanford University**
> Developed the social-cognitive line of achievement-goal research, framing the distinction as *learning goals vs performance goals* and embedding it within the broader theory of *implicit theories of intelligence* (mindsets). Her work on attributional patterns following failure and on the "helpless" vs "mastery-oriented" response patterns established the empirical foundation for the differentiated consequences literature.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Dweck's mindset framework developed in continuous dialogue with Nicholls' developmental work and provides the implicit-theory antecedent of goal orientation. She has remained a continuing presence in the field through her mindset intervention research.

> [!person] **Carole Ames (1945–2017), University of Illinois / Michigan State**
> Developed the goal-climate line of research and the TARGET framework. Her 1992 *Journal of Educational Psychology* paper "Classrooms: Goals, Structures, and Student Motivation" remains the most-cited synthesis of the climate literature and the foundational practical text of the framework.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Ames synthesized Nicholls' and Dweck's work into the climate framework that became the basis of intervention research. Her TARGET framework remains the dominant practical tool.

> [!person] **Martin Maehr (1932–2017), University of Michigan / University of Illinois**
> One of the framework's founders; developed the *personal investment theory* of motivation that paralleled the achievement-goal work and contributed to the climate literature. Co-author of foundational synthesis papers in the 1980s and 1990s.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Maehr was a senior collaborator with Nicholls and Ames; his work provided much of the early empirical infrastructure for the framework.

> [!person] **Andrew Elliot (b. 1962), University of Rochester**
> The principal architect of the approach-avoidance refinement and the 2×2 framework. His 1997 trichotomous-model paper and 2001 paper with Holly McGregor introducing the 2×2 framework are the field's most-cited contemporary structural papers. He has also led the development of the 3×2 framework and remains the dominant voice in structural debates.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Elliot's work refined and extended the dichotomous model that Nicholls, Dweck, and Ames had established; his approach-avoidance integration drew on broader work in motivation science (regulatory focus theory, hierarchical models of motivation).

> [!person] **Paul Pintrich (1953–2003), University of Michigan**
> Integrated achievement-goal research with self-regulated learning theory; developed the influential 4×2 framework integrating personal and classroom goal orientations across approach and avoidance valences. His Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ) became one of the field's most-used measurement instruments.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Pintrich bridged the achievement-goal tradition with the broader self-regulated-learning literature; his early death cut short what would likely have been the framework's most thorough integration with cognitive learning theory.

> [!person] **Carol Midgley (1948–2002), University of Michigan**
> Co-developer of the Patterns of Adaptive Learning Survey (PALS) and the most prominent voice in the long-term-cost critique of performance-approach goals. Her work emphasized the developmental costs of performance climates and goals even when short-term achievement effects appeared positive.
>
> **Relationship to other figures:** Midgley's critique sharpened the performance-approach paradox that remains the framework's most active empirical debate.

### A.3 Conceptual Tensions and Open Questions

> [!tension] **The Mastery-Avoidance Operationalization Problem**
> **Position A** (the cell is genuinely distinct, supported by Elliot, McGregor, and most contemporary structural researchers): Mastery-avoidance is a conceptually distinct orientation that captures motivational configurations particularly visible in older adults, experts, and perfectionists; the operationalization difficulties are tractable methodological problems, not evidence against the construct.
>
> **Position B** (the cell is partially absorbed by adjacent constructs, supported by skeptical structural researchers and some perfectionism researchers): Mastery-avoidance fails to demonstrate consistent discriminant validity from related constructs (perfectionism, fear of failure, self-criticism), and its inclusion in the framework reflects theoretical symmetry rather than empirical necessity.
>
> **Current state of evidence:** Mixed; the cell has accumulated some supportive evidence but consistently shows weaker discriminant validity than the other three cells.
>
> **Why it matters:** If the cell is artifactual, the entire 2×2 framework should be rebuilt as a 2×2-minus-one structure; if it is genuine, future research needs to develop better measurement tools and identify the populations and contexts where it is most clearly active.
>
> **This report's stance:** The cell captures real phenomena in specific populations but its inclusion in the general 2×2 framework introduces measurement noise; we lean toward Position A but with appropriate epistemic caution.

> [!tension] **The Performance-Approach Paradox**
> **Position A** (Elliot and contemporary mainstream): Performance-approach goals are *contextually adaptive*; their achievement benefits in evaluative contexts are real and substantial, and the long-term costs documented by critics are overestimated, attributable to confounded climate effects, or specific to subpopulations.
>
> **Position B** (Midgley and the long-term-cost critics): Performance-approach goals are *short-term effective but long-term costly*; the achievement gains come at hidden costs to intrinsic interest, deep processing, and developmental trajectory that only become visible across multi-year studies.
>
> **Current state of evidence:** Mixed and methodologically dependent; cross-sectional and short-term studies tend to favor Position A, while longitudinal and intervention studies more often favor Position B.
>
> **Why it matters:** The debate has direct practical consequences for whether climate interventions should attempt to suppress or redirect performance-approach goals (Position B) or accept them as adaptive in evaluative contexts (Position A).
>
> **This report's stance:** The conditionality of the mastery advantage is real (performance-approach goals are well-matched to genuinely competitive evaluative contexts); the long-term-cost critique is genuine for learning-focused contexts where the actual stake is skill development rather than ranking. Both positions capture genuine variance and the resolution depends on context.

> [!open-question] **The Trait-State Relationship**
> The disposition-situation debate has resolved into a consensus that achievement goals are *partially* trait-like and *partially* state-like. But the relative magnitudes of these components, the mechanisms by which they interact, and the conditions under which one or the other dominates remain incompletely specified. Hierarchical-modeling studies are accumulating but have not yet produced a settled account, and the question is methodologically demanding because it requires both substantial within-person and between-person sampling.

> [!open-question] **The Configural Future of the Framework**
> If goal complexes (configurations of multiple simultaneously-held goals) predict outcomes better than single goals in isolation, what is the appropriate structural framework for representing them? Latent profile analysis has identified consistent profiles but has not produced a theoretically motivated taxonomy of complexes. The framework's likely next major structural development will be in this direction, but its mature form has not yet been articulated.

### A.7 Practical Application Protocols

> [!protocol] **TARGET-Based Mastery Climate Construction (for educators)**
> **Purpose:** A six-dimensional checklist for designing or auditing a learning environment along the dimensions Ames identified as constituting the perceived goal climate.
>
> 1. **Task design:** Are tasks varied, intrinsically interesting, and structured to permit improvement? Do they provide feedback about understanding rather than only about correctness?
> 2. **Authority distribution:** Do learners have meaningful agency in *what* to engage and *how* to engage it? Is autonomy supported in ways that connect to competence development?
> 3. **Recognition practices:** Is recognition criterion-referenced (against the learner's own prior level) rather than norm-referenced (against peer rankings)? Is effort and improvement explicitly named in recognition language?
> 4. **Grouping arrangements:** Are groupings flexible and varied rather than fixed by ability? Do groupings emphasize collaboration over competition?
> 5. **Evaluation criteria:** Are evaluation standards transparent and oriented toward criterion-referenced understanding? Is normative ranking minimized in routine evaluation?
> 6. **Time allocation:** Do time structures permit individual pacing where possible? Do they protect time for deep engagement rather than enforcing surface coverage of breadth?
>
> Each dimension is independently modifiable; partial implementation produces partial climate effects.

> [!checklist] **Self-Diagnostic for Personal Goal Orientation**
> Use this checklist to surface your dominant goal orientation in a specific achievement domain. Score each item 1 (rarely) to 5 (consistently) and note the cell with the highest aggregate score.
>
> **Mastery-Approach indicators:**
> - I seek out tasks that will stretch my current understanding.
> - I treat errors as information about where my understanding is incomplete.
> - I evaluate my progress against my own prior level.
> - I find difficulty engaging rather than threatening.
>
> **Mastery-Avoidance indicators:**
> - I worry about losing skills I have already developed.
> - I worry about not understanding as deeply as I think I should.
> - I avoid challenges that might reveal limits in my current mastery.
>
> **Performance-Approach indicators:**
> - I want to demonstrate that I am better than my peers.
> - I am energized by visible comparative success.
> - I select tasks where favorable comparison is likely.
>
> **Performance-Avoidance indicators:**
> - I worry about looking incompetent in front of others.
> - I avoid challenges where I might be exposed as not knowing.
> - I withhold effort to maintain plausible deniability about ability.

> [!decision-tree] **Choosing a Goal Orientation for a Specific Achievement Episode**
> ```
> What is the actual stake of this episode?
>   ├─ Skill development & long-term understanding
>   │    → Mastery-approach is the well-matched orientation.
>   │      Cultivate deep processing, error-as-information stance,
>   │      criterion-referenced self-evaluation.
>   │
>   ├─ Demonstration of competence with consequential ranking
>   │    (e.g., admissions, licensing, selection)
>   │    → Performance-approach is appropriate; the actual stake
>   │      is normative comparison, and the orientation should match.
>   │      Maintain mastery orientation in the preparation phase.
>   │
>   ├─ Mixed (some real learning, some real ranking)
>   │    → Construct a goal complex: mastery-approach as the
>   │      organizing frame for engagement; performance-approach
>   │      activated specifically for the evaluative moments.
>   │
>   └─ Genuinely high-stakes performance with no learning value
>        (rare)
>        → Performance-approach with explicit suppression of
>          performance-avoidance; the goal is execution, not
>          identity-protection.
> ```

### A.8 Spaced Repetition Seeds

> [!flashcard] **What is an achievement goal? (Definition)**
> A cognitive-affective representation of the *purpose* for engaging in a competence-relevant activity, specifying the standard against which performance will be evaluated. It is an interpretive frame, not a target outcome.
>
> **Source:** §1. **Difficulty:** Basic. **Tags:** definition, foundational

> [!flashcard] **Distinguish: mastery goal vs performance goal (Distinction)**
> Mastery goals define competence in *task-referenced or self-referenced* terms (success as understanding, mastery, improvement). Performance goals define competence in *normative or other-referenced* terms (success as outperforming relevant others or meeting external ability standards).
>
> **Source:** §3. **Difficulty:** Basic. **Tags:** distinction, structural

> [!flashcard] **What developmental phenomenon grounds the mastery-performance distinction? (Process)**
> Around ages 10–13, children differentiate the *concept of ability from the concept of effort*. The undifferentiated conception treats them as equivalent; the differentiated conception treats ability as a capacity distinct from (and inversely signaled by) effort. This developmental shift makes performance-goal pursuit conceptually possible.
>
> **Source:** §2. **Difficulty:** Intermediate. **Tags:** developmental, mechanism

> [!flashcard] **Why was approach-avoidance refinement necessary? (Distinction)**
> The dichotomous mastery-vs-performance model produced inconsistent empirical findings for performance goals — sometimes adaptive, sometimes maladaptive. Elliot's approach-avoidance distinction resolved the inconsistency: performance-approach (demonstrate ability) is meaningfully different from performance-avoidance (avoid demonstrating low ability), and the dichotomous model had been conflating them.
>
> **Source:** §4. **Difficulty:** Intermediate. **Tags:** distinction, history

> [!flashcard] **Name and briefly characterize the four cells of the 2×2 framework. (Application)**
> **Mastery-approach:** pursue task/self-referenced competence.
> **Mastery-avoidance:** avoid task/self-referenced incompetence.
> **Performance-approach:** demonstrate normative competence.
> **Performance-avoidance:** avoid demonstrating normative incompetence.
>
> **Source:** §5. **Difficulty:** Intermediate. **Tags:** structural, application

> [!flashcard] **What does TARGET stand for, and what is each dimension? (Application)**
> **T**ask (task design and variety), **A**uthority (autonomy distribution), **R**ecognition (criterion vs norm-referenced), **G**rouping (flexible vs ability-fixed), **E**valuation (transparent criterion-based), **T**ime (individual pacing). Six dimensions of classroom practice that constitute the perceived goal climate.
>
> **Source:** §6. **Difficulty:** Intermediate. **Tags:** application, climate

> [!flashcard] **What is the "compound interest" of goal orientations? (Connection)**
> The differences in cognitive engagement between mastery-approach and performance-avoidance learners in any single learning episode are modest, but they accumulate across thousands of episodes over years into divergent expertise trajectories. Early intervention is qualitatively higher-leverage because it shifts the architecture that will be doing the learning in all future episodes.
>
> **Source:** §7. **Difficulty:** Advanced. **Tags:** connection, developmental

> [!flashcard] **State the performance-approach paradox. (Distinction)**
> Performance-approach goals reliably produce strong achievement effects in evaluative contexts (suggesting they are adaptive) but are also associated with hidden long-term costs to intrinsic interest, deep processing, and developmental trajectory (suggesting they are maladaptive). The empirical literature is split on whether the short-term gains or long-term costs deserve primacy.
>
> **Source:** §8. **Difficulty:** Advanced. **Tags:** debate, paradox

> [!flashcard] **What is the bifurcation-refinement-multiplication progression? (Connection)**
> A pattern in the framework's history: an initial *bifurcation* (mastery vs performance), followed by a *refinement* (approach vs avoidance) that subdivides one category along a new dimension, followed by *multiplication* (2×2, then 3×2, then goal complexes) extending the refinement across both categories. The pattern recurs in other psychological frameworks (attribution theory, dual-process theory), suggesting it may be a general developmental pattern in empirically productive frameworks.
>
> **Source:** §8 (original synthesis). **Difficulty:** Advanced. **Tags:** meta, history, original

> [!flashcard] **Why is goal climate a *perception* rather than an objective property? (Process)**
> Climate is constructed by the learner from the cues, practices, and norms communicated by the environment. Two learners in the same classroom can perceive different climates depending on which cues they attend to, because climate is the *evaluative architecture as interpreted*. This is why interventions must target the cues that get interpreted, not abstract environmental features.
>
> **Source:** §6. **Difficulty:** Advanced. **Tags:** mechanism, climate
