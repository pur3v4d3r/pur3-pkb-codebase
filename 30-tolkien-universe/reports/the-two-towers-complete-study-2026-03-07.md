# The Two Towers — A Complete Study
### *Parallel Narratives, Divided Fates, and the Deepening of Darkness*

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Thematic Focus

> *"Frodo was alive but taken by the Enemy."*
> — [[Sam Gamgee]]'s first — and catastrophically wrong — conclusion, *The Two Towers*, Book IV, Ch. 10

That single sentence, appearing near the end of *The Two Towers*, encapsulates one of the most devastating structural ironies Tolkien ever constructed. Sam's error — reasonable, heartbroken, logical — leads him to the brink of abandoning the quest entirely, before a sound from a dark tower above changes everything. The moment is characteristic of the volume as a whole: a world in which the evidence available to any individual character points toward one conclusion, and the truth is elsewhere, hidden, working through channels no one can see from where they stand.

*The Two Towers* is the second volume of *The Lord of the Rings*, covering Books III and IV of the six-book structure Tolkien envisioned. It is, formally and thematically, the most experimental of the three volumes. Where [[The Fellowship of the Ring]] follows a single (if expanding) group of companions, and where [[The Return of the King]] drives toward convergence and resolution, *The Two Towers* bifurcates entirely. Book III follows [[Aragorn]], [[Legolas]], and [[Gimli]] through Rohan — their pursuit of the [[Uruk-hai]], the [[Ents]]' war on [[Isengard]], the awakening of [[Théoden King]], and the battle of [[Helm's Deep]]. Book IV follows [[Frodo Baggins]] and [[Sam Gamgee]] — their capture and mastering of [[Gollum]], their journey through the [[Dead Marshes]], their encounter with [[Faramir]] in [[Ithilien]], and their approach to [[Shelob]]'s lair. These two threads run in parallel for the entire volume and never intersect. Frodo and Sam do not appear in Book III at all. Aragorn and his companions do not appear in Book IV.

> [!key-claim] **The Central Argument of This Report**
> The bifurcated structure of *The Two Towers* is not a narrative convenience — it is itself a thematic argument. The volume insists that the fate of the West cannot be decided by heroism alone. The great battles at [[Helm's Deep]] and [[Isengard]], spectacular as they are, settle nothing. What matters — what ultimately determines everything — is the slow, apparently purposeless journey of two small hobbits and their treacherous guide through the wasteland toward a mountain of fire. Tolkien uses the split structure to make the reader feel, viscerally, the *disproportion* between cause and effect that lies at the heart of his theology of providence.

**Scope and Boundaries**: This report examines the complete *Two Towers* volume — Books III and IV — with particular analytical depth focused on five elements: the parallel narrative architecture, [[Gollum]]'s psychological complexity, [[Faramir]]'s deliberate contrast with [[Boromir]], the [[Ents]] as philosophical embodiment, and [[Saruman]]'s corruption. The military details of [[Helm's Deep]], the full history of [[Rohan]], and the complete analysis of [[Shelob]]'s mythological lineage are treated in condensed form here and developed in dedicated expansion reports. For the cosmological background to the [[One Ring]]'s nature, see [[The One Ring — Nature and Theology]].

**Chronological Position**: The events of *The Two Towers* take place in late February through mid-March of [[Third Age]] 3019, approximately 78 years after the events of *[[The Hobbit]]*, and during the final months before the War of the Ring reaches its crisis.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Prediction Point**
> Before reading further, consider: Tolkien wrote the entirety of *The Lord of the Rings* before dividing it into volumes for publication. He then had to decide where Book III ended and Book IV began — whether to alternate chapters between the two storylines, or to complete one thread entirely before the other. He chose the latter: tell all of Book III, then all of Book IV. Why might he have made this structural choice rather than alternating? What does it risk? What might it gain that alternation cannot? Commit to an answer before proceeding.

## Phase II: Historical & Textual Framework

### The Textual Context: How *The Two Towers* Came to Be

> [!definition] **The Two Towers** (Title)
> Tolkien's chosen title for the second volume, referring — in his own preferred interpretation — to [[Orthanc]] (Saruman's tower at Isengard) and [[Minas Morgul]] (the Morgul Vale fortress of the [[Nazgûl]]). Tolkien was characteristically ambivalent about the title, since there are several towers of significance in the volume and different readers have assumed different pairings. In a letter to his publisher (Letter 143), he acknowledged that the title was "not perfectly satisfactory" but preferred Orthanc and Minas Morgul as his pairing because they represent the two great enemy strongholds that frame the action of both books: Saruman's power dominating Book III, Sauron's shadow (through [[Shelob]] and [[Cirith Ungol]]) dominating Book IV.

The division of *The Lord of the Rings* into three volumes was a commercial decision by Allen & Unwin, not an artistic one by Tolkien, who had written the work as a single continuous narrative. The six "Books" are Tolkien's own structural divisions. He was not always happy with where the volume breaks fell: the ending of *The Two Towers* on Sam's desperate resolution — "I'm coming, Mr. Frodo!" — is genuinely, almost unbearably, cliffhanger in quality. This was intentional. Tolkien understood that the commercial division created a reader experience he could not fully control, and he shaped his book-endings accordingly.

> [!definition] **Book III** (*The Treason of Isengard*)
> The third of Tolkien's six books, covering the period from the Breaking of the Fellowship at Amon Hen through to the aftermath of the Battle of Isengard and Pippin's impulsive use of the palantír. Principal characters: Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, Merry, Pippin, Gandalf (returned as Gandalf the White), Théoden, Éomer, Treebeard.

> [!definition] **Book IV** (*The Ring Goes East*)
> The fourth of Tolkien's six books, covering the period from Frodo and Sam's navigation of the Emyn Muil through Shelob's lair and the capture of Frodo. Principal characters: Frodo, Sam, Gollum, Faramir, Shelob.

### Key Conceptual Distinctions

The analysis of *The Two Towers* requires three foundational distinctions that will structure everything that follows:

> [!definition] **Sméagol vs. Gollum**
> [[Gollum]]'s name for himself was originally *Sméagol* — a [[Stoor]] hobbit of the [[Gladden Fields]] who killed his friend [[Déagol]] to possess the [[One Ring]] around Third Age 2463. The name "Gollum" is external, derived from the horrible swallowing sound he makes in his throat. Within the narrative of *The Two Towers*, Tolkien dramatizes the conflict between *Sméagol* (the diminished, pathetic remnant of his original personhood, capable of loyalty and even affection, particularly toward Frodo) and *Gollum* (the personality created by five centuries of the Ring's possession, possessive, treacherous, murderous). These are not simply good and evil impulses in a person — they are nearly two distinct beings sharing one wretched body, and the tragedy of Gollum is precisely that Sméagol cannot survive.

> [!definition] **The Entmoot**
> The great gathering of Ents called by [[Treebeard]] (Sindarin: *Fangorn*) to decide whether to go to war against Saruman. An *Entmoot* is, by the nature of Ents, an extraordinarily slow affair — it lasts three days in the narrative. This is not Tolkien making a point about bureaucratic inefficiency. It is a philosophically serious depiction of a people for whom deliberation is identical with moral seriousness: the Ents do not act hastily because hasty action is, for them, ethically suspect. Their slowness is their virtue.

> [!definition] **Eucatastrophe**
> Tolkien's term — coined in his 1947 essay "[[On Fairy-Stories]]" — for the sudden, joyous turn that redeems an apparently hopeless situation. He considered this the defining characteristic of great fairy-story, and theologically, he associated it with the structure of Providence. *The Two Towers* contains multiple eucatastrophes: Gandalf's arrival at [[Helm's Deep]] at dawn with Éomer and the Rohirrim; the Ents' destruction of Isengard; and — across the volume break — Sam's discovery that Frodo is alive.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Conceptual Checkpoint**
> Before the analysis proceeds: can you articulate, in your own words, what distinguishes *Sméagol* from *Gollum* within the text? Not as a metaphor for good and evil impulses generally, but as a specific narrative and characterological fact in Tolkien's rendering? If you find yourself reaching for abstractions, return to the passages in Book IV where Gollum literally argues with himself — particularly the scene in *The Taming of Sméagol* — and observe what precisely is happening there.

### The Textual Authority Picture

The primary source for this study is *The Two Towers* itself (Tier 1 — Canonical Published). *Unfinished Tales* (Tier 3) provides supplementary material on Gollum's history, particularly "The Hunt for the Ring." *The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien* (Tier 5) offers Tolkien's own commentary on Gollum (Letters 181, 246), Faramir (Letters 66, 180), and the structure of the Ring narrative (Letter 131). Christopher Tolkien's editorial volumes in *The History of Middle-earth* reveal the evolution of Gollum's role (he was initially a minor figure) and Faramir's conception, but these are not primary sources for the analysis here.

## Phase III: Evidence from the Texts

### The Structure of Parallelism: What the Two Threads Actually Do

The most remarkable structural fact about *The Two Towers* is that the two narrative threads are not simply concurrent — they are *thematically counterpointed*. Tolkien arranged them so that the great, visible, spectacular action of Book III (the Battle of Helm's Deep, the destruction of Isengard, the confrontation with Saruman) runs simultaneously with the quiet, invisible, apparently insignificant action of Book IV. This counterpoint generates the volume's central emotional and philosophical argument.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Knowledge State — Before**
> Before reading the next section, record your current sense of the relationship between the two books. Which thread do you find more gripping? Which seems more *important* to the fate of Middle-earth? Hold this response — we will return to it.

Consider what happens in each thread during the same general period. In Book III: the three hunters pursue the Uruk-hai across Rohan; Merry and Pippin escape into Fangorn; the Ents spend three days deliberating; Gandalf returns and breaks Saruman's hold on Théoden; thousands of men and orcs die at Helm's Deep; the Ents destroy a fortress that took centuries to build. In Book IV: Frodo and Sam climb down a cliff, cross a swamp, are captured by Faramir's rangers, eat some food, cross some roads, and crawl through a tunnel.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Structural Argument Against Heroism**
> Tolkien, by running these threads simultaneously and giving each equal narrative weight, is making a structural argument that cannot be made through plot alone. The argument is this: all the battles in Book III — the slaughter at Helm's Deep, the destruction of Isengard, the humiliation of Saruman — are *necessary but not sufficient*. They clear away Sauron's western tools. They do not touch Sauron himself, because Sauron is not in the West. He is in Mordor, watching the approach of a small creature carrying a small golden ring. If Frodo fails — if he takes one wrong step in Book IV — then everything in Book III is meaningless wreckage. The parallel structure embeds this logic into the reading experience: the reader *knows* both threads are running simultaneously, and this knowledge creates a tension no battle sequence can match.

### Gollum in the Text: Sméagol, Precious, and the Logic of Possession

Gollum's first extended appearance in *The Two Towers* begins the entirety of Book IV. The chapter "[[The Taming of Sméagol]]" is one of the most psychologically intense in the volume. Frodo and Sam have descended the cliffs of the Emyn Muil when they become aware they are being followed. They trap Gollum and, in a moment of crisis, Frodo does something extraordinary:

> "But at last Frodo spoke with hesitation. 'I will not touch the creature. For now that I see him, I do pity him.'"
> — *The Two Towers*, Book IV, Ch. 1

This is the pivot on which the entire history of the Ring will ultimately turn. Frodo's pity is not sentimentality. It is, Tolkien insists structurally and through Gandalf's earlier words (in *The Fellowship of the Ring*: "Many that live deserve death. And some that die deserve life. Can you give it to them?"), an act rooted in wisdom about the limits of mortal judgment.

What follows is the "taming" — a word Tolkien uses with deliberate irony. Frodo binds Gollum's ankle with the [[Elven-rope]] from [[Lothlórien]], and Gollum reacts as if burnt:

> "'Ow! Ss!' hissed Gollum. 'Nassty rope! It hurts us, it does, it hurts us, and we haven't done nothing.'"
> — *The Two Towers*, Book IV, Ch. 1

> [!evidence] **Textual Note: The Rope and Elven-making**
> Gollum's agonized reaction to the Elven-rope is textually significant. Sam attributes it to Gollum's general nastiness; but the rope was made in [[Lothlórien]] under the influence of [[Galadriel]], whose light and power Sauron's creatures cannot endure. Gollum's body, which has been reshaped by five centuries of the Ring, is perhaps no longer entirely able to tolerate contact with things made by those whose hearts are oriented toward the good. This is a tiny instance of a much larger Tolkienian principle: evil, as [[Augustinian]] privation, cannot touch the truly good without pain.

The most celebrated — and most carefully crafted — passages in Gollum's chapters are the soliloquies in which he argues with himself. The clearest example is in "[[The Passage of the Marshes]]":

> "Sméagol promised... Yes, yes, my precious... we promises, yes I promise! I will serve the master of the Precious. Good master, kind master, he lets us go. Yes, we goes. We goes."
> — *The Two Towers*, Book IV, Ch. 2

And later, in the darkest moment of "[[The Stairs of Cirith Ungol]]," the two personalities split almost into separate speakers — Gollum attempting to turn Sméagol against the hobbits, Sméagol resisting in fragmentary guilt.

> [!evidence] **Textual Note: Gollum as Free Indirect Discourse**
> Tolkien's narration of Gollum is technically sophisticated. He uses third-person narration that slips into Gollum's own diction and logic — a form of free indirect discourse unusual in fantasy of the period. When the narrator describes what Gollum "thought" or "felt," the prose frequently adopts Gollum's own confused, possessive, third-person-self-referential grammar. This gives the reader access to Gollum's interiority without ever letting Gollum speak for himself without distortion — a formal enactment of the split between his two selves.

### Faramir and the Test of the Ring

Faramir, Captain of the Rangers of [[Ithilien]] and younger son of [[Denethor]] the Steward of [[Gondor]], appears in Book IV's middle chapters in circumstances that constitute the volume's most explicit test of moral character. He captures Frodo and Sam, learns that they travel with the [[One Ring]], and faces the same temptation that destroyed his elder brother.

Tolkien renders Faramir's response with striking clarity. Faramir declares:

> "'But I am not such a man... Not if I found it on the highway would I take it, I said. Even if I were such a man as to desire this thing, and even though I knew not clearly what this thing was when I spoke, still I should take those words as a vow, and be held by them.'"
> — *The Two Towers*, Book IV, Ch. 5

This passage is the most direct statement in the volume of what might be called the ethics of self-binding: Faramir understands that his earlier declaration was, in the moment of temptation, a vow that he cannot break without destroying what makes him Faramir. His identity is constituted by his word.

> [!key-insight] **Linguistic Observation: "I should take those words as a vow"**
> The word "vow" in this passage carries enormous semantic weight in Tolkien's legendarium. Oaths and vows are among the most powerful forces in the legendarium — the [[Oath of Fëanor]] drives the entire tragedy of the First Age; the vow of the [[Dead Men of Dunharrow]] shapes the strategy of the War of the Ring. Faramir's casual use of "vow" signals that he inhabits a moral world where words bind absolutely, where speech is not merely expressive but constitutive of obligation. This is a genuinely Númenórean ethical vocabulary, and it distinguishes Faramir sharply from the more pragmatic, more power-oriented thinking of his brother and father.

### The Ents and the March on Isengard

[[Treebeard]]'s introduction — and through him, the entire Ent civilization — occupies the early chapters of Book III. Merry and Pippin, having escaped the Uruk-hai into [[Fangorn Forest]], encounter Treebeard in a scene that is among Tolkien's most carefully calibrated tonal achievements: it manages to be simultaneously deeply comic (two hobbits being "shelved" and examined by a twelve-foot tree-creature) and genuinely awe-inspiring.

Treebeard's famous judgment on Saruman is rendered with the weight of geological patience:

> "'He is plotting to become a Power. He has a mind of metal and wheels; and he does not care for growing things, except as far as they serve him for the moment.'"
> — *The Two Towers*, Book III, Ch. 4

Three days of [[Entmoot]] follow. The Ents' conclusion — to march on Isengard — is presented as genuinely surprising to Merry and Pippin, who had assumed the deliberation would end in inaction. But it is not surprising to a reader who has understood what the Entmoot means: the Ents' slowness is not timidity. It is the opposite. When an Ent decides to act, it has decided absolutely and cannot be stopped.

The destruction of Isengard — which the reader sees only through the after-action report of Merry and Pippin, sitting on the rubble eating pipeweed, surrounded by devastation — is one of Tolkien's most economical narrative choices. We are told of Saruman's fortress only after it has been reduced to wreckage. This compression is itself meaningful: the Ents' action was irresistible, and Tolkien does not slow his narrative to make it dramatic, because the drama is already contained in the aftermath.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Prediction Point**
> Treebeard famously says that the Ents "have not troubled about the wars of Men and Wizards" for a long time. He adds: "It is not our war." Yet the Ents march. What changes his mind? Before the analysis in Phase IV examines this question, consider: is Treebeard persuaded by argument, by emotion, by Merry and Pippin's story, or by something else entirely? The answer has implications for how we understand the Ents' role in the legendarium's moral architecture.

## Phase IV: Deep Analysis & Interpretation

### Gollum: The Most Complex Character in the Legendarium

It is a defensible claim — and this Loremaster will defend it — that [[Gollum]] is Tolkien's most psychologically achieved creation. This is not an obvious claim. Tolkien is celebrated for his world-building, his languages, his mythology; his character psychology is sometimes dismissed as simplistic, his heroes as archetypes rather than individuals. But Gollum defies this criticism entirely.

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: Gollum and the Augustinian Logic of Possession**
> In Augustinian theology, evil is not a substance but a privation — a diminishment, a hollowing out of what ought to be there. When Tolkien describes Gollum's physical condition after five centuries of the Ring — "a small slimy creature... with pale lamp-like eyes," a body that has become almost translucent, a lifespan extended beyond all natural measure into an existence that is barely life — he is giving dramatic form to this theological principle. The Ring does not make Gollum powerful; it makes him *less*. It extends his years by consuming his substance. The creature we meet in *The Two Towers* is not what Sméagol was — it is what remains after the Ring has spent Sméagol's vitality on its own purposes for five hundred years. Gollum is, in the most literal theological sense, a being that has been *privated* — drained of goodness until only appetite and memory remain.

What makes Gollum psychologically extraordinary is that Tolkien refuses to let this privation be complete. Sméagol persists — fractured, degraded, capable of only the most elementary responses of affection and gratitude, but genuinely present. Frodo recognizes this presence, and his pity is not deluded. There is something in Gollum that can still be reached, and Frodo reaches it. The tragedy of the narrative is that this reaching is not sufficient to save Gollum — but it is sufficient to save the world.

Tolkien was explicit about the theological stakes in a letter to his son Michael (Letter 246): "I do not think that Tolkien's conception of Gollum is unChristian. He represents the ultimate in degradation: a being that has become entirely the slave of a possessive and consuming will, which has eaten up his real selfhood." But Tolkien also insisted, in the same letter, that Gollum retained free will throughout — that his choices at the [[Cracks of Doom]] were genuine choices, however distorted by the Ring's power. The question of whether Gollum could have been saved — whether Frodo's mercy was in vain — is one Tolkien did not resolve, and perhaps could not.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Three-Way Mirror: Gollum as Frodo's Future**
> Tolkien's most devastating structural use of Gollum is as a mirror for Frodo's possible future. Frodo carries the Ring for much less than five centuries — a matter of months — but the parallels between his degradation and Gollum's are deliberately drawn. Both are hobbits. Both found the Ring (Bilbo found it first, but Frodo inherits it). Both come to call it "my precious" under its influence. When Frodo, in a moment of terrible clarity at [[Cirith Ungol]], threatens Gollum with the Ring's power using the exact grammar Gollum uses for himself ("I will hurt you if you touch me!"), the reader understands that Tolkien is not showing us a hero unaffected by evil but a hero being slowly consumed by it — and choosing, repeatedly and barely, not to be consumed yet.

### Faramir as Counter-Boromir: The Architecture of Contrast

[[Faramir]]'s role in *The Two Towers* is impossible to read without understanding that Tolkien constructed him as a direct response to Boromir — indeed, Tolkien admitted in Letter 180 that Faramir was conceived partly as a corrective to avoid the implication that all Men of Gondor were susceptible to the Ring's lure.

> [!authorial-intent] **Tolkien's Own Words on Faramir**
> In Letter 180, responding to a reader who felt Faramir's resistance to the Ring was implausible, Tolkien wrote: "Faramir is not less strong than Boromir. He has the same quality of courage and pride, and yet his nature is different... He has a love of lore and learning that Boromir has not; he is a man of peace rather than a man of war, in a world that makes war unavoidable." Tolkien considered Faramir the character most like himself, and this self-identification is instructive: the man of learning who loves peace and is called to war is, for Tolkien, the more fully human condition.

The contrast is embedded in the text itself. Boromir, as we know from Book II of *The Fellowship of the Ring*, framed his desire for the Ring in military terms: Gondor needs a weapon; the Ring is a weapon; ergo, Gondor should have the Ring. This is instrumental logic — the Ring as tool. Faramir explicitly rejects this framing:

> "'I would not take this thing, if it lay by the highway. Not were Minas Tirith falling in ruin and I alone could save her, so, using the weapon of the Dark Lord for her good and my glory. No, I do not wish for such triumphs, Frodo son of Drogo.'"
> — *The Two Towers*, Book IV, Ch. 5

The difference is not courage — both brothers have courage in abundance. The difference is *wisdom about power*: Faramir understands, where Boromir did not, that the Ring cannot be used for Gondor's good because it will transform the user's definition of "good" until it aligns with the Ring's purposes. This is the same understanding that [[Gandalf]] and [[Galadriel]] and [[Elrond]] display in their refusals of the Ring in *The Fellowship of the Ring*, but Faramir arrives at it without any of their supernatural insight — through nothing more than his habit of thought and his love of wisdom for its own sake.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Faramir ↔ The Platonic Philosopher-King**
> Tolkien was a deeply educated man who had read Plato's *Republic* at Oxford, and Faramir has a structural resemblance to the Platonic philosopher-king: the person who is fit to rule precisely because they do not want power for its own sake. Plato argued that cities are ruled badly when governed by those who love power (they will use it for themselves) or by those who hate it (they will be ineffective) — the ideal ruler is the person who rules from duty and wisdom, considering power a necessary burden rather than a desirable good. Faramir's rejection of the Ring is precisely this: he does not want it because he does not want *power* — he wants to protect what he loves (Gondor, the learning of Númenor, the peace of Ithilien) and recognizes that the Ring offers the wrong kind of protection. The parallel is not allegorical; Tolkien did not intend Faramir as a figure of Plato. But both Tolkien and Plato were drawing on the same deep understanding of the relationship between wisdom and power that runs through the Western philosophical and literary tradition.

### The Ents: Slowness as Moral Philosophy

The philosophical significance of the [[Ents]] is not immediately obvious, because Treebeard and his people are often received as delightful comic figures — the "tree-people" who are endearing in their ponderous deliberateness and alarming in their capacity for destruction. This reading is not wrong, but it is incomplete.

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: Entmoot and the Ethics of Deliberation**
> The [[Entmoot]] that Merry and Pippin observe is presented as almost comically slow — three days to reach a decision that seems (to hobbit impatience) obvious. But consider what Tolkien is actually depicting: a people for whom hasty decision is not merely impractical but ethically *wrong*. The Ents have watched the forests of Middle-earth for thousands of years, have watched the short-lived races — Men, even Dwarves — make decisions in haste and repent them in ruin. Their slowness is not sloth; it is the formal enactment of the conviction that any decision worth making deserves complete consideration. In a legendarium that repeatedly shows the catastrophic consequences of hasty action (the Kinslaying at [[Alqualondë]] was partly a consequence of haste; [[Boromir]]'s fall was a product of acting on an impulse rather than deliberating), the Ents' pace is a form of wisdom.

Treebeard's account of what the Ents have lost — the [[Entwives]], who went east to tend their gardens and were never found again — is one of the most quietly heartbreaking passages in the volume. He sings fragments of a dialogue between an Ent and an Entwife, and the songs reveal not nostalgia but something deeper: a fundamental divergence in ecological philosophy between the Ents (who love the wild, untended growth of forests) and the Entwives (who loved gardens, ordered rows, cultivated things). The Entwives' disappearance is never explained, and Tolkien deliberately left it unexplained — he wrote in Letter 144 that he believed the Entwives had been destroyed in the burning of the [[Brown Lands]] during Sauron's wars in the [[Second Age]], but he never resolved this in the text itself.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Entwives and Tolkien's Environmental Mourning**
> The lost Entwives represent one of the legendarium's most specifically *ecological* griefs. The Ents — wild, forest-loving, ancient — and the Entwives — garden-loving, cultivating, teaching — were complementary halves of a relationship between wildness and culture. Their separation, and the Entwives' loss, leaves the Ents without future: Treebeard says quietly that there will be no more Ents because there are no more Entwives to make "Entings." The Ents are a civilization in its final generation, their biological continuation severed by the destruction of the natural world's cultivated aspect. In a legendarium concerned throughout with the relationship between industrial power and the living world — Saruman's deforestation of Fangorn, Sauron's devastation of the [[Brown Lands]], the [[Scouring of the Shire]] — the Ents' situation is a sustained ecological lament. The great trees march on Isengard not merely because of an insult or a political grievance but because their homes have been *cut down*. It is one of the very few passages in the legendarium where the non-human world is shown organizing in its own defense rather than simply suffering.

### Saruman: The Corruption of Knowledge by Power

[[Saruman]] — [[Curunír]] in Sindarin, "Man of Skill" — is glimpsed throughout *The Two Towers* primarily through his effects: the devastation of [[Isengard]], the demoralization of [[Rohan]], the corruption of [[Grima Wormtongue]]. His direct appearance — the confrontation at [[Orthanc]]'s window in "The Voice of Saruman" — is one of Tolkien's most precisely written passages, because it is the only scene in the legendarium where we see how Saruman's power actually works: through language.

> [!evidence] **Textual Note: The Voice of Saruman**
> In the chapter "The Voice of Saruman" (Book III, Ch. 10), Saruman speaks from the tower window and his voice has an almost physical effect: "even now, even as he spoke they had begun to listen to his pleading, for his voice had lost nothing of its power. Men seemed to doubt their own judgment, seemed unsure whether they were mad, or whether he was sane." Tolkien is describing something specific here: the corruption of rational agency through eloquence. Saruman does not argue; he *persuades*, which is different. His voice bypasses rational evaluation and strikes directly at the listener's sense of their own reliability. This is exactly what the [[One Ring]] does — it corrupts the will — but Saruman does it through pure rhetoric.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Saruman ↔ Tolkien's Critique of the Technologist**
> Tolkien was explicit in Letter 131 that the Ring represented "the Machine" — the will to dominate and reshape the world through power and technique rather than through subcreation and love. Saruman is the character who most completely embodies the Machine-mind among the Istari. He has studied the arts of Sauron — he has, as Gandalf tells the Council of Elrond, spent years studying ring-craft and the history of power — and he has come to believe that the *techniques* of domination can be separated from their evil purposes: that one can study how Sauron controls minds without becoming Sauron. He is wrong. The "mind of metal and wheels" that Treebeard identifies is not the consequence of Saruman studying power; it *is* the study of power carried to its logical end. Tolkien's critique here is not anti-intellectual — it is anti-instrumentalist. Knowledge in the service of power, divorced from wisdom and love, becomes indistinguishable from the thing it studies.

The confrontation at Orthanc ends with an important piece of symbolism: Pippin, unable to resist, picks up the [[palantír]] that fell from Orthanc's window — and later, alone at night, gazes into it. He sees the Eye of Sauron and is nearly broken. The palantír is Tolkien's most compressed image of the relationship between knowledge, power, and corruption. These seeing-stones were made in [[Valinor]] and given to the [[Dúnedain]] for the good governance of their realm — genuine tools of illumination. But Sauron possesses the master-stone of [[Minas Ithil]], and through that stone he can corrupt the vision seen in any other. The palantír that Pippin uses does not lie — it shows things that are true. But it shows only what Sauron wants shown, framing truths in ways that serve his purposes. This is the logic of propaganda: not falsehood, but selective truth.

If the steps are not clear, consider: what does Treebeard mean when he says Saruman "has a mind of metal and wheels, and he does not care for growing things, *except as far as they serve him for the moment*"?

## Phase V: Themes, Significance & Limitations

### The Great Theme: Smallness and Consequence

*The Two Towers* is, among all the volumes of *The Lord of the Rings*, the one most insistently organized around the contrast between apparent importance and actual consequence. Every major dramatic event in Book III — Helm's Deep, the Entmoot, the humiliation of Saruman — is spectacular and decisive within its own frame, but ultimately peripheral to the question that actually determines the fate of Middle-earth. That question is answered in Book IV, in the quietest possible way, by two hobbits and a wretched creature following a path toward darkness.

This is not to diminish Book III's events — they matter enormously in the political and military frame. But Tolkien never allows the reader to forget that the political and military frame is the secondary one. He does this structurally, by reserving Book IV until last; and he does it thematically, through repeated images of littleness, hiddenness, and smallness being the vehicle of what actually matters. [[Pippin]] gazing into a [[palantír]] and thereby revealing to Sauron that a hobbit has been involved is, paradoxically, a gift: it misdirects Sauron's attention toward [[Minas Tirith]] and away from [[Mount Doom]]. Small things, in the wrong hands (or the right ones), change everything.

### Pity, Mercy, and the Hidden Logic of Providence

The most theologically significant element of *The Two Towers* is Frodo's pity for Gollum, because it establishes — structurally, before the reader knows how the story ends — the causal chain that will determine the quest's outcome. Tolkien stated explicitly in Letter 246 that Gollum's role at the [[Cracks of Doom]] was providentially arranged: that Frodo's mercy had, without Frodo knowing it, preserved the instrument by which the Ring would be destroyed. The mercy was right in itself; the providence that made it effective was, for Tolkien, a gift from [[Eru Ilúvatar]] — an instance of how the Music of the Ainur bends even the worst actions (Gollum's theft of the Ring from Frodo, Gollum's fall into the fire) toward an end that serves the good.

> [!warning] **Common Misconception: The Ring Was Destroyed by Heroism**
> The most widespread misreading of the Ring narrative's conclusion — already set up in *The Two Towers* — is that Frodo destroys the Ring at Mount Doom through an act of will and heroism. He does not. He cannot. At the crucial moment, he *claims* the Ring. The Ring is destroyed by Gollum, accidentally, in the grip of his own possessiveness. The entire elaborate campaign of the War of the Ring did not destroy the Ring — the mercy Frodo showed toward Gollum in the [[Emyn Muil]], which preserved Gollum to be present at the Cracks of Doom, destroyed the Ring. Tolkien insisted on this point. The lesson is not that heroism is unnecessary — it is that heroism is not enough, and that Providence works through the least likely instruments.

### The Ecological Dimension: Ents, Trees, and the Machine

*The Two Towers* is Tolkien's most explicitly environmentalist volume. The Ents' march on Isengard is, at its most literal, a forest rising to destroy an industrial complex that has been consuming it. Saruman's Isengard is described as a pit of fire and noise, with "great furnaces," "tunnels," "smithies," and "workshops" — a language drawn unmistakably from the industrial landscape that Tolkien had watched destroy the fields and forests of his childhood Midlands. The Ents do not philosophize about this — they act, and the action is cathartic in ways that no military victory in the volume quite matches.

> [!important] **Limitation: The Rohirrim Deserve More**
> This report has given relatively compressed treatment to [[Rohan]], [[Théoden]], [[Éomer]], and the cultural world of the [[Rohirrim]] — a people modeled partly on the [[Anglo-Saxons]] and presenting Tolkien's most sustained engagement with the heroic-elegiac mode of Old English literature. The Rohirrim's aesthetic, the [[Meduseld]] (echoing [[Beowulf]]'s Heorot), Théoden's awakening under Gandalf's intervention, and Éomer's rough nobility all deserve dedicated treatment. See expansion topic [[Rohan and the Legacy of the Éothéod]] for fuller analysis.

### Limitations of the Narrative's Reach

There are elements of *The Two Towers* that even a long report cannot resolve. Gollum's ultimate redemption — or non-redemption — is theologically undecidable within Tolkien's framework: did he choose, at the [[Cracks of Doom]], to seize the Ring? Or was he seized by the Ring's will? Tolkien's late letters suggest both readings are defensible. The [[Entwives]] remain permanently unresolved — their fate is one of the legendarium's genuine open wounds. And the nature of [[Shelob]] — whether she is descended from [[Ungoliant]] of the First Age, whether she has any intelligence or purpose beyond appetite — is stated in the text but not elaborated.

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

### What Becomes Visible When All Threads Are Assembled

The two books of *The Two Towers*, when read together with full awareness of their simultaneity, produce a single argument about the relationship between power and insignificance that is Tolkien's most distinctive contribution to literary mythology. It is not simply that "small things matter" — that is a commonplace. It is something more precise: that the visible exercise of great power is *always and necessarily* secondary to the invisible operation of something that looks, from any particular vantage point, like chance, accident, or failure.

Gandalf's army does not destroy the Ring. The Ents do not destroy the Ring. Aragorn's brilliant military and political strategy does not destroy the Ring. Sam's courage and love do not destroy the Ring. Frodo's mercy does not destroy the Ring. Gollum's greed destroys the Ring — and Gollum's greed was able to be present at the crucial moment because of Frodo's mercy, and Frodo's mercy was able to operate because Gandalf told Bilbo to let Gollum live, and Gandalf's counsel rested on something beyond all of them: the conviction that even the most corrupted creature retains some thread of personhood through which grace might work.

> [!original-synthesis] **The Two Towers as Theodicy**
> Tolkien described his legendarium in Letter 131 as "fundamentally religious and Catholic." The Two Towers is where this claim becomes most structurally visible. The volume is organized as a *theodicy* — a justification of the ways of God to men — in narrative form. The problem of theodicy is: if there is a good Providence, why does evil seem to be winning? The Two Towers answers by showing that evil is not winning where it appears to be winning (in the great battles, the political crises, the military disasters) and is being undone where it appears to be wholly in control (in the apparent hopelessness of Frodo and Sam's journey). More precisely: what looks like the decisive theater — Rohan, Isengard, Helm's Deep — is the secondary one. The decisive theater is Gollum's conflicted soul and the question of whether Frodo will show him mercy. Providence works, in Tolkien's cosmology, not through overwhelming force but through the transformation of the least likely instruments — and the transformation begins with an act of pity that costs Frodo nothing except the patience and humility to see something worth pitying in a wretched creature.

> [!original-synthesis] **Saruman as the Shadow of Tolkien**
> The most unsettling reading of Saruman's corruption, and one the text supports without asserting, is that Saruman represents the danger of the scholar: the man who loves knowledge so completely that he cannot resist the lure of the ultimate object of knowledge — power itself. Tolkien was a scholar who built an entire mythology out of linguistic love; Saruman was a scholar who built an empire out of the study of ring-craft. The difference between them is not intelligence or learning but the *purpose* of the knowledge: Tolkien's subcreation is oriented toward beauty and truth for their own sake; Saruman's scholarship is oriented toward the acquisition of the power to reshape the world. The frightening implication is that this difference is not always visible from outside — and that the scholar's own sense of the purity of their motives is not a reliable guide. Saruman almost certainly believed, for a long time, that he studied power only in order to understand and resist it.

### The Central Question Revisited

This report began with the question of why Tolkien chose to tell the two books completely rather than alternating. The answer, by now, should be clear. Alternating chapters would have preserved the simultaneity at the cost of the disproportion. When the reader reads all of Book III before encountering Book IV, they have spent hundreds of pages in the company of armies, kings, great battles, and the decisive political struggle for the West — and *then* they must drop into the company of two hobbits and a snuffling guide in a swamp. The deflation is deliberate and profound. It forces the reader to feel, in their own reading experience, the same truth that Tolkien is asserting theologically: the thing that looks small and marginal and hopeless is the thing that matters.

> [!key-takeaway] **The Loremaster's Final Synthesis**
> *The Two Towers* is Tolkien's most formally experimental volume and his most theologically concentrated one. Its bifurcated structure is not a narrative accident but the argument's medium: by separating the spectacular from the essential and holding them in parallel tension, Tolkien creates a reading experience that enacts, rather than merely asserts, the providential logic of his world. The great battles are real and necessary. But the real question — the one on which everything depends — is being answered in a swamp, in a tunnel, in the conversation between a man who has forgotten his name and a hobbit who refuses to forget it for him.

>

He said the legendarium was not an allegory of Christianity, but was informed by Christian values. ## Phase VIII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Ents** (Old English: *ent*, "giant")
> The oldest living beings in Middle-earth who have not passed into the West; shepherds of the trees, created by [[Yavanna]] Kementári in response to [[Aulë]]'s making of the Dwarves, and given life and guardianship by [[Eru Ilúvatar]]'s sanction. The Ents appear in Sindarin as *Onodrim* ("Stone People" — perhaps a reference to their solidity and age) and in Quenya as *Enyd*. Treebeard's Sindarin name is *Fangorn* ("tree-beard"). Ents are immensely long-lived, immensely strong, immensely slow, and immensely patient — and when roused, are among the most physically destructive forces in Middle-earth.

> [!definition] **Gollum / Sméagol**
> Born Sméagol, one of the [[Stoors]], a branch of the [[Hobbits]] who lived in the [[Gladden Fields]]. Murdered his cousin [[Déagol]] to possess the [[One Ring]] in Third Age 2463. Called "Gollum" from the swallowing sound he makes. Possessed the Ring for approximately 478 years before losing it to [[Bilbo Baggins]] in Third Age 2941. Spent decades searching and was eventually captured by [[Sauron]], who learned of "the Shire" and "Baggins" from him under torture. Freed by Sauron; captured by Aragorn; held in [[Thranduil]]'s halls; escaped; eventually guided Frodo and Sam to Mordor. His fall into the [[Cracks of Doom]] — clutching the Ring — destroyed it.

> [!definition] **Faramir** (Sindarin: "Sufficient Jewel" or "Jewel of Arda"; interpretations vary)
> Second son of [[Denethor II]], Steward of [[Gondor]], and younger brother of [[Boromir]]. Captain of the [[Ithilien Rangers]]. Scholar, soldier, and philosopher; described by Tolkien as the character most like himself. Unlike Boromir, Faramir had no desire to possess the Ring and released Frodo and Sam without attempting to take it. Later Steward of Gondor under King Elessar ([[Aragorn]]), and Prince of Ithilien. Married [[Éowyn]] of Rohan.

> [!definition] **Palantír** (Quenya: "far-seer" or "that which looks far away," pl. *palantíri*)
> Seven seeing-stones made in [[Valinor]] by the Noldor and brought to Middle-earth by the Númenóreans. They allowed communication between those who held them and true sight of distant places or persons — but the person using them had to have sufficient strength of will to control what they saw. [[Sauron]] possessed the Ithil-stone and could use any other palantír against its user, showing them only what he chose. The stone found at Orthanc had been used by Saruman for communication with Sauron, enabling his corruption.

> [!definition] **Entmoot** (Ent + Old English *mōt*, "meeting, assembly")
> The great gathering of Ents in the deep forest of [[Fangorn]], convened by [[Treebeard]] to deliberate on whether to act against Saruman's devastation. Lasted three days by the hobbits' reckoning. The Entmoot's slowness reflects the Ents' constitutional principle that no decision of consequence should be made hastily.

> [!definition] **Shelob** (likely Old English: *scēo*, "she" + *lobbe*, "spider")
> The great spider of [[Cirith Ungol]], described as the last child of [[Ungoliant]] — the primordial spider-creature of the First Age who drank the light of the [[Two Trees of Valinor]] and assisted [[Morgoth]]'s escape. Shelob dwells in a system of tunnels above the pass of Cirith Ungol and feeds on all living creatures who attempt to cross. Neither Sauron's servant nor independent ally — she is an ancient and semi-autonomous evil who "served none but herself." She is driven off by the [[Phial of Galadriel]] and wounded by [[Sting]], and Sam's counterattack is described as the most complete reversal of power in the volume: the smallest of heroes wounding the oldest of evils.

> [!definition] **Treebeard / Fangorn** (Sindarin: *Fangorn*, "tree-beard")
> The oldest of the Ents and, arguably, the oldest living being in Middle-earth by the time of the War of the Ring. Called by himself the "oldest living thing that walks upon the earth under sky." He is the herdsman of the great forest of [[Fangorn]], which is named for him. His Entish name is unpronounceable and takes "a long time in the telling." He is described as being "not quite" like any other thing Merry and Pippin had encountered — eight feet tall, with a face both old and very young, like "something that grew in the ground."

> [!definition] **Wormtongue / Gríma** (Rohirric: *Gríma* — likely from Old Norse *grímr*, "masked one" or "hood"; also resonant with Old English *grima*, "mask, ghost")
> [[Théoden King]]'s counselor, secretly in the pay of [[Saruman]]. His role in the narrative is to dramatize how a corrupter operates through legitimate institutional channels: Gríma does not seize power — he whispers, discourages, isolates, and gradually transforms the King's perception of his own situation until Théoden is too demoralized to act. His name's resonance with "grime" (filth) and "grim" (fierce, masked) are unlikely to be coincidental.

> [!definition] **Dead Marshes**
> An area of fens and bogs to the northwest of Mordor, formed from the flooded remnants of the battlefield of [[Dagorlad]] — where the Last Alliance of Elves and Men defeated Sauron in [[Second Age]] 3434. The faces visible in the murky pools — the drowned dead of the battle, preserved in uncanny stasis — are spectral remnants. Neither fully present nor truly dead. Gollum warns that the dead must not be touched, and Tolkien leaves the precise nature of their existence, characteristically, undefined.

> [!definition] **Isengard / Orthanc**
> *Isengard* (Old English: "iron enclosure"): the fortress-ring built by the [[Dúnedain]] of [[Gondor]] around the natural rock-pillar of Orthanc, later given to the Istari Saruman as his seat. *Orthanc* (Old English: "cunning mind," or "forked height"; the Old English pun is deliberate — the tower is both physically forked and indicative of Saruman's cunning) — the indestructible black tower of fused volcanic rock at Isengard's center. The Ents flood and destroy the enclosure but cannot damage Orthanc itself.

> [!definition] **Ithilien**
> The land east of the [[Anduin]] and west of the [[Mountains of Shadow]] (Ephel Dúath), once a fair province of [[Gondor]], by the War of the Ring long contested and partially ruined by [[Mordor]]'s influence. Faramir's rangers operate there secretly. Its description in "[[Of Herbs and Stewed Rabbit]]" is one of Tolkien's most lovingly rendered landscapes — a reminder of what Gondor's civilization was at its height and what is being lost.
