# The Shire and the Shadow
## A Loremaster's Study of *The Fellowship of the Ring*, Book I (Chapters 1–12)

---

## Phase I: Orientation & Thematic Focus

> *"All we have to decide is what to do with the time that is given us."*
> — Gandalf to Frodo, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "The Shadow of the Past"

This sentence, delivered in the comfortable study of [[Bag End]] on a morning when sunlight is still streaming through the window, is one of the most quietly radical statements in all of [[The Lord of the Rings]]. It is not a call to heroism. It is not a warrior's battle cry. It is a philosopher's observation — delivered by a wizard in grey who smells of pipe-smoke — that the only meaningful question any creature can ask is not *why is this happening to me* but *what do I do now*. It reframes the entire problem of Frodo's inheritance from tragedy into responsibility, from victimhood into agency, and it does so with a gentleness entirely characteristic of the book's opening movement.

That opening movement — the first twelve chapters of *The Fellowship of the Ring*, what this report will call the **Book I Sequence** — is one of the most carefully constructed narrative progressions in twentieth-century fiction. Tolkien spent years on these chapters, revising and deepening them, and the compositional record preserved in [[The Return of the Shadow]] (Volume Six of [[The History of Middle-earth]]) shows just how much labor went into making the danger feel real, the world feel inhabited, and the protagonist feel genuinely unprepared for what is coming for him.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Prediction Point — Before You Read Further**
> Before this report traces the twelve-chapter journey in detail, pause to consider: what, precisely, makes the opening of *The Lord of the Rings* feel so different from *The Hobbit*, even though the same world and many of the same characters appear? Try to articulate the difference not just in terms of tone ("it's darker") but in terms of narrative architecture — what structural choices make the shadow feel present in a way it never quite does in *The Hobbit*? Keep your answer in mind as we proceed.

**This report examines** the following central question: *How does Tolkien construct the transition from pastoral innocence to civilizational peril across the first twelve chapters of The Fellowship of the Ring, and what does this transition reveal about his deepest thematic and theological concerns?*

The scope of this study encompasses all twelve chapters of Book I, from "A Long-Expected Party" through "Flight to the Ford." We will pay particular attention to the [[Shire]] as a carefully constructed secondary world with mythological significance; to Chapter 2's function as the engine of revelation; to the [[Nazgûl]] as a theological rather than merely narrative invention; to the enigma of [[Tom Bombadil]]; and to the meeting with [[Aragorn / Strider]] as the introduction of Tolkien's central theme of hidden nobility and legitimate authority. The [[Council of Elrond]] (Book II, Chapter 2) and the subsequent Fellowship's journey south fall outside this report's scope and will be treated in a companion study.

**Why this particular sequence rewards deep attention** is not merely because it is beloved or famous, though it is both. It rewards attention because Tolkien does something here that is extraordinarily difficult to do in fiction: he creates dread that builds without a single moment of graphic horror, he presents evil as a genuine metaphysical force without ever making it cartoonish, and he situates the most ordinary sort of creatures imaginable — comfort-loving, food-obsessed, unheroic [[Hobbits]] — at the centre of an apocalyptic contest for the fate of the world. The question of how he manages this is a question about craft, theology, philology, and myth all at once.

## Phase II: Historical & Textual Framework

### The World Before the Story Begins

> [!definition] **The Shire** (Westron: *i Súza*)
> The homeland of the [[Hobbits]], settled in the year T.A. 1601 when brothers Marcho and Blanco led a Hobbit migration across the [[Baranduin]] (the Brandywine River) with the permission of the King of [[Arnor]] — though by the time of our story, the North Kingdom had long since fallen, and the Hobbits had forgotten they ever had a King. The Shire encompasses the four Farthings (North, South, East, West) and the special district of [[Buckland]] east of the Brandywine, along with the Westmarch added later. Its administrative capital, in a very informal Hobbit sense, is [[Michel Delving]] in the Westfarthing. [[Hobbiton]], where [[Bag End]] stands on the hillside, is in the Westfarthing.

> [!definition] **The One Ring** (*Uin Corf* in Quenya, the **Ruling Ring** or **Master-ring**)
> Forged by [[Sauron]] in the fires of [[Orodruin]] (Mount Doom) in the Second Age, approximately S.A. 1600, as the instrument by which he intended to dominate all other Ring-bearers and, through them, all peoples of Middle-earth. Sauron poured much of his own native power and will into its making; the Ring is thus not merely a weapon but a fragment of Sauron's being. It was cut from his hand by [[Isildur]] at the end of the Second Age, passed through many hands across millennia, lay for centuries in the [[River Anduin]], was found by [[Sméagol]] (later [[Gollum]]), carried by Gollum for roughly 500 years beneath the [[Misty Mountains]], and was then found by [[Bilbo Baggins]] during the events of *The Hobbit* (T.A. 2941), eventually passing to [[Frodo Baggins]] in T.A. 3001.

> [!definition] **The Nazgûl** (Sindarin: "Ringwraiths"; also called the **Nine**, the **Black Riders**, the **Úlairi**)
> The nine great mortal kings to whom [[Sauron]] gave [[Rings of Power]] in the Second Age — three men of [[Númenor]], and six kings of lesser realms. The Rings extended their lives beyond nature but gradually dominated their wills, and they became enslaved to the [[One Ring]] and to Sauron's will. They became neither living nor dead — the Rings preserved their bodies but their spirits faded into shadow. By the Third Age they are among the most terrible servants in Middle-earth: invisible to normal sight except as black-robed presences, perceiving the world primarily in the [[Unseen Realm]], drawn irresistibly to the One Ring and commanded by Sauron to find and recover it.

The story we are about to trace unfolds in [[Third Age]] year 3001 at the party (Chapter 1) and then leaps seventeen years to T.A. 3018 for the main action (Chapters 2–12). This seventeen-year gap is not laziness but architecture: Tolkien needs Frodo to have had time to settle into the comfortable routines of life at Bag End with the Ring before the hammer falls. It also allows Gandalf's long absence to be narratively motivated — he has spent those years pursuing leads, eventually finding [[Gollum]], and conducting research into the Ring's history that the reader will learn only in pieces.

> [!key-claim] **The Seventeen-Year Gap as Narrative Compression**
> The gap between Chapter 1 (the party, T.A. 3001) and Chapter 2 (Gandalf's return, T.A. 3018) is one of Tolkien's boldest structural choices. It skips over nearly two decades in which Frodo leads a quiet, happy life — precisely the kind of life the Ring should be poisoning. Tolkien's point is subtle: the Ring does not corrupt Frodo visibly because Frodo never uses it and never craves its power. He wears it only occasionally, and without purpose. This patience in the narrative — this refusal to rush the corruption — makes the danger of the Ring feel more real when Gandalf finally explains it. The reader understands that what they *see* is not what the Ring is doing beneath the surface.

### The Textual World of Book I

The primary text for this report is the [[50th Anniversary Edition of The Lord of the Rings]], which represents the most carefully edited version of Tolkien's text, restoring punctuation and wording from earlier printings that had been altered by typesetters without authorization. For the history of the Nazgûl, we draw on [[Unfinished Tales]], specifically the essay "The Hunt for the Ring," which provides crucial detail about the Nine's mobilization. For questions of authorial intent, we draw primarily on [[The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien]], especially Letters 131 (to Milton Waldman — the great synoptic letter explaining the entire legendarium), 144 (to Naomi Mitchison — which addresses Tom Bombadil directly), and 153 (to Peter Hastings — which addresses the question of Bombadil's nature more fully).

> [!important] **A Note on Tonal Register: *The Hobbit* vs. *The Fellowship***
> Perhaps the most important structural fact about Book I is that it consciously begins in the register of *The Hobbit* — warm, domestic, slightly comic — and shifts register gradually as the chapters accumulate. Chapter 1 reads very nearly like *The Hobbit* might. By Chapter 11 (the attack at [[Weathertop]]), we are in a completely different kind of story. This tonal migration is deliberate and crucial: Tolkien does not want readers to feel manipulated by an abrupt gear-shift. He wants the shadow to grow as naturally as it grows in real life — so gradually that you cannot identify the precise moment it became overwhelming.

> [!ask-yourself-this] **Conceptual Checkpoint**
> Before we proceed to the evidence, make sure the following distinction is secure in your understanding: the difference between the **Ring's general corrupting influence** (its tendency to extend life while eroding will, to whisper promises of power to its bearer) and the **specific threat of the Morgul-blade** (a weapon designed to splinter a fragment of itself into a victim and draw them into the wraith-world). These are two different mechanisms. Can you explain, in your own words, why Frodo at the end of Chapter 12 is in more immediate danger than Frodo has been at any point before — not because the Ring is corrupting him, but because of something else entirely?

## Phase III: Evidence from the Texts

### The Birthday Party and Bilbo's Departure (Chapter 1)

The opening chapters of *The Fellowship of the Ring* are deceptive in their warmth. The extended party scene of Chapter 1 — "A Long-Expected Party" — is a celebration of Hobbit domestic comedy: the fireworks, the speeches, the gossip, the absurd gift-giving customs with their elaborate classifications of Mathoms. But even here, in the first pages, Tolkien is laying foundations that will bear enormous weight.

> [!evidence] **Textual Note: "Stretched" — Bilbo's Diagnosis**
> Bilbo's farewell speech to Gandalf contains a passage often quoted for its comedy but seldom examined for its clinical precision: "I feel all thin, sort of stretched, if you know what I mean: like butter that has been scraped over too much bread. That can't be right. I need a change, or something." (*The Fellowship of the Ring*, "A Long-Expected Party") The image is not merely comic. The sensation Bilbo describes — thinness, extension, being spread too far — is the first direct description in the text of what the Ring does to its bearers over time. The Ring *extends* life by tethering the bearer to the material world beyond their natural span, but the cost is exactly this: the spirit thins, the person is stretched over too great a surface. Bilbo, at 111, has felt for years what he cannot quite name.

Bilbo's departure — his vanishing with a flash and a bang, leaving behind his ring among the silver and gold — is played for laughs and something approaching tears. But what Tolkien is doing structurally is establishing the Ring's first dramatic quality: its reluctance to be parted from its bearer. Even Bilbo, who genuinely wants to leave, who has planned this departure for years, cannot let the Ring go without a struggle. Gandalf must intervene, must speak to him with unusual force, before Bilbo drops it. This is the first demonstration of the Ring's will — not as a malevolent cartoon, but as a gravitational pull that subtly distorts the choices of even a hobbit who loves adventure and freedom above possessions.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Architecture of the Birthday as Threshold**
> "A Long-Expected Party" functions structurally as a threshold-crossing disguised as a festivity. The party is the last fully ordinary moment in the legendarium — the last time [[the Shire]] appears entirely at peace, entirely unaware, entirely itself. From Chapter 2 onward, the Shire is a place under threat even when its residents do not know it. Tolkien makes the party exceptionally long, exceptionally warm, and exceptionally funny because he is giving the reader (and himself) a proper farewell to the domestic world before the shadow enters. This is not indulgence but architecture: the more fully the reader inhabits the Shire's warmth, the more keenly they will feel its vulnerability.

### The Revelation: Gandalf's Return (Chapter 2)

Chapter 2, "The Shadow of the Past," is the engine that drives the entire narrative. It is, by Tolkien's own account in the Foreword, one of the oldest parts of the tale, written early and changed relatively little through the long composition. Everything the book needs to know about its own stakes is communicated here — the history of the Ring, the nature of Sauron's power, the threat of the Nazgûl, the importance of Gollum, and the doctrine of pity that will rule the fate of many.

Gandalf's revelation proceeds in layers, each one deepening the reader's understanding. He first produces the Ring and throws it into Frodo's fire, to Frodo's horror. When the Ring is recovered from the coals unscathed, Gandalf holds it up to the light of the shuttered room:

> "I cannot read the fiery letters," said Frodo in a quavering voice.
> "No," said Gandalf, "but I can. The letters are Elvish, of an ancient mode, but the language is that of Mordor, which I will not utter here."
> — *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "The Shadow of the Past"

> [!evidence] **Textual Note: The Fire-Writing Scene**
> The fire-writing scene is one of the great dramatic moments of twentieth-century literature, and it is constructed with precise theatrical economy. Gandalf closes the shutters, darkening the room. He throws the Ring into the fire — a gesture of apparently reckless confidence that is actually a proof. He waits. He retrieves the Ring with tongs. He hands it to Frodo cool to the touch. He tells Frodo to hold it up and look closely. And only then do the letters appear: lines of fire, *finer than the finest pen-strokes*, *piercingly bright yet remote*. The delay, the darkness, the gradual revelation — all of it creates a theatre of dread. We do not simply learn that the Ring is dangerous; we see it *demonstrate* its own nature in a controlled experiment that the reader experiences alongside Frodo.

The layers of the revelation continue as Gandalf traces the Ring's history from the [[Second Age]] — from the forging in [[Orodruin]], to its cutting from Sauron's hand by [[Isildur]], to its loss in the [[Anduin]], to Gollum's finding it, and finally to Bilbo's finding in the dark under the [[Misty Mountains]]. This is a masterwork of compressed history: Tolkien takes five thousand years of the Second and Third Ages and renders them in perhaps twenty minutes of conversation in a Hobbit-hole study.

Then comes the key moral moment of Book I — perhaps the key moral moment of the entire trilogy:

> "'He deserves death.'
> 'Deserves it! I daresay he does. Many that live deserve death. And some that die deserve life. Can you give it to them? Then do not be too eager to deal out death in judgement. For even the very wise cannot see all ends. I have not much hope that Gollum can be cured before he dies, but there is a chance of it. And he is bound up with the fate of the Ring. My heart tells me that he has some part to play yet, for good or ill, before the end; and when that comes, the pity of Bilbo may rule the fate of many — yours not least.'"
> — Gandalf to Frodo, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "The Shadow of the Past"

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: The Architecture of Providence Through Pity**
> Gandalf's speech about pity is not merely a counsel of mercy — it is a window into the theological structure of [[The Lord of the Rings]]. Tolkien was deeply Catholic, and his understanding of providence — the operation of a higher will through apparently contingent events — shapes the entire narrative. Frodo is not asked to believe that good will triumph; he is asked to refrain from presuming to know all ends. The pity of Bilbo, which allowed Gollum to live, will eventually (as the reader who has reached Mount Doom knows) literally save the world. Providence operates here not through miraculous intervention but through the consequences of mercy shown to an undeserving creature. This is the theological claim that the book is making: that acts of mercy and pity, even when directed at the seemingly unworthy, propagate effects that no human wisdom could have foreseen. It is a profoundly Augustinian and Boethian vision of time — in which events whose meaning is opaque in the moment become legible from a later vantage point.

### The First Encounter: Black Riders in the Shire (Chapters 3–5)

The first appearance of a [[Black Rider]] on the road out of Hobbiton — Chapter 3, "Three Is Company" — is a masterpiece of horror through indirection. The hobbits hear a horse approaching, and feel an urgency to hide that they cannot quite explain. They dive behind tree-roots just as the rider comes around the bend and stops directly above them. He does not speak, but the narrative renders his presence in physical terms that make the reader's skin crawl:

The Rider *sniffs* the air. He *crouches* toward where the hobbits are hidden. Only the arrival of a company of Elves singing drives him away — their light and song literally repelling his presence. The hobbits do not see his face. The reader does not see his face. And this invisibility — this presentation of evil as a *sensed presence* rather than a *seen figure* — is far more effective than any description of a monster could be.

> [!analytical-insight] **Horror Through Indirection: Tolkien's Technique of Implied Dread**
> Tolkien's handling of the Nazgûl in the early chapters demonstrates a principle of horror construction that is far more sophisticated than most popular fantasy manages: the reader's imagination, given partial information and strong physical sensation, will construct a threat more frightening than any explicit description. We know the Rider *sniffs* — which implies he perceives the hobbits through smell rather than sight, which implies a kind of inhuman animal awareness that is deeply unsettling. We know he *bends toward them* — which means he has some perception of their presence without quite locating it. We do not know what would happen if he found them. That uncertainty is the source of the dread. Tolkien manages this restraint across four chapters before we receive any explicit information about what the Black Riders actually are.

### Gildor Inglorion and the Limits of Elvish Counsel (Chapter 3)

The company of Elves led by [[Gildor Inglorion]] — who introduces himself as of the House of [[Finrod]], one of the great Noldorin lords, exiles from [[Valinor]] now returning at last to the West — provides Frodo with shelter, warmth, and an inkling of the danger that pursues him. But Gildor is characteristically Elvish in his reluctance to speak plainly:

> "'I warn you that peril is now both before you and behind you, and upon either side... Flee them! Speak no words to them! They are deadly. Ask no more of me! But my heart forbodes that, ere all is ended, you, Frodo son of Drogo, will know more of these fell things than Gildor Inglorion.'"
> — *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "Three Is Company"

> [!key-insight] **Linguistic Observation: "Ere all is ended"**
> Gildor's phrase "ere all is ended" carries the weight of Elvish temporal consciousness. For an Elf — particularly an exile of the [[House of Finrod]] who has lived through the [[First Age]] and much of the Third — "all" ending means something of enormous scope. Gildor is speaking, perhaps without fully articulating it even to himself, of an ending that has been building since the [[Music of the Ainur]] — the resolution of the long contest between Light and Shadow that has been running since [[Morgoth]] first introduced discord. For Frodo, it sounds like a dire personal prophecy; for Gildor, it is a statement about the end of an age.

### The Old Forest and Tom Bombadil (Chapters 6–7)

The [[Old Forest]] and the two chapters spent with [[Tom Bombadil]] represent what is arguably the most debated episode in all of [[The Lord of the Rings]] — beloved by some, perplexing to many, and cut entirely from nearly every adaptation. To understand why Tolkien put it here — so early, so seemingly tangential to the main quest — requires understanding what it *is* that Bombadil represents.

The Old Forest is one of the oldest surviving fragments of the vast prehistoric woodland that once covered much of [[Eriador]]. The trees within it are not merely old — they are resentful, conscious, hostile. Old Man Willow, the most powerful and malicious of the ancient trees, nearly kills Merry and Pippin by drawing them into enchanted sleep and folding them into his roots. This is horror of a different kind from the Black Riders: it is the horror of a nature that is not benign, not pastoral, not the comforting Nature of romantic poetry, but ancient, proud, and filled with a cold malice toward anything that goes free upon the earth.

Into this scene Tom Bombadil arrives, singing nonsensically, and rescues the hobbits with a command. He is jovial, inexplicable, and enormous. His very nature resists categories.

Tom handles the Ring — puts it on his little finger — and does not vanish. He spins it in the air, makes it disappear as a trick, hands it back. The Ring has no hold over him. And when Frodo slips the Ring on in a test, to see if he becomes invisible to Tom, Tom can see him perfectly well. Frodo slips the Ring on, and Tom's *eyes* simply see him, ring and all.

> "'Eldest, that's what I am. Mark my words, my friends: Tom was here before the river and the trees; Tom remembers the first raindrop and the first acorn. He made paths before the Big People, and saw the little People arriving. He was here before the Kings and the graves and the Barrow-wights. When the Elves passed westward, Tom was here already, before the seas were bent. He knew the dark under the stars when it was fearless — before the Dark Lord came from Outside.'"
> — Tom Bombadil, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "In the House of Tom Bombadil"

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: Tom Bombadil and the Uncorruptible**
> The question of why the Ring has no power over Tom Bombadil is one of the great unresolved puzzles of the legendarium. Tolkien's most direct statement comes in Letter 144, where he explains that Tom represents something outside the quest for power and domination entirely: he is not interested in possession. The Ring works by offering dominion — it amplifies the desire for control that already exists in a person's heart. Tom simply does not have that desire. He is the "spirit of the (vanishing) Oxford and Berkshire countryside," Tolkien wrote — a nature spirit, perhaps a Maia of [[Aulë]] or [[Yavanna]] who chose to remain in the material world for love of it, not in the sense of possessing it but in the sense of delighting in it. He cannot be corrupted because there is nothing in him to corrupt in the relevant sense: no will to dominate, no desire to be more than he is. The Ring's grammar of temptation is simply inapplicable to him. He is, paradoxically, invulnerable to ultimate evil not through strength but through a kind of radical contentment.

### The Barrow-downs (Chapter 8)

The [[Barrow-downs]] episode — in which the hobbits are trapped in an ancient burial mound by a Barrow-wight, one of the evil spirits sent by the [[Witch-king of Angmar]] to haunt the burial mounds of the [[Men of Cardolan]] — is another episode frequently omitted from adaptations, and another that rewards close attention. Its function is tripartite: it provides a genuine brush with death that prepares the hobbits emotionally for what is coming; it gives Merry, Pippin, and Sam a gift of [[Númenórean]] barrow-swords that will later prove crucial (Merry's blade is the only one in Middle-earth that could have destroyed the sorcery protecting the [[Witch-king]]'s body); and it gives us a different register of ancient horror from either the Black Riders or the Old Forest — the cold, whispering dread of the long dead reaching out to claim the living.

Frodo, lying in the barrow, does not succumb. He remembers Tom Bombadil's rescue-song. In a moment of desperate courage entirely unexpected from the comfort-loving hobbit we met in Chapter 1, he recites it, and Tom comes.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **The Barrow-downs ↔ Anglo-Saxon Burial Archaeology**
> The Barrow-downs are one of the places in [[The Lord of the Rings]] where Tolkien's professional expertise as an [[Old English]] and [[Anglo-Saxon]] scholar most directly shapes his subcreation. Burial mounds — *beorgas*, *hlæws* — are sites of tremendous numinous power in Anglo-Saxon poetry. In *Beowulf*, the dragon guards a hoard in a burial mound; the warrior Wiglaf notes that the cursed gold brings death. In Old English *elegy* (poems like "The Wanderer" and "The Ruin"), the memory of the dead presses upon the living with a cold, formal melancholy. Tolkien, who wrote extensively on *Beowulf*, understood these burial mounds as portals between the living world and the world of the unquiet dead. The [[Barrow-wights]] are not invented from nothing — they are a transposition of the genuine dread that Anglo-Saxon literature builds around burial mounds, given specific narrative form. The swords the hobbits find there — made during the wars of [[Arnor]] against [[Angmar]] in the Third Age — are another layer of Tolkien's characteristic method: every detail is embedded in a history, and that history matters.

### Bree, Strider, and the Prancing Pony (Chapters 9–10)

[[Bree]] is the first town the hobbits reach outside the Shire, and it is a genuinely different place — a village at the crossing of the [[East-West Road]] and the [[Greenway]], inhabited by both Men and Hobbits, with an atmosphere of blunt commerce and curious eyes very unlike the insular warmth of Hobbiton. [[The Prancing Pony]], the inn where they take shelter, is immediately recognizable as a place of multiple agendas: Frodo's disastrous performance (he slips on the Ring accidentally and vanishes in front of the entire common room) has already alerted every potential informant to the presence of a Hobbit with a magical ring.

But the crucial encounter is not in the common room. It is with the dark, weather-beaten figure sitting alone in the corner, who calls himself Strider.

> "'I am older than I look,'" Strider tells the hobbits. "'I might prove useful.'"
> — *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "Strider"

This is the understatement that anchors one of Tolkien's great sustained effects: the revelation of [[Aragorn]]'s true nature, which is not completed in full until the Council of Elrond (Book II, Chapter 2), but which is prepared in these Bree chapters with exquisite care. Strider appears sinister, evasive, and potentially dangerous. Sam distrusts him immediately and vocally. The hobbits have every reason to be suspicious, and Tolkien plays that suspicion honestly — he does not stack the deck in Strider's favor too quickly. When Strider reveals that he knows Gandalf, when he produces the letter from Butterbur that Gandalf left months ago, when Frodo reads it and reads the verse about the broken sword reforged, the pieces begin to fall into place — but only pieces, not the full picture.

> [!analytical-insight] **Strider and the Theme of Hidden Nobility**
> The introduction of Aragorn as Strider — the filthy Ranger whose evident care and competence is masked by an exterior of deliberate seediness — is the first major deployment of a theme that runs throughout *The Lord of the Rings*: that true authority and nobility are not displayed but concealed, that the greatest among those living in Middle-earth in the Third Age have chosen anonymity and service over status and recognition. The line of [[Isildur]] has been guarding the Shire and the North for generations without acknowledgment, without gratitude, wearing down year by year. Tolkien, in a letter to his publisher (Letter 131), described this aspect of his theme as connected to his conviction that "the most heroic people" are often "unnoticed." The greatest king of his age is mistaken for a dangerous tramp. This is not incidental irony — it is a theological point about the nature of true greatness.

### Weathertop and the Morgul-blade (Chapter 11)

[[Weathertop]] — [[Amon Sûl]], the old watch-tower of [[Arnor]] — is the point at which the narrative crosses from danger into genuine catastrophe. On its summit, Strider shows the hobbits the ruins of the ancient tower, and they find signs that [[Gandalf]] was there recently and fought someone. Then, in the dell below, at night, the five [[Ringwraiths]] come.

Frodo, in a moment of desperate confusion — in the narrative it feels almost involuntary, like the hand of the Ring itself forcing him — puts on the Ring. He sees the Riders as they truly are: tall, menacing figures robed in grey and white, with faces and crowns only visible through the Ring's revelation of the Unseen Realm. The leader of the Nine — the [[Witch-king of Angmar]], greatest of the Nazgûl, once a great King of Men — stabs Frodo in the left shoulder with a long thin knife.

> [!evidence] **Textual Note: The Knife in the Dark**
> The Morgul-blade is one of the more precisely described magical objects in [[The Lord of the Rings]]. It is not merely a poison weapon — it is a sorcerous instrument designed to leave a shard of itself inside the wound that will work its way toward the victim's heart. If the shard reaches the heart, the victim will become a wraith — not dead, but in the fullest sense of the Nazgûl, *un*dead, belonging entirely to the world of shadow. Gandalf later tells Frodo (Book II) that he was "beginning to fade" — that the wound at its deepest was not merely physical but spiritual, that Frodo was being drawn into the Wraith-world as the Nine were drawn. This makes Frodo's wound not a narrative obstacle (a hero injuring a leg, so the plot can slow down) but a specific articulation of the Ring's ultimate threat: not merely death but a perversion of existence in which the person continues, but as a slave to Sauron's will.

### Flight to the Ford (Chapter 12)

The final chapter of Book I is a sustained chase scene across the wild country between Weathertop and the [[Ford of Bruinen]] — roughly two hundred miles of increasingly desperate travel, with Frodo weakening by the day as the Morgul shard works deeper. The arrival of [[Glorfindel]] (the Elf-lord from [[Rivendell]] who is one of the few beings in Middle-earth with the power to openly defy the Nine) and the flight across the Ford — with the Nine pursuing and a great flood conjured by [[Elrond]] and [[Gandalf]] sweeping them away — brings Book I to its culmination.

> "'Noro lim, noro lim, Asfaloth!'"
> — Glorfindel to his horse, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "Flight to the Ford"

> [!key-insight] **Linguistic Observation: Sindarin in the Crisis**
> Glorfindel's command to his horse — *noro lim, noro lim* — is Sindarin for something like "run swift, run swift." The choice to render this moment in [[Sindarin]] rather than the Common Speech is not accidental. When the deepest urgency arrives, when the Elf-lord is calling on all the power and speed of his ancient blood, the natural register is not Westron but Elvish. This is consistent with Tolkien's conception of the Elvish languages as closer to the Music of the Ainur — more deeply consonant with the fabric of Arda — than the languages of Men. The Elvish command is the sound of one of the greatest of the Eldar commanding in the mode proper to his nature, and the horse, bred and named in that tradition, responds accordingly.

## Phase IV: Deep Analysis & Interpretation

### What the Shire Is For

To understand what Book I is doing, you need to understand what the [[Shire]] represents in Tolkien's imaginative economy — and it is both more and less than the obvious answer of "rural England" or "pastoral nostalgia."

Tolkien described the Shire, in Letter 131, as a version of the English countryside as it existed in his memory before World War I — the landscape of Sarehole, where he spent part of his childhood, with its mill and its pond and its farmers. There is nostalgia in it, certainly. But to read the Shire as pure nostalgia is to miss its darker function. The Shire is not the ideal world. It is a world that has deliberately forgotten history. The Hobbits of Frodo's time do not remember the North Kingdom that gave them their land. They do not know the meaning of the ruins of the [[Towers of the Three Watchers]]. They do not wonder about the strange men who pass along their roads. They have built, collectively, an impressive architecture of not-knowing, and they have come to feel that this architecture is simply the nature of things.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **The Shire ↔ Beatus Ille and the Pastoral Tradition**
> Tolkien was too learned a classicist not to be aware that the literary pastoral — from Virgil's *Eclogues* through *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* to the English countryside literature he loved — always places the innocent rural world in tension with a larger, more violent world it cannot fully exclude. The Horatian *beatus ille* — the blessed man who lives simply far from the corruption of ambition and war — was always an ideal shadowed by its own impossibility. Tolkien's Shire participates in this tradition but presses it further: the Shire's pastoral innocence is historically contingent, purchased by centuries of Ranger guardianship that the Hobbits neither know about nor acknowledge. When Gandalf says to Strider, at the Council of Elrond, that the Hobbits have been "shielded from the dark" without their knowledge, he is naming a moral condition as well as a historical fact. The Shire is not Eden — it is a protected enclosure whose protection is invisible to its inhabitants. The shadow that falls across it in Chapter 2 is the shadow of history asserting its claims.

### The Nazgûl as Theological Statement

The [[Nazgûl]] are one of the great inventions of *The Lord of the Rings* — and their greatness lies not in their power but in what they mean. Understanding them requires tracing their origin through Gandalf's account in Chapter 2: nine mortal kings who were given Rings of Power, whose Rings "ensnared" them, who "fell under the dominion of the One" and became "shadows under his great Shadow."

> "'Nine he gave to Mortal Men, proud and great, and so ensnared them. Long ago they fell under the dominion of the One, and they became Ringwraiths, shadows under his great Shadow, his most terrible servants.'"
> — Gandalf, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "The Shadow of the Past"

What Tolkien is describing here — in a register that may not be immediately obvious — is a corruption of the [[Gift of Men]]. In the legendarium's theology, the [[Gift of Men]] is death: mortality, the capacity to leave the world, to go beyond the circles of Arda into an unknown but not feared destination. The [[Elves]] are bound to the world; their immortality is a kind of sublime imprisonment in time. Men are free of time — they die, and go elsewhere. This is the [[Doom of Men]] and also, in the deepest theological sense, their greatest gift.

The Rings of Power promised the Nine something that sounded like the reversal of this gift: extended life, continuing power, immortality. And they received it — at a cost. They could not die, but their spirit faded, stretched beyond its natural span until it was thin as vapor, inhabiting a body that grew ever more transparent to the ordinary world and more real in the Unseen Realm. They became neither living nor dead, trapped in the world they had refused to leave, servants of the will that had given them their terrible tenure.

> [!thought-experiment] **Theological Reflection: The Nazgûl as Inverted Gift**
> The theological architecture here is precisely Augustinian *privatio boni* — evil as not a substance but a privation, a removing of a good that was proper to the creature. The Nine were once great kings. Their greatness was proper to them — it was real. What the Rings did was not add evil to them but remove the goods that had defined their humanity: freedom, mortality, the capacity for genuine choice, the ability to die and go to their appointed end. What remains is not a new kind of creature but a *diminished* creature — power without soul, existence without life, continuity without purpose. The [[Witch-king of Angmar]], who at the height of his power in the Third Age brought down the North Kingdom of Arnor, is not the most powerful being in Middle-earth; he is the most terrifyingly *diminished* — a king who chose to remain and became a slave. That is the horror of the Nazgûl, and why they are genuinely frightening rather than merely monstrous: they are not other than human; they are the destination that awaits human pride and the desire for domination carried to its ultimate conclusion.

### Gradual Revelation as Narrative Theology

One of the most consistent techniques across the twelve chapters of Book I is what might be called *Tolkien's pedagogy of dread*: the information about the Ring and its danger is released in calibrated doses, with each chapter giving the reader and Frodo one more layer of understanding without ever arriving at complete knowledge. In Chapter 3, Frodo feels the Black Rider's presence without understanding what it is. In Chapter 7, Tom Bombadil handles the Ring safely, suggesting limits to its power the reader does not yet fully understand. In Chapter 9, Strider tells them the Riders are "terrible" without specifying how. In Chapter 11, we see the Riders in the Unseen Realm — and only then do we begin to understand the Ring's own optical grammar, how it shifts the wearer's perception toward the world in which the Nazgûl are most real.

> [!analytical-insight] **The Pedagogy of Dread: Tolkien's Distributed Revelation**
> The technique of distributed revelation — parceling out dangerous knowledge across many chapters rather than delivering it in one scene — is not merely a pacing device. It enacts, structurally, the epistemological condition of the characters in the story. Frodo does not have access to the *Silmarillion*'s full history of Sauron. He does not know what the Council of Elrond will later make plain. He knows exactly as much as he needs to know to take the next step, and no more. Tolkien is asking his reader to inhabit Frodo's incomplete understanding rather than adopt a God's-eye view of the legendarium. The effect is that the *reader's* learning curve mirrors *Frodo's* — we become anxious alongside him because we are uncertain alongside him. This is a profound narrative choice, and it creates the specific quality of dread that *The Lord of the Rings* generates: not the dread of knowing a monster is behind the door, but the dread of knowing *something* is there and not quite being able to name it.

### The Encounter With Strider and the Trust Problem

The Strider chapters present the reader with a question that is not fully resolved within Book I: can Frodo — can any of the hobbits — trust a being they know so little about, in a crisis where trust itself is a weapon that enemies might exploit? Gandalf's letter, which Butterbur has forgotten to deliver for months, gives Strider partial validation. But it is a test of character as much as a resolution of uncertainty. Frodo ultimately decides to trust Strider not on the basis of complete proof but on an intuition of quality:

> "'No,' he said slowly. 'I don't agree. I think, I think you are not really as you choose to look. You began to talk to me like the Bree-folk, but your voice has changed.'"
> — Frodo to Strider, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, "Strider"

This moment — Frodo's perception that Strider's *voice* carries a different register than his appearance would suggest — is the first moment of genuinely heroic discernment in the narrative. Frodo is not safe. He is not confident. But he recognizes, in the way that only great literature can demonstrate, that surface and depth are different things, and that the depth in Strider's voice tells a story different from his battered exterior. It is the intuition of a person who has, without knowing it, been prepared to see beneath surfaces by his upbringing in Bilbo's household and his long friendship with an old wanderer who himself valued the hidden depth of things.

> [!cross-domain-connection] **Strider's Concealment ↔ the Norse *Grimnismál***
> Tolkien was deeply versed in Norse mythology, and the figure of the disguised god — [[Odin]] wandering in human form, testing the worthiness of those he encounters — is one of the Norse tradition's signature contributions. In the *Grimnismál*, the *Prose Edda*, and the *Vafþrúðnismál*, Odin appears as a ragged, one-eyed wanderer, dismissed and mistreated by those who should know better, eventually revealing himself to devastating effect. Strider participates in this tradition: he is the hidden king, the wandering protector, the figure of greatest authority in the narrative who presents himself as a dangerous vagabond. Tolkien does not copy the Norse template — Aragorn is not a trickster, and his revelation will carry different theological weight — but the archetype is there, recognized at a deep level by readers steeped in the same mythological tradition that shaped Tolkien's imagination.

## Phase V: Themes, Significance & Limitations

### Mortality, the Gift of Men, and the Perversion of Both

The Nazgûl are not the only place in Book I where Tolkien's theology of mortality surfaces. The entire theme of the Ring's gift — extended life — is shot through with a meditation on what mortality means. [[Bilbo]], at 111, feels "stretched": this is the Ring's version of immortality, which is not the Elves' profound rootedness in history and song but a thinning, a straining, a stretching of the self beyond its proper span. The Elves who move through the Shire headed West are going *home*; they are immortal, but their immortality is designed — it has a direction and a purpose. The Ring's immortality is purposeless and directionless, merely a continuation for its own sake.

[[Frodo]]'s wound changes his relationship to mortality in a specific way. The Morgul-blade is drawing him toward wraith-hood — not death but *un*death, a perversion of both mortality (the natural end that sets Men free) and immortality (the rootedness in the world that gives the Elves their purpose). He would become neither. He would become a shadow, belonging to [[Sauron]]'s will entirely.

### The Pastoral and Its Costs

> [!warning] **Common Misconception: "The Shire Is Entirely Safe Before the Quest Begins"**
> Readers sometimes assume that the Shire's peril only begins when Frodo departs. In fact, the [[Nazgûl]] enter the Shire during the period of Frodo's journey — they question [[Farmer Maggot]], they station riders near the [[Brandywine Ferry]], and one eventually reaches [[Hobbiton]]. The Shire's safety was never absolute; it was maintained by [[Gandalf]]'s vigilance and the [[Rangers]]' guardianship, both of which are in crisis at the moment Frodo needs them most. What the quest narrative does is not introduce danger into a safe world but make visible a danger that was already present and already being managed.

The pastoral ideal of the Shire has a further complication: it depends on forgetting. The Hobbits do not mourn the North Kingdom they cannot remember; they do not honor the Rangers who protect them without acknowledgment; they do not think of the wider world's struggles as their concern. This comfortable forgetting is not presented as purely innocent. Tolkien, in the voice of [[Gildor]], touches on it: "But this is not your own Shire... Others dwelt here before hobbits were; and others will dwell here again when hobbits are no more." The Shire belongs to no one ultimately; its peacefulness is borrowed, contingent, temporary.

### Limitations of This Analysis

This report focuses primarily on narrative structure, theological resonance, and thematic architecture. It does not treat in depth:

- The full political geography of [[Eriador]] in the Third Age, which provides the historical context for why the East-West Road matters and what [[Bree]] represents as a cultural threshold;
- The complete history of the [[Nazgûl]] as developed in [[Unfinished Tales]], which provides important detail about which nine kings received the Rings, and the specific role of the [[Witch-king of Angmar]];
- The compositional history of the early chapters, traced in [[The Return of the Shadow]], which reveals how radically the story evolved — how Strider was once called Trotter and was at one stage a Hobbit, how the Black Riders were originally not identified as Ringwraiths until late in composition.

These are each topics that reward their own dedicated reports.

## Phase VI: Synthesis, Integration & Original Contribution

### The Twelve-Chapter Arc as Complete Mythological Journey

Book I of *The Fellowship of the Ring* is, among other things, a complete mythological journey compressed into twelve chapters. It follows a structure that mythologists from Ovid to Joseph Campbell have described — the movement from the *mundus*, the known world, through a series of liminal thresholds into a transformed world where different rules apply — with the specificity and care of someone who knows the tradition without being confined by it.

The journey runs: **Home** (Bag End, Chapters 1–2) → **Departure with Incomplete Knowledge** (Chapters 3–5, crossing the Shire) → **First Threshold Guardians** (Chapter 6–7, the Old Forest and Tom Bombadil) → **The Underworld** (Chapter 8, the Barrow-downs) → **The World Beyond the Known** (Chapters 9–10, Bree and Strider) → **The Wound** (Chapter 11, Weathertop) → **The Desperate Flight** (Chapter 12, toward Rivendell). What awaits at the end of this journey — the [[Council of Elrond]], the revelation of the full legendarium, the formation of the [[Fellowship]] — is the second, grander arc: having been prepared by Book I, Frodo (and the reader) is now capable of understanding what they are actually dealing with.

> [!original-synthesis] **Book I as an Education in Kinds of Fear**
> Looking across the twelve chapters, what becomes visible is that Tolkien has provided Frodo — and the reader — with a systematic education in *kinds of fear*. Each major episode introduces a different register of dread: the first Black Rider is fear of the unseen, of a presence perceived but not identified; the Old Forest is fear of nature without moral alignment — of a world that was ancient before hobbits and will be ancient after; Tom Bombadil represents the uncanny (the fearful encounter with something that does not fit categories); the Barrow-downs introduce fear of death reaching back across time to claim the living; Strider represents the fear of misplaced trust; Weathertop introduces fear of bodily harm and spiritual diminishment; the flight to the Ford is fear of being overtaken, of a deadline that cannot be outrun. By the end of Book I, Frodo has been educated in every major form that danger takes in the legendarium — not in the abstract, but in his body, in his shoulder, in his increasingly dim sight. He arrives at [[Rivendell]] not merely as a wounded hobbit but as someone who has, for the first time, fully understood the scale of what he is carrying. The Council of Elrond's revelations will not shock him because Book I has prepared him — and us — to absorb them.

### What the Pity of Bilbo Means for Book I

The key theological insight of Book I — planted in Chapter 2 and not harvested until the very end of the entire trilogy — is the doctrine of providential mercy. "The pity of Bilbo may rule the fate of many." This is a statement about how [[Eru Ilúvatar]]'s will operates in history: not through the grand gestures of armies and heroes but through small, private acts of mercy extended to the unworthy, whose consequences ripple forward in ways that no wisdom at the time of the act could have predicted.

Frodo is not the Ring-bearer because he is the most powerful, the most wise, or the most heroic. Gandalf says so explicitly: "You may be sure that it was not for any merit that others do not possess." He is the Ring-bearer because of a chain of mercy: Bilbo's pity for Gollum allowed Gollum to live; Gollum's survival preserved the Ring in a lineage that could eventually deliver it to [[Mount Doom]]. This is not a plot convenience — it is the theological architecture of the story.

> [!original-synthesis] **The Theological Argument of Book I in Summary**
> Book I makes, in narrative form, the following theological argument: The world is in peril from a power whose nature is the privation of good — the hollowing-out of real greatness into mere domination. This power operates by exploiting the desire for immortality and dominance latent in all rational creatures. The only counter-force that actually works against it is not superior power but the refusal to participate in its logic: the pity that does not deal out death in judgment, the contentment that does not desire to rule, the small hobbits who bear rather than wield. The Ring cannot be used against [[Sauron]] because any use of it, by anyone, enters the Ring's grammar of domination. It can only be resisted by carrying it without using it, and finally destroyed by those who are willing to destroy their own power rather than exercise it. This is the logic that runs from Bilbo's pity in Chapter 2 all the way to the fires of [[Orodruin]] — and Book I, across twelve chapters of warmth and terror and gradual revelation, is the introduction to that logic.

## Phase VIII: Appendix

### A. Lexicon of Key Terms

> [!definition] **Bag End** (Westron/English hybrid)
> The hole in the Hill above [[Hobbiton]] in the Westfarthing of [[the Shire]], home of the [[Baggins]] family and specifically of [[Bilbo Baggins]] and his heir [[Frodo Baggins]]. The name is a good-natured pun: in English, a "bag end" (or cul-de-sac) is a street that has no exit — a dead end. The Baggins home is literally at the end of a bag-shaped road. The significance is not just topographic comedy: Bag End is a comfortable, finished, contained world — the kind of place where one need never look too far ahead, because the road ends there. The quest begins when that containment is ruptured.

> [!definition] **Ringwraith** (Common Speech; also **Nazgûl**, Sindarin; **Úlairi**, Quenya)
> One of the nine former kings of Men who were given [[Rings of Power]] by [[Sauron]] in the Second Age, and who became enslaved to the [[One Ring]] and faded into wraith-form. They perceive the world primarily in the Unseen Realm (the spirit-world that interpenetrates the physical world of Middle-earth), and are drawn irresistibly to the One Ring, which they can perceive and feel as a great burning wheel of fire. They are in some sense among the most pitiable creatures in the legendarium: not dead, not alive, not free, not destroyed — merely continuing, in servitude, forever.

> [!definition] **Morgul-blade** (from Sindarin *morgul*, "dark sorcery")
> A long, thin knife of Nazgûl-craft, designed to leave a shard within its victim that works toward the heart. If the shard reaches the heart, the victim is transformed into a wraith — drawn into the shadow-world and compelled to serve Sauron's will. The blade is destroyed on contact with ordinary light and air; what remains (the hilt which Strider keeps) is the evidence of its nature. The wound it inflicts is not merely physical but spiritual: it is a hole in the barrier between the ordinary world and the wraith-world, through which the victim slowly falls.

> [!definition] **Amon Sûl** (Sindarin: "Hill of Wind"; anglicized as **Weathertop**)
> The southernmost peak of the [[Weather Hills]], located roughly halfway between [[Bree]] and the [[Ford of Bruinen]]. On its summit stood one of the greatest [[Palantíri]]-towers of [[Arnor]], built in the early Third Age; by the time of our story, it is a ruin — "a tumbled ring, like a rough crown on the old hill's head," as Strider describes it. Historically, it is one of the most significant sites in [[Eriador]]: Elendil is said to have watched from it for the coming of [[Gil-galad]] during the [[Last Alliance]]. It was at Weathertop that the [[Witch-king of Angmar]] struck his decisive blow against the North Kingdom in T.A. 1409, burning and destroying the tower, after which the Palantír of Amon Sûl was evacuated to Fornost.

> [!definition] **Gildor Inglorion** (Quenya/Sindarin hybrid: of the House of Finrod)
> A [[Noldorin]] [[Elf]] of the House of [[Finrod Felagund]] encountered by Frodo and company on the road out of Hobbiton. He is one of the *Exiles* — the descendants of the Noldor who came to Middle-earth in the [[Years of the Trees]] and who are now, in the late Third Age, returning to [[Valinor]]. His greeting to Frodo — "Elen síla lúmenn' omentielvo" ("A star shines on the hour of our meeting") — is the first fully attested Quenya sentence in the novel. He cannot tell Frodo everything, and does not, but his warning is genuine and his urgency real.

> [!definition] **Tom Bombadil** (English; Elvish name: **Iarwain Ben-adar**, "Oldest and Fatherless"; Dwarvish: **Forn**; Men of the North: **Orald**)
> An ancient and inexplicable figure dwelling in the valley of the [[Withywindle]] between [[the Old Forest]] and the [[Barrow-downs]]. He is described as the eldest living thing in [[Arda]] — pre-dating the coming of the Elves, the building of the Barrows, even the bending of the seas when the world was changed from flat to round. He is immune to the [[One Ring]] because he does not desire to possess or dominate; the Ring's temptations are structurally inapplicable to a being whose nature is defined by delight rather than desire for power. His wife [[Goldberry]], the River-daughter, is similarly outside the normal categories of the legendarium.

> [!definition] **The Barrow-wights** (Old English *wight*, "creature" or "being"; specifically a revenent)
> Evil spirits sent by the [[Witch-king of Angmar]] to inhabit the burial mounds — *barrows* — of the [[Men of Cardolan]], one of the sub-kingdoms of [[Arnor]] that fell in the wars against Angmar. These spirits animate the bones of the dead and create an atmosphere of cold, whispering dread. They are not the spirits of the men buried there; they are alien entities that have taken possession of the graves, using the accumulated gold and bone to attract and trap living creatures. The swords found in the Barrow by the hobbits are from the Arnor wars and have specific significance: they were made specifically to counter the sorcery of Angmar.

> [!definition] **Strider / Aragorn** (Common Speech name / Quenya heritage: **Aragorn II**, son of Arathorn)
> The heir of [[Isildur]], [[Chieftain of the Dúnedain]], and by birthright the rightful King of both [[Arnor]] and [[Gondor]], the two successor-states of [[Númenor]]. He has lived most of his eighty-eight years in anonymity, serving as a Ranger of the North and protecting the [[Shire]] and [[Bree-land]] without acknowledgment. He is counted among the greatest hunters and trackers in Middle-earth; he is approximately 87 years old at the time of the quest, which for a man of Númenórean descent means he is in full middle age. His introduction as Strider — suspicious, battered, apparently dangerous — is one of Tolkien's great sustained deceptions of the reader.

> [!definition] **The Old Forest** (Common Speech)
> The ancient woodland between [[Buckland]] and the [[Barrow-downs]], a remnant of the vast primordial forests that once covered all of [[Eriador]]. The trees within it are ancient, proud, and malicious — they remember when they were lords of the land and resent anything that moves freely upon the earth. Old Man Willow, the most powerful of the Forest's trees, is their effective ruler: his roots run beneath the soil for miles, his whispered song spreads drowsiness and enchantment, and his deepest desire is to absorb the free things of the world into himself.

> [!definition] **The Ford of Bruinen** (Sindarin: *Bruinen*, "Loudwater"; Westron rendering of *Loudwater*)
> The river-crossing at the eastern end of the [[East-West Road]] that marks the boundary of [[Rivendell]]'s territory. The river [[Bruinen]] runs from the [[Misty Mountains]] down through the valley of Rivendell; [[Elrond]] has the power to command its flooding at need, which is the mechanism by which the pursuing Nazgûl are destroyed at the end of Book I. The ford is one of the three great defensive thresholds of northern Middle-earth, alongside the Bridge of [[Khazad-dûm]] and the Argonath.

> [!definition] **Eucatastrophe** (Tolkien's coinage; Greek *eu* + *katastrophe*, "good reversal")
> Tolkien's term for the sudden, joyful reversal — the unexpected turn from disaster to redemption — that he identified as the defining feature of the fairy-story at its highest pitch, and which he connected to the Christian concept of the Gospel (the "good news") as the eucatastrophe of human history. Book I does not contain a eucatastrophe; it contains the conditions for one. The flooding of the [[Bruinen]] that washes away the Nazgûl is the nearest thing to it in these chapters, and it is significant that it is conjured not by Frodo's heroism but by [[Elrond]] and [[Gandalf]] working in coordination — the unseen hands of the higher powers that shape the narrative's providential arc.
