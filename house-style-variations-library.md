# House Style Variations Library

> [!abstract] What This Document Is
> Ten variations on the [[Pur3v4d3r's House Style — Canonical Reference|Patient Synthesizer (Figured)]] baseline. Every variation **preserves the baseline DNA** — cross-paragraph integrative arcs, mixed-wave cadence, scholarly register, controlled metaphor — and varies a **single dimension** to suit a particular section type, generator, or affect. Use this when you want your voice but tuned for context. Use the canonical reference when you want the default.

---

## What Stays Constant Across All Variations

> [!definition] Invariant DNA
> Every variation in this library preserves these elements without exception:
> - **Cadence:** Mixed-wave — long developmental sentences (35–50 words) breathing into short release sentences (5–15 words); mean ~22 words; high variance.
> - **Architecture:** Cross-paragraph integrative arcs; the unit of meaning is the paragraph, not the sentence.
> - **Voice:** Third-person impersonal; no first person.
> - **Register:** Scholarly, considered, willing to qualify.
> - **Texture:** Controlled metaphor; one or two figures per paragraph; never flowery.
> - **Signature move:** Family-resemblance / convergent-recognition arguments draw distinct phenomena under shared frames.

What varies between entries is the **opening tendency**, **citation handling**, **affect**, or **reader positioning** — surface choices that change feel without changing identity.

## Baseline Reference (For Comparison)

> [!example] The canonical voice (no variation applied)
> Across nearly a century of cognitive research, the concept of the schema has functioned as a kind of theoretical solvent, dissolving the apparent boundaries between phenomena that earlier psychology had treated as distinct and revealing, beneath the surface differences, a shared architectural commitment. The dissolution happened gradually. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a convergent recognition that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible. The schema, in this sense, is less a discovery than a commitment.

## The Ten Variations

Each variation below specifies what dimension is varied, what is preserved, and when to use it. The exemplar prose follows the same content arc as the baseline (schemas → multiple traditions → convergent recognition → synthesis) so you can isolate the change cleanly.

---

### Variation 1 — Claim-First Opening

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Paragraph-opening tendency. The synthetic claim leads; the development unpacks it. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Synthesis sections; conclusions; sections where the takeaway should arrive immediately rather than be earned through development.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Foundational-Report]] synthesis sections, [[Comparative-Architecture]] verdict sections, the closing chapters of any [[Deep Dive Report]].

> The schema is less a discovery than a commitment, and one of the most consequential commitments cognitive psychology has made. It dissolves boundaries that earlier traditions had treated as fixed, revealing, beneath the surface differences, a shared architectural intuition that the field had long sensed but not yet named. The recognition came gradually. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, refer to the same construct — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and developed different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a single underlying claim: that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place, and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible.

### Variation 2 — Phenomenon-First Opening

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** The empirical anchor leads; the synthesis emerges from the data. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Empirical sections; sections where you want the phenomenon to do the rhetorical work; chapters that introduce a finding before its theoretical home.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Annotated Critical Analysis]] evidence sections, [[Foundational-Report]] empirical chapters, the methodology and findings sections of any generator.

> When Bartlett's Cambridge undergraduates reread "The War of the Ghosts" twenty hours later, then a week later, then weeks after that, what they returned to him was not the story he had given them but a steadily reshaping artifact of their own minds at work. The supernatural elements faded; the unfamiliar logic gave way to English narrative convention. This was not a failure of memory. It was memory operating as memory always operates — by fitting the unfamiliar into structures already in place. The construct Bartlett needed to name what he had seen was the schema, and a century later it has functioned as a kind of theoretical solvent, dissolving the boundaries between Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes. None of these traditions speaks the same formal language. None reduces to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, and the resemblance reflects a convergent recognition that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place — and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible.

### Variation 3 — History-First Opening

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Intellectual lineage is the entry point; the prose moves through time before arriving at synthesis. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Tracing the development of an idea; sections where chronology carries explanatory weight; the opening of generators built around lineage.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Historical-Genealogical]] (its native voice), opening chapters of [[Foundational-Report]] when the topic has a clear origin, the lineage sections of [[First Principles Analysis]].

> The schema entered cognitive psychology in 1932, when Frederic Bartlett published his account of why English readers could not faithfully recall a Native American folktale. From that beginning the construct has migrated, adapted, and recombined across nearly every subsequent program of cognitive research. The trajectory was not linear. Piaget developed it in a developmental vocabulary; Schank and Abelson translated it into the language of scripts and routine action; Minsky reformulated it as frames suitable for artificial intelligence; Rosch extended it through her work on prototypes and category structure. These traditions did not always communicate well across their boundaries. Their formal vocabularies remained, and remain, distinct. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a convergent recognition that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible. The schema, in this sense, is less a discovery than a commitment — one that the field has spent nearly a century learning how to articulate.

### Variation 4 — Question-First Opening

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** A puzzle drives the entry; the synthesis answers it. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Problem-framing sections; chapters that motivate a body of theory by the question it answers; sections that benefit from epistemic tension upfront.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Socratic Exploration]] body sections, [[First Principles Analysis]] motivating chapters, the framing sections of [[Dialectical Report]].

> Why does the same cognitive mechanism that makes us efficient also make us systematically wrong? The question is older than cognitive psychology, but the field has produced what may be the cleanest answer to it: the schema, that organized knowledge structure abstracted from prior experience, which guides perception, comprehension, and recall in ways largely invisible to the person whose cognition it shapes. The answer was assembled gradually. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a convergent recognition that the same structures that organize knowledge for efficient use are precisely the structures that distort what does not fit them. The schema is the form cognition takes. The cost of its efficiency is its invisibility.

### Variation 5 — Contrast-First Opening

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Dialectical entry — the tension is set up, then resolved through the synthesis. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Sections built around an opposition or paradigm shift; chapters where the older framing must be visible for the new one to make sense.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Dialectical Report]] (its native voice), [[Comparative-Architecture]] sections that contrast alternatives, the framing sections of [[First Principles Analysis]] when prior assumptions need to be made visible.

> Earlier psychology tended to treat memory and perception as distinct domains, governed by distinct mechanisms, properly studied by distinct methods. The cognitive concept of the schema has done as much as any single construct to unsettle that division. It has functioned, across nearly a century of research, as a kind of theoretical solvent, dissolving the apparent boundaries between phenomena that the earlier framework had held apart. The dissolution happened gradually. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a convergent recognition that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place. Once that recognition takes hold, the older domain boundaries no longer carve the phenomena at their joints.

### Variation 6 — Inline Parenthetical Citations

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Citation handling. APA-style parenthetical citations integrated into the prose. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Empirically-leaning sections; chapters making claims that should be grounded in source visibility; the methodology and findings sections of research-intensive reports.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Annotated Critical Analysis]] (its citation discipline aligns naturally), the empirical chapters of [[Foundational-Report]] and [[Deep Dive Report]], any section presenting research findings.

> Across nearly a century of cognitive research, the concept of the schema has functioned as a kind of theoretical solvent, dissolving the apparent boundaries between phenomena that earlier psychology had treated as distinct (Alba & Hasher, 1983) and revealing, beneath the surface differences, a shared architectural commitment. The dissolution happened gradually. Bartlett's reconstructive memory (Bartlett, 1932), Piaget's developmental assimilation (Piaget, 1952), Schank and Abelson's scripts (Schank & Abelson, 1977), Minsky's frames (Minsky, 1975), and Rosch's prototypes (Rosch, 1975) do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a convergent recognition that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy (Brewer & Treyens, 1981) and perception's distortion (Loftus & Palmer, 1974) possible. The schema, in this sense, is less a discovery than a commitment.

### Variation 7 — Quietly Elegiac

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Affect dial turned slightly toward looking back across a long arc, with reverence for the work that built the construct. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Historical surveys; chapters that span generations of work; sections where the human dimension of the intellectual tradition matters.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Historical-Genealogical]] late chapters, retrospective sections of [[Foundational-Report]] and [[Deep Dive Report]], conclusion chapters of any generator surveying a long tradition.

> Across nearly a century of cognitive research — across generations of investigators who never met, working in laboratories now closed and traditions now superseded — the concept of the schema has functioned as a kind of theoretical solvent, dissolving the apparent boundaries between phenomena that earlier psychology had treated as distinct. The dissolution happened slowly, and largely without ceremony. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation, and the people who developed them are, by now, nearly all gone. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, and the resemblance is what survives the people. It reflects a convergent recognition, arrived at by routes that often did not know of one another, that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place, and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible. The schema, in this sense, is less a discovery than an inheritance.

### Variation 8 — Subtly Urgent

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Affect dial turned toward stakes. The practical consequences of the construct are visible in the prose. Not alarmist — but the reader feels that this matters. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Application sections; chapters bridging theory to consequence; sections of practitioner-oriented or policy-relevant generators.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Practitioner's Field Guide]] (despite its different default voice, this variant brings the stakes-awareness practitioners need), the application chapters of [[Foundational-Report]], the consequences sections of [[First Principles Analysis]].

> Across nearly a century of cognitive research, the concept of the schema has functioned as a kind of theoretical solvent, dissolving the apparent boundaries between phenomena that earlier psychology had treated as distinct and revealing, beneath the surface differences, a shared architectural commitment whose practical consequences extend well beyond the laboratory. The dissolution happened gradually, but its implications did not stay in academic journals. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and reflects a convergent recognition that bears directly on how eyewitness testimony should be evaluated, how educational materials should be designed, how stereotypes should be understood, and how ordinary remembering should be trusted: that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place, and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible. The schema is not just a theoretical commitment. It is a fact about minds with practical weight.

### Variation 9 — Lightly Inclusive

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Reader positioning. Uses "we" naturally — the reader is a fellow inquirer rather than spectator. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA, *except* the strict no-first-person rule. **Use when:** Sections that benefit from collaborative framing; reflective passages; conclusion chapters where the reader's own thinking is being addressed.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Socratic Exploration]] (compatible with its dialogic frame), reflective sections of [[Dialectical Report]] and [[First Principles Analysis]], synthesis chapters where the reader is being invited to take on the conclusions.
>
> **Caveat:** This variation deliberately violates the canonical no-first-person rule. Use it intentionally; do not let it bleed into other sections.

> Across nearly a century of cognitive research, the concept of the schema has given us a kind of theoretical solvent — one that has dissolved the apparent boundaries between phenomena that earlier psychology had treated as distinct, and that has revealed, beneath the surface differences, a shared architectural commitment we are still learning to articulate. The dissolution happened gradually. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that resist easy translation. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development, and it reflects a convergent recognition that ought to change how we think about thinking: that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place, and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible. The schema, in this sense, is less a discovery we have made than a commitment we have been led, by the evidence, to take on.

### Variation 10 — Lightly Wry

> [!style-card]
> **Varies:** Affect dial. Knowing, occasionally ironic. Not sarcastic — but the prose acknowledges the strangeness or irony of what it reports. **Preserves:** All baseline DNA. **Use when:** Sections that benefit from a light touch; chapters discussing intellectual stubbornness, disciplinary blind spots, or the strangeness of foundational findings; passages where complete earnestness would feel naive.
>
> **Best paired with:** [[Annotated Critical Analysis]] critical sections (where the wry texture pairs naturally with the epistemic honesty), [[Dialectical Report]] sections discussing entrenched positions, opening chapters of any report on a topic with a contested intellectual history.
>
> **Caveat:** Use sparingly. Sustained wryness across an entire 10K-word report is fatiguing. Best as a flavor for one or two sections.

> Across nearly a century of cognitive research, the concept of the schema has functioned as a kind of theoretical solvent — quietly dissolving boundaries that earlier psychology had spent considerable effort drawing in the first place. The dissolution happened gradually, and not always with the field's full awareness that it was happening. Bartlett's reconstructive memory, Piaget's developmental assimilation, Schank and Abelson's scripts, Minsky's frames, and Rosch's prototypes do not, on the surface, name the same thing — they emerged from different intellectual traditions and committed themselves to different formal vocabularies that, with characteristic disciplinary thoroughness, declined to translate into one another. None of these vocabularies is reducible to the others, and their proponents have generally preferred it that way. And yet the family resemblance among them is striking, sustained across decades of independent theoretical development that often pretended not to notice it, and reflects a convergent recognition that cognition is structured by knowledge already in place and that this structure is what makes both perception's economy and perception's distortion possible. The schema, in this sense, is less a discovery than a commitment — one that the field has been making for nearly a century while occasionally insisting it was making something else.

## How to Deploy a Variation

> [!methodology-and-sources] Two deployment patterns
>
> **Pattern A — Whole-report variation:** Replace the canonical exemplar in your style directive with the variation's exemplar. The entire report adopts the variant voice.
>
> **Pattern B — Section-specific variation:** Use the canonical directive for the report as a whole, but inject a *section-specific override* for one or two sections. Example pattern below.

### Section-specific override pattern

When you want most of the report in the canonical voice but one section in a variation:

```
<section-style-override section="Historical Background">
For this section ONLY, replace the canonical exemplar with the following 
variation. After this section, return to the canonical voice for the remainder 
of the report.

VARIATION USED: Quietly Elegiac (Variation 7 from House Style Variations Library)

VARIATION EXEMPLAR:
"""
[Paste exemplar from Variation 7 here]
"""

This override applies to section "Historical Background" only. All other sections 
remain in the canonical voice as specified in the original style directive.
</section-style-override>
```

> [!warning] Don't stack variations within a single section
> Each section should use exactly one voice — either the canonical or one variation. Mixing within a section produces tonal incoherence that readers notice even if they cannot name what is wrong.

## Combining Variations as Hybrids

> [!example] Useful hybrid pairings within the library
> Two variations can be combined when the dimensions they vary do not conflict. The most useful combinations:
>
> | Hybrid | Components | Use Case |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Question + Contrast** | V4 + V5 | Sections that motivate a puzzle by setting up an opposition |
> | **History + Quietly Elegiac** | V3 + V7 | Long retrospective surveys of a tradition |
> | **Phenomenon + Inline-Parenthetical** | V2 + V6 | Empirical findings sections with full citation discipline |
> | **Claim + Subtly Urgent** | V1 + V8 | Synthesis sections where the takeaway carries practical weight |
> | **Question + Lightly Wry** | V4 + V10 | Opening puzzles where the field's own confusion is part of the puzzle |
>
> To combine, specify both opening tendencies or both affect dials in the directive's voice profile. Do not paste two exemplars — pick the single exemplar that best demonstrates the hybrid you want and describe the second dimension in the profile.

## Further Exploration

> [!further-exploration]
> Topics worth developing into their own PKB documents:

> [!topic-idea] Variation Decay Profiles
> Each variation has a distinct decay profile — the dimension it varies is also the dimension most prone to drift. Empirically map which variations decay fastest under sustained generation. Use the resulting data to specify per-variation counter-measures.

> [!topic-idea] Section-Detection Auto-Routing
> Build a small classifier that reads the topic and section headers of a planned report and recommends the appropriate variation per section. Reduces the operator burden of choosing variations manually for long, multi-section reports.

> [!topic-idea] Hybrid Combinatorics
> The current document identifies five useful hybrids. The full combinatorial space is 45 pairs (not all viable). Systematically test which pairs produce stable hybrids and which collapse into incoherence. Build a hybrid-stability matrix.

> [!topic-idea] Domain-Specific Variation Sets
> Develop parallel variation libraries for non-cognitive-science topics — e.g., the Patient Synthesizer applied to philosophical, historical, technical, or literary topics may need different default exemplars even if the dimensional structure remains the same.

> [!topic-idea] Reverse-Engineering New Variations
> Identify writers (living or dead) whose voices align with the Patient Synthesizer baseline but tune dimensions not yet covered here. Reverse-engineer their style choices into new variations. Candidates worth analyzing: late Bernard Williams, Charles Taylor, Iris Murdoch's philosophical prose.
