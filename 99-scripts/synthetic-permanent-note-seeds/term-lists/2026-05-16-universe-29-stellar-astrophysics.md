# Batch: Universe 29 — Stellar Astrophysics

## Stellar Evolution

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, hertzsprung-russell-diagram]
- aliases: [stellar life cycle]
- broader: [astrophysical evolution]
- related: [hertzsprung-russell-diagram, main-sequence, red-giant, white-dwarf, supernova]
- prerequisites: [stellar-structure]

**definition**: Stellar Evolution is the systematic change in a star's structure, composition, luminosity, and surface temperature over its lifetime, driven primarily by nuclear-fuel exhaustion in successive burning stages and by gravitational contraction between them; the trajectory through the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram depends almost entirely on the star's initial mass and slightly on metallicity.

**key_claim**: Stellar Evolution theory is one of the empirically best-tested branches of astrophysics: predictions for main-sequence lifetimes, post-main-sequence positions in the H-R diagram, and final remnant masses match observations of stellar populations from globular clusters to galactic open clusters with quantitative precision.

**warning**: Stellar Evolution models depend on parametrised treatments of convection, mass loss, rotation, and binary-interaction effects whose physics is not derived from first principles; treating model predictions for advanced burning stages and pre-supernova structure as precise overstates the calibration uncertainty in these phenomenological inputs.

## Stellar Wind

- secondary_domains: [mass-loss, stellar-atmospheres]
- aliases: [stellar mass loss]
- broader: [stellar mass-loss processes]
- related: [solar-wind, mass-loss, luminous-blue-variable, wolf-rayet-star, radiation-pressure]
- prerequisites: [stellar-atmospheres]

**definition**: A Stellar Wind is the continuous outflow of mass from a star's photosphere driven by combinations of radiation pressure on metal-line absorbers, magnetic effects, and pulsational instability; mass-loss rates range from ~10⁻¹⁴ M☉/yr for solar-type stars to ~10⁻⁴ M☉/yr for luminous blue variables, decisively shaping post-main-sequence evolution.

**key_claim**: Stellar Wind mass loss in massive stars (M ≳ 25 M☉) can remove a substantial fraction of the original stellar mass during the main sequence, fundamentally altering the type of supernova produced (stripped-envelope Type Ib/c rather than Type II) and the mass of the resulting compact remnant.

**warning**: Stellar Wind mass-loss rates have been progressively revised downward by a factor of 2–3 since the 2000s as wind-clumping effects have been better characterised; treating older mass-loss prescriptions as authoritative produces stellar-evolution and remnant-mass predictions that are systematically biased.

## Supernova Remnant

- secondary_domains: [stellar-explosions, interstellar-medium]
- aliases: [SNR]
- broader: [stellar-explosion remnants]
- related: [supernova, crab-nebula, cosmic-ray, chandra-x-ray-observatory, neutron-star]
- prerequisites: [supernova, interstellar-medium]

**definition**: A Supernova Remnant is the expanding shell of ejecta and shock-heated interstellar medium produced by a supernova explosion, typically visible as an extended X-ray and radio source for ~10⁵ years before dissipating; SNRs are the principal sources of cosmic rays, the dominant injectors of heavy elements into the ISM, and laboratories for studying collisionless shock physics.

**key_claim**: Supernova Remnants accelerate cosmic rays to energies up to ~10¹⁵ eV via diffusive shock acceleration at their forward shocks; this mechanism has been established through joint X-ray, gamma-ray (Fermi, HESS), and radio observations of remnants such as RX J1713.7−3946 and Cassiopeia A.

**warning**: A Supernova Remnant interpretation requires distinguishing among ejecta-dominated, Sedov-Taylor, and snowplough phases whose physics differ qualitatively; treating all SNRs with a single set of evolutionary equations produces age and energy estimates that are systematically wrong in non-canonical phase regimes.

## Crab Nebula

- secondary_domains: [supernova-remnant, pulsar]
- aliases: [Messier 1, NGC 1952]
- broader: [supernova remnants]
- related: [supernova-remnant, pulsar, neutron-star, sn-1054, pulsar-wind-nebula]
- prerequisites: [supernova, pulsar]

**definition**: The Crab Nebula is the supernova remnant of SN 1054 (recorded as a "guest star" by Chinese astronomers), located ~6,500 light-years away in Taurus; it consists of an expanding shell of supernova ejecta surrounding a central pulsar (the Crab Pulsar, PSR B0531+21) whose spin-down powers the nebula's synchrotron emission across the entire electromagnetic spectrum.

**key_claim**: The Crab Nebula is the most extensively studied supernova remnant in astronomy and historically served as the standard candle for X-ray and gamma-ray flux calibration; it provided the empirical link between supernovae, neutron-star formation, and pulsar-wind-nebula physics, anchoring decades of high-energy astrophysics.

**warning**: The Crab Nebula's flaring behaviour at GeV gamma-ray energies (Fermi-detected flares, 2010–2011) demonstrated that even the canonical "stable" calibration source is not actually stable on year timescales; using historical Crab Nebula data as a fixed reference without acknowledging temporal variability has been a source of cross-mission flux calibration discrepancies.

## Eta Carinae

- secondary_domains: [luminous-blue-variable, massive-stars]
- aliases: [η Car, η Carinae]
- broader: [luminous blue variables]
- related: [luminous-blue-variable, mass-loss, supernova-impostor, homunculus-nebula, great-eruption]
- prerequisites: [luminous-blue-variable]

**definition**: Eta Carinae is a binary system in the Carina Nebula consisting of a luminous blue variable primary (~100 M☉) and a hot O-type companion (~30 M☉); the primary underwent the Great Eruption in the 1840s, briefly becoming the second-brightest star in the sky and ejecting the bipolar Homunculus Nebula visible today, and is widely expected to undergo a true supernova explosion within geologically short timescales.

**key_claim**: Eta Carinae is the closest and best-studied example of a massive-star eruption that ejected several solar masses of material without destroying the star — a "supernova impostor" event — providing the principal local laboratory for understanding the eruptive mass-loss episodes that shape the most massive stars' evolution.

**warning**: Eta Carinae's expected supernova fate and timeline are not predictable to better than centuries; treating the system as imminent-supernova candidate in any specific timeframe overstates what theory and modelling actually constrain, since the underlying instability mechanism is not fully understood.

## Luminous Blue Variable

- secondary_domains: [massive-stars, mass-loss]
- aliases: [LBV, S Doradus variable]
- broader: [massive-star evolutionary phases]
- related: [eta-carinae, mass-loss, wolf-rayet-star, hertzsprung-russell-diagram, supernova-impostor]
- prerequisites: [massive-stars, stellar-evolution]

**definition**: A Luminous Blue Variable is a class of massive (M ≳ 25 M☉), highly luminous, intrinsically unstable stars exhibiting episodic giant eruptions and S Doradus variations on timescales of decades; LBVs occupy a forbidden region of the upper Hertzsprung-Russell diagram (the Humphreys-Davidson limit) and are believed to represent a brief, mass-loss-dominated transition from main-sequence O stars to Wolf-Rayet stars.

**key_claim**: A Luminous Blue Variable phase is now thought to be the principal site of episodic giant mass-loss in massive-star evolution, removing several solar masses in single eruptions and substantially altering the supernova type and remnant produced; this conflicts with the older paradigm in which mass loss was viewed as predominantly continuous and steady.

**warning**: A Luminous Blue Variable status is sometimes assigned based on a single observed eruption, but the eruption physics may be common to multiple massive-star evolutionary phases; treating LBV as a sharp phase rather than a mass-loss-dominated transition zone in the H-R diagram overstates how cleanly the class is defined.

## Yellow Hypergiant

- secondary_domains: [massive-stars, stellar-evolution]
- aliases: [yellow hypergiant star, YHG]
- broader: [hypergiant evolutionary phases]
- related: [luminous-blue-variable, red-supergiant, stellar-wind, mass-loss, post-red-supergiant]
- prerequisites: [massive-stars, stellar-evolution]

**definition**: A Yellow Hypergiant is an extremely massive (typically 20–40 M☉) and luminous (10⁵–10⁶ L☉) post-red-supergiant star with effective temperature ~4,000–8,000 K, occupying a temperature range called the "yellow void" through which the star evolves rapidly because of strong instability; well-known examples include Rho Cassiopeiae and IRC +10420.

**key_claim**: A Yellow Hypergiant phase is so unstable that the star transits the yellow void in ~10⁴ years (an instant on stellar-evolution timescales), with each pass marked by major mass-loss episodes; this brief, dynamic phase provides the principal observational evidence that very massive stars do not evolve smoothly through intermediate temperatures.

**warning**: A Yellow Hypergiant identification is rare (perhaps 10–20 known in the Galaxy) and based on small samples; treating yellow-hypergiant phenomenology as fully characterised overstates what limited statistics actually permit, and individual star variability is large.

## Red Supergiant

- secondary_domains: [massive-stars, stellar-evolution]
- aliases: [RSG]
- broader: [supergiant evolutionary phases]
- related: [stellar-evolution, supernova, type-ii-supernova, betelgeuse, mass-loss]
- prerequisites: [massive-stars, stellar-evolution]

**definition**: A Red Supergiant is the late evolutionary phase of stars with initial masses ~8–25 M☉, characterised by extremely large radii (500–1500 R☉), low effective temperatures (3,500–4,500 K), and the highest luminosities at the cool end of the upper Hertzsprung-Russell diagram; nearly all Type II-P supernovae arise from red supergiant progenitors.

**key_claim**: A Red Supergiant population provides the direct progenitors of the most common type of core-collapse supernova (Type II-P), and the observed mass distribution of detected RSG progenitors of nearby supernovae appears to exclude the most massive RSGs (M ≳ 17 M☉), creating the so-called "red supergiant problem" of missing high-mass SNe II-P progenitors.

**warning**: A Red Supergiant mass estimates from photometry depend on bolometric corrections, distance, and circumstellar dust extinction whose systematic uncertainties are substantial; treating the RSG mass problem as established new physics overstates a result that may partly reflect systematic biases in mass inference.

## Blue Straggler

- secondary_domains: [stellar-clusters, binary-stellar-evolution]
- aliases: [blue straggler star, BSS]
- broader: [anomalous-stellar-population members]
- related: [stellar-cluster, binary-stellar-evolution, mass-transfer-binary, stellar-collision, common-envelope-evolution]
- prerequisites: [hertzsprung-russell-diagram, binary-stellar-evolution]

**definition**: A Blue Straggler is a main-sequence star found in a stellar cluster at a position bluer and brighter than the cluster's main-sequence turnoff, where stars of that mass should already have evolved off the main sequence; blue stragglers are typically attributed to mass acquisition either through binary mass transfer or through direct stellar collisions in dense cluster environments.

**key_claim**: A Blue Straggler population in a stellar cluster provides direct observational evidence that binary interactions and stellar collisions are common in dense stellar systems; the relative abundance and spatial distribution of blue stragglers serve as a sensitive probe of cluster dynamical history and binary fractions.

**warning**: A Blue Straggler formation channel — mass transfer vs. collision vs. mergers — cannot generally be distinguished from spectroscopy and photometry alone; treating any individual blue straggler as having a known origin overstates inferences that statistical-population analyses can support but individual-object analyses cannot.

## Subdwarf Star

- secondary_domains: [stellar-evolution, post-asymptotic-giant-branch]
- aliases: [hot subdwarf, sdB, sdO]
- broader: [post-AGB evolutionary phases]
- related: [horizontal-branch, white-dwarf, helium-flash, common-envelope-evolution, mass-transfer-binary]
- prerequisites: [stellar-evolution]

**definition**: A Subdwarf Star (specifically the hot subdwarfs sdB and sdO) is a low-mass core-helium-burning star with effective temperature 20,000–50,000 K and an extraordinarily thin hydrogen envelope (<0.01 M☉); subdwarfs sit on the extreme horizontal branch and likely form through binary mass-loss processes that strip the red-giant envelope before the helium flash.

**key_claim**: A Subdwarf Star population's high binary fraction (~50% in close binaries) provides direct evidence that binary mass loss is the dominant formation channel; the existence of subdwarfs constrains common-envelope efficiency parameters used throughout binary-stellar-evolution models.

**warning**: A Subdwarf Star evolutionary history sometimes includes tidal mass transfer, common-envelope ejection, or stable Roche-lobe overflow; treating subdwarfs as a homogeneous population produced by a single channel overstates the heterogeneity that the data actually support.

## Helium Flash

- secondary_domains: [stellar-evolution, nuclear-astrophysics]
- aliases: [core helium flash]
- broader: [thermonuclear runaway events in stars]
- related: [helium-burning, red-giant, horizontal-branch, degenerate-matter, triple-alpha-process]
- prerequisites: [helium-burning, degenerate-matter]

**definition**: The Helium Flash is the explosive ignition of helium burning in the degenerate helium core of a low-mass red-giant star (M ≲ 2 M☉) when the core temperature reaches ~10⁸ K; degeneracy pressure does not respond to temperature increases, so the energy released by triple-alpha burning produces a thermonuclear runaway that lasts seconds, raises core temperature to ~3×10⁸ K, and lifts the degeneracy.

**key_claim**: The Helium Flash converts a degenerate helium core into a non-degenerate one and reorganises the entire stellar structure, settling the star onto the horizontal branch where it spends ~10⁸ years burning helium quiescently; the flash itself is hydrodynamically subtle but does not visibly disturb the star's surface luminosity.

**warning**: The Helium Flash is currently understood through one-dimensional spherically symmetric stellar models that smooth out potentially important multi-dimensional convective and rotational effects; treating canonical 1D flash predictions as fully reliable overstates the agreement with first-principles 3D simulations that are still computationally limited.

## Helium Burning

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, stellar-evolution]
- aliases: [triple-alpha process, core helium burning]
- broader: [nuclear burning stages]
- related: [helium-flash, triple-alpha-process, horizontal-branch, carbon-burning-process, stellar-nucleosynthesis]
- prerequisites: [stellar-nucleosynthesis]

**definition**: Helium Burning is the nuclear-fusion stage in which three ⁴He nuclei combine via the triple-alpha process to form ¹²C (and subsequently ¹⁶O via α capture), occurring at core temperatures ≳10⁸ K; helium burning powers horizontal-branch stars (low-mass) and core-helium-burning blue supergiants (intermediate- and high-mass), and is the principal source of carbon and oxygen in the universe.

**key_claim**: Helium Burning's triple-alpha rate depends extraordinarily sensitively on the energy of the Hoyle resonance in ¹²C — a 7.6 MeV excited state predicted by Hoyle in 1953 specifically to explain the observed cosmic abundance of carbon, and one of the most often-cited examples of anthropic reasoning in physics.

**warning**: Helium Burning rates depend on uncertain reaction cross-sections (notably ¹²C(α,γ)¹⁶O) that are difficult to measure at stellar energies; treating stellar carbon-to-oxygen ratios as precisely predicted overstates how well the underlying nuclear-physics inputs are actually constrained.

## Carbon Burning Process

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, stellar-evolution]
- aliases: [carbon burning, C burning]
- broader: [advanced nuclear burning stages]
- related: [helium-burning, neon-burning-process, oxygen-burning-process, type-ia-supernova, massive-stars]
- prerequisites: [helium-burning]

**definition**: The Carbon Burning Process is the nuclear-fusion stage in which two ¹²C nuclei combine to produce primarily ²⁰Ne + α or ²³Na + p, occurring at core temperatures ~5×10⁸ to 10⁹ K in stars with initial mass M ≳ 8 M☉; it is the first burning stage that requires non-degenerate conditions in massive stars and is bypassed by lower-mass stars whose carbon-oxygen cores never reach ignition temperature.

**key_claim**: The Carbon Burning Process is the empirical threshold separating the evolutionary fates of intermediate-mass stars (which end as carbon-oxygen white dwarfs) from massive stars (which proceed through neon, oxygen, and silicon burning to core collapse); the precise mass boundary near 7–10 M☉ depends sensitively on the carbon-burning ignition condition.

**warning**: The Carbon Burning Process is also relevant for type Ia supernovae, where carbon ignites explosively in degenerate white-dwarf material; the difference between quiescent massive-star burning and explosive thermonuclear burning is large, and conflating the two contexts misrepresents the underlying physics.

## Neon Burning Process

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, advanced-stellar-burning]
- aliases: [neon burning, Ne burning]
- broader: [advanced nuclear burning stages]
- related: [carbon-burning-process, oxygen-burning-process, silicon-burning-process, photodisintegration, massive-stars]
- prerequisites: [carbon-burning-process]

**definition**: The Neon Burning Process is the nuclear-burning stage in massive-star cores at temperatures ~1.2–2×10⁹ K in which photodisintegration of ²⁰Ne by high-energy thermal photons (²⁰Ne + γ → ¹⁶O + α) liberates alpha particles that capture on remaining ²⁰Ne to produce ²⁴Mg; it lasts only years to decades and represents the first burning stage dominated by photodisintegration rather than direct nuclear fusion.

**key_claim**: The Neon Burning Process marks the transition in massive-star evolution where photodisintegration becomes thermodynamically important, foreshadowing the more violent photodisintegration-driven processes of the silicon-burning and core-collapse stages; understanding neon burning is essential for accurate pre-supernova stellar models.

**warning**: The Neon Burning Process duration of years to decades is short enough that mixing, rotation, and non-spherical effects can substantially alter the results obtained from spherically symmetric models; treating 1D neon-burning predictions as quantitatively precise overstates the modelling fidelity for such a short-lived dynamical phase.

## Oxygen Burning Process

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, advanced-stellar-burning]
- aliases: [oxygen burning, O burning]
- broader: [advanced nuclear burning stages]
- related: [neon-burning-process, silicon-burning-process, carbon-burning-process, sulfur, alpha-process]
- prerequisites: [neon-burning-process]

**definition**: The Oxygen Burning Process is the nuclear-burning stage in massive-star cores at temperatures ~1.5–3×10⁹ K in which two ¹⁶O nuclei fuse to produce primarily ²⁸Si + α or ³¹P + p, with secondary contributions producing ³²S; it lasts only ~6 months in a 25 M☉ star and produces the silicon-rich core that will undergo silicon burning.

**key_claim**: The Oxygen Burning Process establishes the silicon-rich core that will become the iron-group ash of silicon burning and ultimately collapse to form a neutron star or black hole; the products of oxygen burning trace directly onto observed supernova nucleosynthesis yields and the abundances of silicon, sulphur, and argon in the universe.

**warning**: The Oxygen Burning Process is so brief that hydrostatic-equilibrium assumptions begin to break down, and convective-burning shells couple non-trivially to ensuing burning stages; treating oxygen burning in isolation from the neon and silicon stages overlooks the highly coupled nature of advanced massive-star nucleosynthesis.

## Silicon Burning Process

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, advanced-stellar-burning]
- aliases: [silicon burning, Si burning, photodisintegration burning]
- broader: [advanced nuclear burning stages]
- related: [oxygen-burning-process, iron-peak-elements, alpha-process, core-collapse, massive-stars]
- prerequisites: [oxygen-burning-process]

**definition**: The Silicon Burning Process is the final hydrostatic burning stage in massive-star cores at temperatures ~3–4×10⁹ K, in which photodisintegration of ²⁸Si liberates alpha particles, protons, and neutrons that successively capture onto progressively heavier nuclei in a quasi-equilibrium chain producing iron-peak nuclei (⁵⁶Fe, ⁵⁶Ni); the process lasts only ~1 day in a 25 M☉ star.

**key_claim**: The Silicon Burning Process produces an iron-peak core whose binding energy per nucleon is the maximum on the nuclear chart, meaning further fusion absorbs energy rather than releasing it; once the core exceeds the Chandrasekhar mass, electron-degeneracy pressure cannot support it and core collapse ensues, triggering a Type II supernova.

**warning**: The Silicon Burning Process duration (one day) is short compared with the dynamical timescale of large-scale convection; treating silicon-burning structure as quasi-static overlooks the rapidly changing balance among nuclear, neutrino, and convective timescales that 3D simulations are only beginning to characterise.

## S-Process Nucleosynthesis

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, stellar-nucleosynthesis]
- aliases: [s-process, slow neutron capture]
- broader: [neutron-capture nucleosynthesis]
- related: [r-process-nucleosynthesis, asymptotic-giant-branch, neutron-capture, heavy-elements, technetium]
- prerequisites: [stellar-nucleosynthesis]

**definition**: S-Process Nucleosynthesis is the production of approximately half of the elements heavier than iron through slow neutron capture on seed nuclei followed by beta decay, where "slow" means the neutron-capture timescale is long compared with relevant beta-decay lifetimes; the s-process operates in low- and intermediate-mass asymptotic-giant-branch stars and in massive-star helium-burning shells.

**key_claim**: S-Process Nucleosynthesis was the first stellar nucleosynthesis process to be empirically established (Merrill's 1952 detection of technetium in red giants demonstrated that heavy elements were being made in stars currently observable), providing the foundational evidence that heavy-element synthesis is an ongoing astrophysical process rather than a primordial fact.

**warning**: S-Process Nucleosynthesis branching ratios depend on (n,γ) cross-sections at stellar energies that are sometimes poorly measured; treating predicted s-process abundance patterns as quantitatively precise overstates the underlying nuclear-data uncertainties.

## R-Process Nucleosynthesis

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, stellar-nucleosynthesis]
- aliases: [r-process, rapid neutron capture]
- broader: [neutron-capture nucleosynthesis]
- related: [s-process-nucleosynthesis, kilonova, neutron-star-merger, heavy-elements, gw170817]
- prerequisites: [stellar-nucleosynthesis]

**definition**: R-Process Nucleosynthesis is the production of approximately half of the elements heavier than iron (and essentially all of the actinides) through rapid neutron capture in extremely neutron-rich environments, where the neutron-capture timescale is short compared with relevant beta-decay lifetimes; the r-process produces nuclei far from stability that subsequently beta-decay to the observed heavy isotopes.

**key_claim**: R-Process Nucleosynthesis was confirmed empirically to occur in neutron-star mergers by the multi-messenger observation of GW170817 and its electromagnetic counterpart AT2017gfo, whose kilonova spectrum exhibited the predicted infrared signatures of lanthanide- and actinide-rich r-process ejecta — an extraordinary direct confirmation of decades-old theoretical predictions.

**warning**: R-Process Nucleosynthesis has additional possible sites (collapsar disks, magneto-rotational core-collapse supernovae) whose relative contributions are unsettled; treating neutron-star mergers as the sole r-process site oversimplifies what is now understood as a multi-source enrichment history with site-dependent abundance patterns.

## Iron Peak Elements

- secondary_domains: [nuclear-astrophysics, stellar-nucleosynthesis]
- aliases: [iron peak nuclei, Fe-peak elements]
- broader: [nucleosynthetic abundance features]
- related: [silicon-burning-process, type-ia-supernova, alpha-process, nuclear-binding-energy, type-ii-supernova]
- prerequisites: [silicon-burning-process]

**definition**: The Iron Peak Elements are the nuclei in the mass range A ~ 50–60 (chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel) that exhibit a characteristic abundance peak in the cosmic abundance distribution, arising because their binding energy per nucleon is the maximum on the nuclear chart; produced primarily through silicon burning in massive-star cores and by explosive burning in Type Ia supernovae.

**key_claim**: The Iron Peak Elements' abundance pattern is one of the most basic and stringent tests of stellar nucleosynthesis: the relative abundances of iron-peak isotopes constrain the equation of state, electron fraction, and explosion mechanism of both core-collapse and thermonuclear supernovae.

**warning**: The Iron Peak Elements' relative abundances vary substantially between Type Ia and Type II supernovae, and disentangling their respective contributions across cosmic history depends on uncertain progenitor delay-time distributions; treating chemical-evolution models as having converged on iron-peak yields overstates the consensus.

## Pair Instability Supernova

- secondary_domains: [supernovae, very-massive-stars]
- aliases: [PISN, pair-instability supernova]
- broader: [supernova types]
- related: [supernova, very-massive-stars, electron-positron-pair-production, population-iii-stars, super-luminous-supernova]
- prerequisites: [very-massive-stars, supernova]

**definition**: A Pair Instability Supernova is a thermonuclear explosion that completely disrupts a very massive (~140–260 M☉) helium core when oxygen-burning conditions trigger electron-positron pair production, removing thermal pressure and causing dynamical collapse that ignites explosive oxygen burning; PISNe leave no compact remnant and produce up to ~50 M☉ of ⁵⁶Ni.

**key_claim**: A Pair Instability Supernova produces the most luminous explosions in the universe (peak luminosities ~10⁴⁴ erg/s) and would have been important in the early universe when Population III stars could reach the requisite masses without losing mass to metal-poor winds; SN 2007bi and SN 2018ibb are leading candidates for observed PISN events.

**warning**: A Pair Instability Supernova interpretation of any specific super-luminous supernova is contested, since alternative power sources (magnetar spin-down, circumstellar interaction) can mimic PISN light curves; treating individual super-luminous events as confirmed PISNe overstates how cleanly the alternatives can be excluded.

## Electron Capture Supernova

- secondary_domains: [supernovae, intermediate-mass-stars]
- aliases: [ECSN, electron capture supernova]
- broader: [supernova types]
- related: [supernova, type-ii-supernova, massive-stars, neutron-star, oxygen-neon-magnesium-core]
- prerequisites: [supernova, stellar-evolution]

**definition**: An Electron Capture Supernova is a core-collapse supernova in stars with initial mass ~8–10 M☉ in which the oxygen-neon-magnesium core (formed because the star was not massive enough to ignite carbon burning under conditions producing iron) collapses when electron-capture reactions on ²⁴Mg and ²⁰Ne remove degeneracy support; ECSNe produce relatively low-mass neutron stars and small explosion energies.

**key_claim**: An Electron Capture Supernova is the leading candidate for SN 1054 (the Crab supernova), whose abundance patterns and remnant neutron-star properties favour an ECSN over a more energetic core-collapse event; ECSNe also offer a natural explanation for the lower-mass tail of the observed neutron-star mass distribution.

**warning**: An Electron Capture Supernova fraction among all core-collapse SNe is poorly determined, with theoretical estimates ranging from a few percent to ~30%; treating any specific value as established overstates the uncertainty in the progenitor mass range and the precise threshold conditions for electron-capture initiation.

## Thermonuclear Runaway

- secondary_domains: [stellar-explosions, degenerate-matter]
- aliases: [thermonuclear instability]
- broader: [explosive nuclear burning]
- related: [helium-flash, type-ia-supernova, nova, classical-nova, x-ray-burst]
- prerequisites: [degenerate-matter, nuclear-burning]

**definition**: A Thermonuclear Runaway is the rapid, exponentially accelerating ignition of nuclear fusion in degenerate matter where the nuclear-energy generation rate increases more rapidly with temperature than the matter can radiate or expand to relieve the heating; degeneracy decouples temperature from pressure, so the temperature climbs without the cooling expansion that would arrest non-degenerate burning.

**key_claim**: A Thermonuclear Runaway is the underlying mechanism for events as diverse as the helium flash (mild and non-disruptive), classical novae (surface burning on a white dwarf), Type Ia supernovae (carbon burning that disrupts the entire white dwarf), and X-ray bursts on neutron stars; the common physics is degeneracy decoupling pressure from temperature.

**warning**: A Thermonuclear Runaway in 1D models often ignites differently from in 3D simulations because deflagration-to-detonation transitions, multi-dimensional turbulence, and burning-front instabilities matter critically; treating 1D Type-Ia supernova predictions as definitive overstates the modelling state of the art.

## Mass Transfer Binary

- secondary_domains: [binary-stellar-evolution, stellar-evolution]
- aliases: [interacting binary, Roche-lobe overflow binary]
- broader: [interacting binary systems]
- related: [common-envelope-evolution, roche-lobe-overflow, x-ray-binary, type-ia-supernova, blue-straggler]
- prerequisites: [binary-stellar-evolution]

**definition**: A Mass Transfer Binary is a stellar binary system in which one component (the donor) overflows its Roche lobe and transfers mass onto the companion (the accretor) via direct stream impact or accretion-disk formation; mass transfer fundamentally alters the evolution of both components, producing systems including X-ray binaries, cataclysmic variables, novae, double neutron stars, and Type Ia supernova progenitors.

**key_claim**: A Mass Transfer Binary's evolution follows from an interplay between orbital angular-momentum loss, mass-ratio changes, and stellar-structure response of the donor, with stable vs. unstable mass transfer determined by the polytropic response of the donor; this framework underlies essentially all of modern compact-binary population synthesis.

**warning**: A Mass Transfer Binary's stability criteria and angular-momentum-loss prescriptions remain partly phenomenological, with substantial uncertainties that propagate into population-synthesis predictions for compact-binary merger rates; treating any specific predicted rate as authoritative overstates the convergence of modelling assumptions.

## Common Envelope Evolution

- secondary_domains: [binary-stellar-evolution, compact-binaries]
- aliases: [CE evolution, common envelope phase, common-envelope phase]
- broader: [interacting binary phases]
- related: [mass-transfer-binary, double-neutron-star, type-ia-supernova, gravitational-wave, compact-binary-merger]
- prerequisites: [mass-transfer-binary]

**definition**: Common Envelope Evolution is the binary-stellar-evolution phase in which an unstable mass-transfer episode envelops both stars in a single shared envelope; orbital energy is transferred to the envelope through dynamical friction, the orbit shrinks rapidly, and the envelope is either ejected (producing a tight binary) or fails to eject (producing a stellar merger).

**key_claim**: Common Envelope Evolution is the principal mechanism for producing the close binaries that ultimately merge as gravitational-wave sources detected by LIGO/Virgo; without CE-driven orbital tightening, the population-synthesis predictions for binary black-hole and binary neutron-star merger rates would be orders of magnitude too low to match observations.

**warning**: Common Envelope Evolution efficiency is parametrised by the αCE energy-efficiency parameter that is poorly constrained from first principles; predicted compact-binary merger rates can vary by an order of magnitude depending on the assumed αCE value, and treating any specific population-synthesis prediction as definitive overstates how well the underlying CE physics is understood.
