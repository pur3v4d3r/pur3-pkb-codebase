# Batch: Learning Theory & Education

## Educational Psychology

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [psychology, education]
- aliases: [ed psych]
- broader: [psychology, learning-science]
- related: [psychology-of-learning, learning-theories, [[instructional-design]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Educational Psychology is the scientific discipline that applies psychological theory and method to questions of learning, instruction, motivation, assessment, and individual differences in educational settings, mediating between basic cognitive and developmental science and the practical design of teaching.

**key_claim**: Educational Psychology provides the empirical foundation that distinguishes evidence-based pedagogy from craft tradition: practices like spaced retrieval, formative assessment, and worked-example study are pedagogical commitments grounded in decades of Educational Psychology research rather than intuitions about teaching.

**warning**: Educational Psychology has a recurrent translation problem — laboratory findings frequently fail to scale to classrooms because the controlled conditions that produce robust effects are rarely available; treating Educational Psychology results as turnkey prescriptions without classroom-scale replication is one of the most common sources of failed reform.

## Educational Development

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [education, developmental-psychology]
- aliases: [educational growth]
- broader: [education, developmental-psychology]
- related: [educational-psychology, [[curriculum-design]], [[teacher-development]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Educational Development is the field concerned with the systematic improvement of educational systems, programmes, and individual capacity over time — encompassing curriculum reform, teacher professional development, institutional research, and the deliberate cultivation of pedagogical practice across an organisation.

**key_claim**: Educational Development reframes "education" from a set of teacher behaviours to an organisational learning problem: durable improvement requires aligned change in curriculum, assessment, professional development, and incentives, which is why isolated teacher-training interventions consistently produce smaller and shorter-lived effects than coordinated system change.

**warning**: Educational Development can drift into managerialism that mistakes activity (workshops attended, frameworks adopted) for outcome (student learning gains); the pedagogical rule of thumb is to require evidence of learner-level change before treating an Educational Development initiative as successful.

## Educational Technology

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [hci, instructional-design]
- aliases: [ed tech, EdTech]
- broader: [education]
- related: [educational-psychology, cognitive-theory-of-multimedia-learning, [[learning-analytics]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Educational Technology is the systematic design, deployment, and evaluation of digital and analogue tools intended to support learning — encompassing learning-management systems, multimedia, intelligent tutoring, simulation, adaptive assessment, and PKB tools — and the methodological tradition of testing whether such tools produce learning gains rather than mere engagement.

**key_claim**: Educational Technology research repeatedly finds that the *medium* matters less than the *instructional design* it carries: the same content delivered through different technologies produces similar learning when design quality is matched, which is why technology adoption uncoupled from pedagogical redesign rarely improves outcomes.

**warning**: Educational Technology is dominated commercially by tools optimised for engagement metrics (time-on-platform, click-through) rather than learning outcomes; uncritical adoption of widely marketed Educational Technology products has repeatedly displaced cheaper, better-evidenced practices, and procurement decisions should require learning-outcome evidence rather than usage statistics.

## Psychology of Learning

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, psychology]
- aliases: [learning psychology]
- broader: [educational-psychology, learning-science]
- related: [principles-of-learning, learning-theories, learning-processes, [[behaviourism]]]
- prerequisites: [educational-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Psychology of Learning is the subfield investigating how organisms acquire, retain, and modify knowledge and skill — historically organised around behaviourist conditioning paradigms and now dominated by cognitive accounts of memory, attention, and skill acquisition — and provides the bridge between basic cognitive science and instructional design.

**key_claim**: The Psychology of Learning establishes that learning is not a single phenomenon but a federation of processes (conditioning, perceptual learning, declarative encoding, skill acquisition, social learning) each governed by partially distinct rules; pedagogy that conflates them under "learning" misapplies findings from one process to a context governed by another.

**warning**: The Psychology of Learning's most well-replicated findings (spacing, retrieval practice, interleaving) are widely cited but unevenly applied; the gap between knowing the Psychology of Learning principles and embedding them in routine practice is large enough that practitioner training in *implementation*, not just principles, is the binding constraint on uptake.

## Principles of Learning

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [learning principles]
- broader: [psychology-of-learning]
- related: [learning-strategies, [[desirable-difficulties]], [[testing-effect]], [[spacing-effect]]]
- prerequisites: [psychology-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Principles of Learning are the well-replicated empirical generalisations governing acquisition and retention — including spacing, retrieval practice, interleaving, generation, dual coding, elaboration, worked examples, feedback timing, and desirable difficulties — that constitute the operational core of evidence-based pedagogy.

**key_claim**: Principles of Learning are characterised by a recurring counter-intuition: practices that *feel* effective in the moment (re-reading, blocked practice, immediate feedback) often produce weaker long-term outcomes than practices that feel harder (testing, interleaving, delayed feedback), and the pedagogical implication is that subjective fluency is an unreliable guide to whether learning is occurring.

**warning**: Principles of Learning are often presented as a checklist that practitioners can apply uniformly, but their effects interact: adding interleaving without sufficient base learning produces confusion rather than discrimination; introducing desirable difficulties to overloaded novices produces failure rather than retrieval strength; principles are levers to be calibrated, not switches to be flipped.

## Learning Theories

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [psychology, education]
- aliases: [theories of learning]
- broader: [learning-science]
- narrower: [cognitive-theories-of-learning, [[constructivism]], [[behaviourism]], [[social-learning-theory]]]
- related: [psychology-of-learning, learning-science, [[connectivism]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Learning Theories are the families of theoretical frameworks — behaviourist, cognitivist, constructivist, social-cognitive, situated, connectivist — that propose distinct mechanisms by which acquisition occurs, distinct explanatory primitives (associations, representations, schemas, communities, networks), and distinct prescriptions for instructional design.

**key_claim**: Learning Theories are best treated as overlapping rather than competing: each captures a real subset of learning phenomena, and skilled pedagogical reasoning involves recognising which Learning Theory applies to which acquisition target, rather than treating one as universally correct.

**warning**: Learning Theories are commonly misused as ideological banners — "I'm a constructivist" — that license pedagogical commitments far beyond what the underlying theory supports; treating Learning Theories as identity markers obscures that competent teaching routinely deploys behaviourist (drill, contingent reinforcement), cognitivist (worked examples, retrieval practice), and constructivist (discovery, project work) techniques in disciplined combination.

## Learning Science

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, education]
- aliases: [the learning sciences, sciences of learning]
- broader: [educational-psychology]
- related: [learning-theories, educational-technology, [[design-based-research]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Learning Science is the interdisciplinary field — drawing on cognitive psychology, neuroscience, computer science, anthropology, and education — that studies how people learn in real-world settings and uses that understanding to design more effective learning environments, tools, and curricula.

**key_claim**: Learning Science distinguishes itself from classical educational psychology through its commitment to *design-based research* — iterative cycles of design, deployment in authentic contexts, measurement, and revision — that treats classrooms as both subjects and partners of inquiry rather than as passive sites of intervention testing.

**warning**: Learning Science's design-based methodology is methodologically less tractable than randomised trials, and findings from Learning Science studies often resist clean causal inference; consumers should recognise this trade-off — design-based work buys ecological validity at the cost of some inferential strength — and weight evidence accordingly rather than rejecting either methodology categorically.

## Learning Strategies

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, study-skills]
- aliases: [study strategies]
- broader: [psychology-of-learning]
- related: [principles-of-learning, learning-processes, [[retrieval-practice]], [[self-regulated-learning]]]
- prerequisites: [psychology-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Learning Strategies are the deliberate cognitive and metacognitive techniques learners deploy to acquire, organise, and consolidate knowledge — including retrieval practice, spaced review, interleaving, elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and concrete-example generation — and constitute the actionable surface of [[principles-of-learning]] for individual study.

**key_claim**: Learning Strategies vary substantially in evidence base, and the Dunlosky et al. (2013) review remains the canonical map: practice testing and distributed practice are top-tier; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice are well-supported; and the popular favourites (highlighting, re-reading, summarisation) sit at the bottom of the evidence ladder.

**warning**: Learning Strategies are often packaged as universal "study tips," but their effectiveness depends on the *content* (interleaving works for discriminating similar categories; less for unrelated topics), the *learner* (worked examples help novices, hinder experts), and the *time horizon* (massed practice can be acceptable for short-term performance, lethal for long-term retention).

## Learning Processes

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science]
- aliases: [processes of learning]
- broader: [psychology-of-learning]
- related: [learning-strategies, memory-consolidation, [[encoding]], [[retrieval]], [[transfer-of-learning]]]
- prerequisites: [psychology-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Learning Processes are the underlying cognitive operations through which acquisition occurs — encoding, consolidation, retrieval, transfer, integration, restructuring — and are the level at which [[learning-strategies]] (deliberate techniques) connect to the [[principles-of-learning]] (empirical generalisations) they exploit.

**key_claim**: Learning Processes is the most useful organising frame for pedagogical decision-making: rather than asking "what strategy should I use," ask "which Learning Process is currently bottlenecking acquisition" (encoding fails → reduce load; retrieval weak → add testing; integration absent → require comparison) and select the strategy that targets that process.

**warning**: Learning Processes are often invoked descriptively without measurement; teachers and learners report which processes they *think* are operating, but without objective probes (delayed test, transfer task, error analysis) the diagnosis is unreliable, and well-intentioned strategy selection based on intuited Learning Processes can systematically miss the actual bottleneck.

## Cognitive Theories of Learning

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science]
- aliases: [cognitivist learning theory]
- broader: [learning-theories]
- narrower: [information-processing-theory, schema-theory-and-learning, cognitive-load-theory, cognitive-theory-of-multimedia-learning]
- related: [psychology-of-learning, [[constructivism]]]
- prerequisites: [learning-theories, cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Theories of Learning are the family of accounts that explain acquisition in terms of internal mental representations and processes — schemas, working memory, attention, encoding, retrieval — rather than in terms of stimulus-response associations or social practice, and provide the dominant theoretical foundation of contemporary instructional design.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Theories of Learning differ in commitments (symbolic vs. connectionist, modular vs. unified, rule-based vs. case-based) but share the move that makes evidence-based pedagogy possible: the postulation of internal mechanisms whose properties (capacity limits, encoding rules, retrieval dynamics) generate testable predictions about which instructional conditions will work.

**warning**: Cognitive Theories of Learning have historically under-weighted social, motivational, and embodied dimensions of acquisition; a curriculum designed purely from Cognitive Theories of Learning often produces well-encoded but unmotivated learners whose acquired knowledge fails to transfer to authentic practice — which is the recurring complaint that constructivist and situated theorists raise.

## Generative Learning Theory

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [generative learning, GLT]
- broader: [cognitive-theories-of-learning]
- related: [elaboration, self-explanation, [[summarisation]], [[concept-mapping]], deep-processing]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-theories-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Generative Learning Theory, traceable to Wittrock (1974) and updated by Fiorella and Mayer (2016), holds that meaningful learning occurs when learners actively *generate* relations between new material and prior knowledge through specific activities — summarising, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting — rather than passively receiving content.

**key_claim**: Generative Learning Theory predicts that the same exposure produces qualitatively different learning depending on whether the learner generates connections during study; meta-analyses confirm moderate-to-large benefits across the generative activities, with the strongest evidence for self-explanation and self-testing.

**warning**: Generative Learning Theory is commonly mistranslated as "any active task is better than passive reading," but generative *activity* must operate on the *target content* and produce semantic integration; busy-work generation (transcribing notes, recopying highlights) feels active but does not generate the integrative connections the theory specifies and therefore yields little benefit.

## Situated Learning Theory

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [anthropology, education]
- aliases: [situated cognition, situated learning]
- broader: [learning-theories]
- related: [communities-of-practice, distributed-cognition, [[legitimate-peripheral-participation]], [[apprenticeship]]]
- prerequisites: [learning-theories]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Situated Learning Theory, developed by Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger, holds that learning is fundamentally a process of becoming a participant in a community of practice — acquiring identity, tools, and skill through legitimate peripheral participation in authentic activity — rather than the abstract acquisition of decontextualised knowledge.

**key_claim**: Situated Learning Theory reframes "knowledge" as inseparable from the practice in which it is exercised, predicting that decontextualised classroom learning will produce knowledge that fails to transfer to the practice settings it ostensibly prepares learners for — a prediction substantially borne out by the [[far-transfer]] literature.

**warning**: Situated Learning Theory is sometimes used to dismiss formal instruction altogether in favour of pure apprenticeship, but apprenticeship has its own well-documented failure modes (idiosyncratic exposure, slow progression, dependence on master availability); the productive synthesis combines situated practice with explicit instruction targeting the components apprenticeship leaves implicit.

## Meaningful Learning Theory

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, education]
- aliases: [Ausubel's theory, subsumption theory]
- broader: [cognitive-theories-of-learning]
- related: [schema-theory-and-learning, prior-knowledge, [[advance-organiser]], assimilation]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-theories-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Meaningful Learning Theory, developed by David Ausubel, distinguishes meaningful from rote acquisition by whether new material is consciously related to relevant existing concepts in the learner's cognitive structure, and holds that "the most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows."

**key_claim**: Meaningful Learning Theory operationalises its central claim through the [[advance-organiser]] — a high-level conceptual scaffold presented before detailed material — that activates relevant prior knowledge and provides anchoring concepts to which new content can be assimilated, producing measurably better integration and retention than expository teaching alone.

**warning**: Meaningful Learning Theory's prescription presupposes that the learner *has* relevant prior knowledge to anchor to; for genuine novices in a domain, advance organisers can present a vocabulary the learner cannot yet parse, creating a second layer of confusion rather than scaffolding — which is why advance-organiser effectiveness is moderated by prior-knowledge level.

## Discovery Learning

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [education, instructional-design]
- aliases: [pure discovery, unguided discovery]
- broader: [learning-theories]
- related: [inquiry-based-learning, [[guided-discovery]], [[bruner]], productive-failure]
- prerequisites: [learning-theories]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Discovery Learning is the instructional approach — championed by Bruner — in which learners are presented with problems, materials, or phenomena and expected to discover the underlying concepts, relations, or procedures themselves rather than receiving them via direct instruction.

**key_claim**: Discovery Learning in its pure unguided form has been repeatedly shown to underperform direct instruction for novice learners (Mayer 2004; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006), because the cognitive resources consumed by search and dead-end exploration leave inadequate capacity for the schema-construction the discovery is supposed to trigger.

**warning**: Discovery Learning's empirical weakness applies specifically to *unguided* discovery; *guided* discovery (with worked-example fading, scaffolded prompts, structured feedback) consistently performs well, and the productive lesson is not "abandon discovery" but "guide it" — the same critique that Mayer (2004) titled "Should there be a three-strikes rule against pure discovery learning?"

## Inquiry-Based Learning

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [education]
- aliases: [inquiry learning, IBL]
- broader: [learning-theories, discovery-learning]
- related: [[problem-based-learning], [[project-based-learning], [[guided-discovery], productive-failure]
- prerequisites: [discovery-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Inquiry-Based Learning is the instructional family in which learners drive acquisition by formulating questions, gathering and evaluating evidence, and constructing explanations under varying degrees of teacher guidance, ranging from highly structured guided inquiry to open inquiry that approaches authentic disciplinary practice.

**key_claim**: Inquiry-Based Learning's effectiveness depends critically on the level of guidance: structured and guided variants consistently outperform unguided inquiry, and the meta-analytic pattern (Furtak et al. 2012; Lazonder & Harmsen 2016) shows that explicit scaffolding of inquiry processes is what differentiates productive from unproductive inquiry.

**warning**: Inquiry-Based Learning is often defended on motivational grounds without measuring learning outcomes; learners frequently *enjoy* inquiry while learning less than they would from explicit instruction, and the pedagogically honest position is to hold Inquiry-Based Learning to learning-outcome evidence rather than to engagement evidence alone.

## Conceptual Change

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, philosophy-of-science]
- aliases: [conceptual restructuring]
- broader: [learning-processes]
- related: [cognitive-disequilibrium, accommodation, [[misconception]], [[ontological-shift]], schema-theory-and-learning]
- prerequisites: [learning-processes]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Conceptual Change is the deep restructuring of a learner's existing concepts — not merely accretion of new facts — required when prior conceptions are systematically incompatible with the target understanding, as in the shift from naive impetus theory to Newtonian mechanics or from a flat-Earth conception to a spherical one.

**key_claim**: Conceptual Change typically requires confrontation with the inadequacy of the existing conception (cognitive dissonance with anomalous data), provision of an intelligible and plausible alternative, and demonstration of the alternative's superior generative power — the conditions specified by Posner et al.'s (1982) classical model and refined by subsequent ontological-shift accounts.

**warning**: Conceptual Change is *slow* and *non-monotonic*: learners who appear to have adopted a target conception frequently revert to the prior conception under stress, novel transfer, or reduced reasoning resources, which means assessment must probe robustness across contexts rather than confirming surface adoption from a single test.

## Schema Theory and Learning

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science]
- aliases: [schema theory in learning]
- broader: [cognitive-theories-of-learning]
- related: [schema-theory, prior-knowledge, [[chunking]], [[abstraction]], assimilation, accommodation]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-theories-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Schema Theory and Learning applies the construct of schemas — organised knowledge structures representing patterns, scripts, and conceptual relations — to explain how prior knowledge structures the encoding, retention, and retrieval of new material, and how expertise consists in the gradual elaboration of richer, more interconnected schemas.

**key_claim**: Schema Theory and Learning predicts that the same instruction produces dramatically different acquisition depending on the schemas the learner already has: novices encode surface features and miss structural patterns, while experts encode deep structure and abstract away surface, which is the well-replicated novice-expert distinction in problem categorisation.

**warning**: Schema Theory and Learning has been criticised for being descriptively rich but mechanistically vague; "build better schemas" is not in itself a pedagogical instruction, and productive use of the framework requires specifying *which* schemas matter, *how* to elicit and probe them, and *what* sequence of experiences reliably restructures them.

## Expertise Development

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, deliberate-practice]
- aliases: [expertise acquisition]
- broader: [learning-processes]
- related: [[deliberate-practice], [[ten-year-rule], cognitive-chunking, [[automaticity], schema-theory-and-learning]
- prerequisites: [learning-processes]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Expertise Development is the multi-year trajectory by which novices become experts in a domain through accumulated deliberate practice, characterised by progressively richer schemas, recognition-based pattern matching, automated subroutines, freed-up working-memory capacity, and qualitatively different problem representations.

**key_claim**: Expertise Development requires deliberate practice — focused work on tasks at the edge of current competence with immediate feedback and explicit revision — rather than mere accumulated experience; experienced practitioners who have stopped engaging in deliberate practice plateau at intermediate levels regardless of years served.

**warning**: Expertise Development's "10,000-hour rule" is widely cited but empirically overstated: the original Ericsson research showed deliberate practice as a *necessary* contributor, not a sufficient one, and the proportion of variance explained varies enormously by domain (high for chess and music, lower for sports, lowest for professions); citing the round number without these qualifications misrepresents the underlying science.

## Knowledge Transfer

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, education]
- aliases: [transfer of learning]
- broader: [learning-processes]
- narrower: [far-transfer, [[near-transfer]]]
- related: [encoding-specificity-principle, [[analogical-transfer]], situated-learning-theory]
- prerequisites: [learning-processes]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Knowledge Transfer is the application of learning acquired in one context to performance in a different context, ranging from near transfer (highly similar contexts) to far transfer (substantially different domains), and is the criterion against which any pedagogy claiming to teach generalisable skills must ultimately be judged.

**key_claim**: Knowledge Transfer is harder to produce and demonstrate than the educational discourse usually admits: most well-controlled studies find robust near transfer but weak or absent far transfer, which forces a re-evaluation of curricula whose advertised payoff is far transfer they cannot demonstrate.

**warning**: Knowledge Transfer is often confused with surface generalisation: a learner who can apply a procedure to structurally similar problems with different cover stories has not demonstrated transfer in the deeper sense of reorganising approach in the face of structurally novel demands; pedagogy that conflates the two over-claims its impact.

## Far Transfer

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, education]
- aliases: [distal transfer]
- broader: [knowledge-transfer]
- related: [[near-transfer], encoding-specificity-principle, [[problem-isomorphs]], [[abstract-principles]]]
- prerequisites: [knowledge-transfer]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Far Transfer is the application of learning to contexts substantially different from those of original acquisition — different surface features, different domains, different problem structures — and is the form of transfer most often invoked to justify general-skills curricula and most often absent in empirical tests.

**key_claim**: Far Transfer is empirically rare: meta-analyses across decades consistently find that learners trained in one domain transfer their gains very modestly to ostensibly related domains, and the conditions that produce Far Transfer when it does occur (multiple varied examples, abstract principle extraction, explicit comparison) are demanding enough that they constitute a distinct pedagogical agenda rather than a side effect of ordinary instruction.

**warning**: Far Transfer claims are the most frequent overreach in educational marketing — "learn coding to think like a scientist," "play chess to improve mathematics" — and almost never survive controlled testing; consumers should require Far Transfer evidence rather than accept theoretical plausibility, and curricula should be evaluated against the transfer they actually produce, not the transfer they advertise.

## Tacit Knowledge

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, expertise-research]
- aliases: [implicit knowledge]
- broader: [knowledge-types]
- related: [procedural-memory, expertise-development, [[polanyi]], [[know-how]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Tacit Knowledge, named by Michael Polanyi in *The Tacit Dimension* (1966), is the knowledge a competent practitioner possesses but cannot fully articulate — embodied skills, perceptual discriminations, situated judgements — captured in the formula "we know more than we can tell" and standing in contrast to explicit propositional knowledge.

**key_claim**: Tacit Knowledge is acquired primarily through participation, observation, and apprenticeship rather than verbal transmission, which explains why textbooks and lectures persistently underdeliver in domains where competence depends on tacit components — clinical diagnosis, craft skill, design judgement, ethnographic interpretation.

**warning**: Tacit Knowledge can be partially externalised through deliberate articulation practices (think-aloud protocols, reflective writing, structured debrief), and treating it as wholly inarticulable abandons productive pedagogical strategies; the productive position is that Tacit Knowledge has an articulable surface that explicit work can reach, surrounding a residue that must still be acquired through practice.

## Assessment Design

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [educational-measurement, instructional-design]
- aliases: [educational assessment]
- broader: [educational-psychology]
- related: [[formative-assessment], [[summative-assessment], [[validity], [[reliability], [[backward-design]]]
- prerequisites: [educational-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Assessment Design is the principled construction of tasks, prompts, and rubrics intended to measure learning outcomes — covering formative assessment (during learning, to inform adjustment) and summative assessment (after learning, to certify achievement) — under explicit constraints of validity, reliability, and consequential impact on instruction.

**key_claim**: Assessment Design exerts more pedagogical leverage than most other instructional choices because *assessment determines what learners attend to*: the constructive alignment principle (Biggs) holds that learning outcomes, learning activities, and assessments must be aligned, and misalignment between intended outcomes and what is assessed reliably reshapes learning toward the assessment.

**warning**: Assessment Design is regularly compromised by what is convenient to grade rather than by what matters: multiple-choice items survive because they scale, even when they encourage surface processing of content whose intended outcome was deep restructuring; pedagogy should resist the gravity that pulls assessment toward measurability at the cost of validity.
