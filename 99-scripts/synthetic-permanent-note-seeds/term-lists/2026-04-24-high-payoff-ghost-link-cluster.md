## Worked Examples

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, instructional-design]
- aliases: [worked-out examples, fully worked examples]
- broader: [instructional-design, cognitive-load-theory]
- narrower: [faded-worked-examples]
- related: [the-worked-example-effect, expertise-reversal-effect, schema-construction, element-interactivity, four-component-instructional-design-4c-id]
- prerequisites: [working-memory, schema, intrinsic-cognitive-load]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Worked Examples are instructional artifacts that present a problem together with its complete, step-by-step solution and the reasoning that justifies each step, so that the learner studies a fully demonstrated trace rather than searching for the solution unaided. Within [[cognitive-load-theory]] they are the canonical instructional vehicle for substituting [[germane-cognitive-load]] (productive [[schema-construction]]) for the [[extraneous-cognitive-load]] generated by means-ends search during conventional problem-solving.

**key_claim**: Worked Examples produce faster and more durable [[schema-construction]] than equivalent practice problems for novices because they free [[working-memory]] from the search load imposed by the means-ends strategy, allowing the learner to attend instead to the structural relations that constitute the target schema — a finding canonized as [[the-worked-example-effect]] and one of the most replicated results in instructional psychology.

**warning**: Worked Examples lose their advantage and can actively harm learning once the learner has acquired the target schema, because the now-redundant explanatory material itself becomes [[extraneous-cognitive-load]]; this reversal — the [[expertise-reversal-effect]] — means Worked Examples must be progressively faded into completion problems and then independent practice as expertise grows, rather than used as a fixed instructional default.

## Self-Efficacy Theory

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [social-cognitive-psychology, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [Bandura self-efficacy theory]
- broader: [social-cognitive-theory]
- narrower: [collective-efficacy, efficacy-calibration, efficacy-hierarchy]
- related: [perceived-self-efficacy, outcome-expectation, triadic-reciprocal-determinism, enactive-mastery-experience, vicarious-experience, verbal-persuasion, physiological-and-affective-states]
- prerequisites: [social-cognitive-theory, autonomous-motivation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Self-Efficacy Theory is Albert Bandura's account, formalised within [[social-cognitive-theory]], of how a person's domain-specific belief in their capability to organise and execute the courses of action required to attain a designated performance — their [[perceived-self-efficacy]] — governs the goals they set, the effort they invest, the persistence they show under setback, and the affective states they experience during pursuit. Self-Efficacy Theory specifies four canonical sources of efficacy information ([[enactive-mastery-experience]], [[vicarious-experience]], [[verbal-persuasion]], and [[physiological-and-affective-states]]) and treats efficacy beliefs as the proximal mediator through which biographical experience translates into self-regulated action.

**key_claim**: Self-Efficacy Theory establishes that capability beliefs are not redundant with actual capability but causally distinct: at equivalent skill levels, learners with higher [[perceived-self-efficacy]] set more challenging goals, deploy more sophisticated strategies, recover faster from failure, and ultimately attain higher performance — making efficacy beliefs the strongest single predictor of behaviour change in domains as varied as academic achievement, clinical recovery, and occupational performance.

**warning**: Self-Efficacy Theory is routinely conflated with self-esteem or generic confidence, but the construct is strictly task-specific and forward-looking; treating it as a global trait collapses [[the-centrality-of-efficacy-over-outcome-expectations]] and licenses the empirically discredited practice of administering generic affirmations as a substitute for the four specific source experiences the theory identifies.

## Extrinsic Motivation

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [externally-regulated motivation]
- broader: [self-determination-theory, organismic-integration-theory]
- narrower: [external-regulation, introjected-regulation, identified-regulation, integrated-regulation]
- related: [intrinsic-motivation, autonomous-motivation, controlled-motivation, internalization, internalization-continuum, intrinsic-vs-extrinsic-goal-contents]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory, the-three-basic-psychological-needs]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Extrinsic Motivation is, within [[self-determination-theory]], the class of motivational states in which an action is undertaken because it leads to a separable outcome — reward, approval, self-worth contingency, instrumental value — rather than for the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself. [[organismic-integration-theory]] resolves Extrinsic Motivation into a four-step [[internalization-continuum]] running from [[external-regulation]] (compliance under contingent reward), through [[introjected-regulation]] (ego-involved self-pressure), to [[identified-regulation]] (endorsement of personal value) and [[integrated-regulation]] (full assimilation into the self-system).

**key_claim**: Extrinsic Motivation is not a uniformly inferior counterpart to [[intrinsic-motivation]] but a graded continuum whose downstream consequences for learning, well-being, and persistence depend on the *quality* of regulation rather than its source; Extrinsic Motivation under [[identified-regulation]] or [[integrated-regulation]] sustains autonomy-relevant outcomes nearly indistinguishable from intrinsic regulation, while [[external-regulation]] and [[introjected-regulation]] forms reliably degrade them.

**warning**: Extrinsic Motivation is widely caricatured as "rewards undermine learning" — a misreading that ignores [[organismic-integration-theory]]'s graded structure; the empirically supported claim is narrower: tangible expected rewards undermine [[intrinsic-motivation]] for already-interesting tasks, while well-designed Extrinsic Motivation (informational feedback, autonomy-supportive rationale, value-congruent goals) reliably *facilitates* internalization toward more autonomous regulation.

## Self-Efficacy

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [social-cognitive-psychology, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [perceived self-efficacy, efficacy belief]
- broader: [social-cognitive-theory, perceived-self-efficacy]
- narrower: [collective-efficacy, efficacy-calibration]
- related: [outcome-expectation, enactive-mastery-experience, vicarious-experience, verbal-persuasion, physiological-and-affective-states, triadic-reciprocal-determinism, the-centrality-of-efficacy-over-outcome-expectations]
- prerequisites: [social-cognitive-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Self-Efficacy is a person's domain-specific judgment of their capability to organise and execute the courses of action required to produce a designated level of attainment — that is, the answer to the operational question *"can I do this, in this situation, to this standard?"* — and stands at the centre of [[social-cognitive-theory]] as the most proximal cognitive mediator between past experience and future self-regulated action. Self-Efficacy is built from four converging information sources: [[enactive-mastery-experience]] (the most potent), [[vicarious-experience]], [[verbal-persuasion]], and [[physiological-and-affective-states]].

**key_claim**: Self-Efficacy is causally distinct from [[outcome-expectation]] and from prior performance: at matched skill, learners higher in Self-Efficacy choose harder goals, deploy more effective strategies, persist longer under aversive feedback, and ultimately outperform — establishing efficacy belief as a primary lever for behaviour change rather than a downstream byproduct of past success.

**warning**: Self-Efficacy is often equated with optimism, self-esteem, or generic confidence, but the construct is strictly task- and standard-specific; treating Self-Efficacy as a global trait — and attempting to raise it via decontextualised affirmations — produces overconfident, miscalibrated learners and collapses the diagnostic value of [[efficacy-calibration]].

## Achievement Goal Theory

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [achievement goal framework, goal orientation theory]
- broader: [self-regulated-learning]
- narrower: [mastery-approach-goal, performance-avoidance-goal, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]
- related: [achievement-goal, mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation, performance-goal-vs-learning-goal, mastery-climate, mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate, the-four-by-four-matrix, conception-of-ability]
- prerequisites: [conception-of-ability, task-value]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Achievement Goal Theory is the family of motivational frameworks — originating in the work of Carol Dweck, John Nicholls, and Carol Ames and refined by Andrew Elliot — that classifies the *purposes* learners pursue in achievement settings according to two orthogonal dimensions: the **definition** of competence (mastery of a task vs. normative comparison to others) and the **valence** of competence (approaching success vs. avoiding failure). The crossing of these dimensions yields [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]] of mastery-approach, mastery-avoidance, performance-approach, and performance-avoidance goals, each with distinct affective, cognitive, and behavioural signatures.

**key_claim**: Achievement Goal Theory predicts that *mastery-approach goals* — pursuing competence for its own sake — most reliably support adaptive learning patterns (deep processing, help-seeking, persistence under failure), whereas *performance-avoidance goals* most reliably produce maladaptive patterns ([[self-handicapping]], surface processing, withdrawal); the theory's central practical lever is the [[mastery-climate]] in which classroom and workplace structures shift learners toward mastery-approach orientation.

**warning**: Achievement Goal Theory is frequently flattened to a "mastery good, performance bad" dichotomy, which both misrepresents the empirical record (performance-approach goals reliably predict performance attainment in competitive contexts) and obscures [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]]'s critical contribution: that approach-vs-avoidance valence, not the mastery-vs-performance distinction alone, carries most of the variance in adaptive vs maladaptive outcomes.

## Attribution Theory

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [social-cognitive-psychology, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [Weiner attribution theory, attributional theory of achievement motivation]
- broader: [motivational-psychology]
- narrower: [attribution, attributional-vocabulary, explanatory-style-attributional-style, attribution-retraining]
- related: [causal-attribution, locus-of-causality, controllability-dimension, stability-dimension, the-locus-of-causality-dimension, the-stability-dimension, the-controllability-dimension, attribution-dependent-emotion, the-attributional-bridge, the-fundamental-attribution-error]
- prerequisites: [causal-attribution, perceived-locus-of-causality]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Attribution Theory is Bernard Weiner's account of how the *causal explanations* a person constructs for success and failure — rather than the outcomes themselves — govern subsequent affect, expectancy, and motivated behaviour. Attribution Theory organises the space of causal explanations along three structural dimensions — [[locus-of-causality]] (internal vs external), [[stability-dimension]] (stable vs unstable), and [[controllability-dimension]] (controllable vs uncontrollable) — and traces how each dimension carries a distinct motivational signature into the next achievement episode.

**key_claim**: Attribution Theory establishes that the motivational consequences of failure are determined by *how the failure is explained*, not by the failure itself: attributing a poor result to lack of effort (internal, unstable, controllable) preserves expectancy and re-engagement, whereas attributing the same result to lack of ability (internal, stable, uncontrollable) produces shame, [[learned-helplessness]], and withdrawal — making causal attribution a primary leverage point for [[attribution-retraining]] interventions.

**warning**: Attribution Theory is regularly misread as endorsing pure effort attributions as a universal corrective, but [[the-stability-expectancy-principle]] and [[the-functional-priority-of-unexpected-and-negative-outcomes]] together imply that effort attributions are protective only when effort was in fact deployed; attributing genuinely strategy-deficient failure to "not trying hard enough" produces [[the-fundamental-attribution-error]] turned inward and licenses repetition of the same failed approach.

## Growth Mindset

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [incremental theory of intelligence, malleable mindset]
- broader: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence]
- narrower: []
- related: [conception-of-ability, mastery-approach-goal, mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation, attribution-theory, the-developmental-differentiation-of-effort-and-ability, learned-helplessness, achievement-goal-theory]
- prerequisites: [conception-of-ability]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Growth Mindset is Carol Dweck's term for the implicit lay theory that intellectual ability is a malleable, cultivable quality that grows through effort, strategy, and instruction — contrasted with the *fixed mindset* (entity theory) that treats ability as an essentially unchangeable endowment. Growth Mindset is one pole of the broader [[implicit-theories-of-intelligence]] construct and predicts a coherent downstream cluster: mastery-oriented response to setbacks, productive [[causal-attribution]] of failure to controllable factors, willingness to undertake [[desirable-difficulties]], and resilience against [[learned-helplessness]].

**key_claim**: Growth Mindset functions as a *meaning system* rather than a single attitude: holding a Growth Mindset reliably predicts that a learner will interpret failure as informative feedback, deploy effort as a strategy rather than a confession of inadequacy, and seek out challenges that maximise [[schema-construction]] — a cluster of behaviours that together produce measurable achievement gains, especially among learners exposed to stereotype threat or chronic underperformance.

**warning**: Growth Mindset has been widely diluted into a slogan ("praise effort, not ability") whose effect sizes in field-scale interventions are far smaller than the original lab studies suggested; treating Growth Mindset as a one-shot motivational poster — without restructuring the [[mastery-climate]], the assessment regime, and the feedback ecology — typically produces null effects and risks training learners to *perform* effort displays rather than internalise an incremental theory.

## Scaffolding

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [instructional-design, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [instructional scaffolding, learning scaffolding]
- broader: [instructional-design]
- narrower: [cognitive-scaffolding, metacognitive-scaffolding, scaffolded-fading, scaffolding-fading-progression]
- related: [the-metacognitive-scaffolding-principle, autonomy-supportive-structure, four-component-instructional-design-4c-id, supportive-information, procedural-information, just-in-time-principle, the-just-in-time-principle, zone-of-proximal-development]
- prerequisites: [working-memory, intrinsic-cognitive-load]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Scaffolding is the deliberate provision of temporary, calibrated supports that allow a learner to perform a task currently beyond their independent capability, on the explicit precondition that the supports will be progressively withdrawn as competence develops. Coined by Wood, Bruner, and Ross (1976) and grounded in Vygotsky's zone of proximal development, Scaffolding is the parent category from which [[cognitive-scaffolding]], [[metacognitive-scaffolding]], and the [[scaffolded-fading]] / [[scaffolding-fading-progression]] design pattern descend.

**key_claim**: Scaffolding's defining commitment is **fading** — supports that are not engineered to be withdrawn cease to be Scaffolding and become permanent crutches that block the [[schema-construction]] and [[strategic-automaticity]] the original supports were meant to bootstrap; this is why effective Scaffolding is identified by its *removal trajectory* rather than its initial richness.

**warning**: Scaffolding is routinely conflated with "any helpful support a teacher provides," collapsing the construct into a vague positive term and erasing the engineering discipline that makes it work; without a specified fading schedule, a transferred-control criterion, and a [[cognitive-forcing-function]] that prevents premature dependence, what is offered as Scaffolding is in practice a recipe for the very dependency it claims to prevent.

## Expectancy-Value Theory

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [Eccles expectancy-value theory, EVT, modern expectancy-value theory]
- broader: [motivational-psychology, achievement-goal-theory]
- narrower: [task-value]
- related: [outcome-expectation, perceived-self-efficacy, intrinsic-motivation, attribution-theory, achievement-goal, conception-of-ability, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework, intrinsic-vs-extrinsic-goal-contents]
- prerequisites: [task-value, outcome-expectation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Expectancy-Value Theory is the family of motivational frameworks — most fully developed by Jacquelynne Eccles and Allan Wigfield — that models achievement-relevant choice, persistence, and performance as the multiplicative product of two appraisals: the learner's *expectancy* of success (closely related to [[perceived-self-efficacy]]) and the *subjective value* the learner places on the task. Expectancy-Value Theory decomposes [[task-value]] into four subcomponents — attainment value (identity-fit), intrinsic value (interest), utility value (instrumental usefulness), and cost (effort, opportunity, emotional risk).

**key_claim**: Expectancy-Value Theory predicts that motivation collapses whenever *either* term approaches zero: a learner who believes they can succeed but finds the task valueless will not engage, and a learner who deeply values an outcome but expects to fail will withdraw — making Expectancy-Value Theory the rare motivational framework that natively diagnoses the *interaction* of capability beliefs and value beliefs rather than treating them as independent additive predictors.

**warning**: Expectancy-Value Theory is often reduced to a slogan ("motivate by raising expectancy and value"), which obscures the cost component that Eccles' modern formulation introduced; ignoring perceived cost — effort cost, opportunity cost, psychological cost — produces interventions that successfully raise expectancy and value yet fail to change behaviour because the learner's net Expectancy-Value Theory calculation remains negative.

## Automaticity

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, instructional-design]
- aliases: [skill automatization, automatic processing]
- broader: [schema-automation, strategic-automaticity]
- narrower: []
- related: [schema-construction, schema, the-power-law-of-practice, power-law-of-practice, deliberate-practice, working-memory, long-term-working-memory, type-1-processing, type-2-processing, dual-process-theory, retrieval-structure]
- prerequisites: [schema, working-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Automaticity is the property a cognitive operation acquires after extensive, well-structured practice whereby the operation can be executed quickly, with minimal demand on [[working-memory]], without conscious attentional control, and without interference from concurrent cognitive load. Within [[cognitive-load-theory]] and skill-acquisition research, Automaticity is the terminal state of [[schema-automation]]: the schema has been compiled to the point that its execution no longer competes for the limited capacity that intrinsic and germane processing demand.

**key_claim**: Automaticity is the proximal mechanism by which expertise circumvents [[working-memory]] limits: the expert is not faster because their working memory is larger but because routine sub-operations have been compiled into automatic schemas, freeing the same finite capacity for high-level integration — this is why [[the-power-law-of-practice]] and the [[expertise-reversal-effect]] together imply that instructional design must shift from supporting controlled processing to demanding the practice that produces Automaticity.

**warning**: Automaticity is routinely treated as an unalloyed good, but automatic execution is by definition resistant to conscious correction; once a flawed sub-procedure has been practiced to Automaticity it is exceptionally hard to extinguish and tends to reassert itself under stress, which is why deliberate-practice protocols emphasise *catching errors before* they automate rather than relying on later remediation.
