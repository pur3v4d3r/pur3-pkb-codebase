# Batch: Critical Thinking & Cognitive Bias

## Critical Thinking

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, education]
- aliases: [CT]
- broader: [reasoning]
- narrower: [critical-reasoning, [[informal-logic], argument-analysis]
- related: [critical-reasoning, intellectual-standards, paul-elder-framework, delphi-report]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Critical Thinking is the disciplined process of actively conceptualising, applying, analysing, synthesising, and evaluating information gathered from observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication as a guide to belief and action — a definition that integrates the cognitive-skills tradition (Ennis, Facione) with the dispositional tradition (Paul, Elder).

**key_claim**: Critical Thinking, on the consensus Delphi-report definition, is best understood as the integration of *cognitive skills* (analysis, evaluation, inference, interpretation, explanation, self-regulation) and *dispositional habits of mind* (truth-seeking, open-mindedness, intellectual humility, systematicity, analyticity); skills without dispositions produce sophistry, dispositions without skills produce sincere confusion.

**warning**: Critical Thinking is widely promoted as a transferable skill but the empirical literature on transfer is sobering — much of what passes for Critical Thinking instruction produces domain-specific gains that do not generalise to ostensibly related domains, and pedagogy claiming broad-spectrum Critical Thinking outcomes should be required to produce evidence at the transfer distances it advertises.

## Critical Reasoning

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [logic, philosophy]
- aliases: [analytic reasoning]
- broader: [critical-thinking, reasoning]
- related: [argument-analysis, [[informal-logic], inference, [[evidence-evaluation]]]
- prerequisites: [critical-thinking]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Critical Reasoning is the analytic core of critical thinking — the deliberate evaluation of arguments and evidence, identification of assumptions, assessment of inferential relations, and judgement of conclusion strength — and is what makes critical-thinking dispositions actionable in evaluating any specific claim.

**key_claim**: Critical Reasoning operates by making argument structure explicit (premises, intermediate conclusions, final conclusion), assessing each premise for plausibility, evaluating the strength of each inferential step, and identifying unstated assumptions, after which the conclusion can be evaluated rather than merely accepted or rejected on surface impression.

**warning**: Critical Reasoning is often confused with mere disagreement or scepticism, but reflexive contrarianism is not Critical Reasoning — it is the inverse failure mode, in which any claim is rejected regardless of evidence; genuine Critical Reasoning includes accepting well-supported conclusions one initially disliked, which is the discipline that distinguishes it from oppositional reflex.

## Intellectual Standards

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, education]
- aliases: [Paul-Elder intellectual standards]
- broader: [critical-thinking, paul-elder-framework]
- related: [paul-elder-critical-thinking-framework, intellectual-humility, [[clarity], [[accuracy], [[precision]]]
- prerequisites: [critical-thinking]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Intellectual Standards, in the Paul-Elder framework, are the criteria — clarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, significance, fairness — that thought must satisfy to be considered well-reasoned, and provide the evaluative vocabulary by which thinking is assessed and improved.

**key_claim**: Intellectual Standards function as a diagnostic checklist: when thinking goes wrong, it typically fails one or more specific Intellectual Standards (unclear, inaccurate, irrelevant, superficial, narrow, illogical, trivial, unfair) and identifying which standards are failing converts vague dissatisfaction with reasoning into actionable revision.

**warning**: Intellectual Standards are often taught as labels to memorise rather than as a working vocabulary deployed during real reasoning; the productive use of Intellectual Standards is in *Socratic questioning* of one's own and others' thinking ("could you be more precise? what evidence supports that? what's an alternative?") and instruction that stops at definitions rarely produces this working facility.

## Intellectual Humility

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [virtue-epistemology, philosophy]
- aliases: [epistemic humility]
- broader: [intellectual-virtues]
- related: [virtue-epistemology, [[open-mindedness], [[fallibilism], [[epistemic-courage]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Intellectual Humility is the disposition to recognise the limits of one's own knowledge and reasoning — including one's susceptibility to bias, the partiality of one's evidence, and the possibility that one is wrong — and to engage with disagreement as potentially informative rather than as threat to be defended against.

**key_claim**: Intellectual Humility is associated in the empirical literature with better belief updating in response to new evidence, more productive disagreement, and reduced susceptibility to motivated reasoning — making it not merely a moral virtue but an epistemic capacity with measurable consequences for the accuracy of one's beliefs.

**warning**: Intellectual Humility can be performatively imitated ("I might be wrong about this, but...") without the underlying disposition that makes it operative; the test of Intellectual Humility is not what one professes about one's fallibility but how one responds when an interlocutor presents evidence one's professed beliefs are wrong, and many self-professed humble thinkers fail that test.

## Virtue Epistemology

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, virtue-ethics]
- aliases: [virtue-theoretic epistemology]
- broader: [epistemology]
- related: [intellectual-humility, [[intellectual-virtues], [[zagzebski], [[sosa], [[reliabilism]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Virtue Epistemology is the family of epistemological theories — developed by Linda Zagzebski, Ernest Sosa, and others — that analyses knowledge and rational belief in terms of the intellectual virtues exercised by the believer (open-mindedness, intellectual humility, intellectual courage, fairness, thoroughness) rather than purely in terms of belief-evidence relations or external reliability.

**key_claim**: Virtue Epistemology argues that what distinguishes knowledge from mere true belief is the *competence* with which the belief was formed — the exercise of intellectual virtues that reliably track truth — and this framing connects epistemology with character formation, education, and the cultivation of intellectual habits.

**warning**: Virtue Epistemology has been criticised for circularity (intellectual virtues defined as those that produce true beliefs, true beliefs identified by virtuously-formed inquiry) and for difficulty operationalising what counts as a virtue in contested domains; treating Virtue Epistemology as a complete theory of knowledge runs into these problems, while treating it as a productive complement to other epistemologies preserves its insights.

## Motivated Reasoning

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [social-psychology, judgement-and-decision-making]
- aliases: [directional motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition]
- broader: [reasoning, cognitive-bias]
- related: [confirmation-bias, [[kunda], [[identity-protective-cognition], [[wishful-thinking]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Motivated Reasoning, formalised in Ziva Kunda's 1990 review, is the empirically robust phenomenon by which preferences, identities, and goals influence which evidence is searched for, attended to, accepted, and remembered, producing a directional bias in beliefs toward conclusions the reasoner is motivated to reach — without the reasoner experiencing the process as biased.

**key_claim**: Motivated Reasoning operates within the constraint of *plausibility* — people don't believe just anything they want to believe, but they accept lower evidence thresholds for desired conclusions and demand higher evidence for unwelcome ones — which makes Motivated Reasoning particularly hard to detect introspectively because the resulting beliefs feel evidence-based.

**warning**: Motivated Reasoning is not eliminated by intelligence, education, or domain expertise; in some research (Kahan et al. on identity-protective cognition), more numerate or scientifically literate participants display *more* polarised reasoning on identity-charged topics, because their greater reasoning capacity is recruited to defend in-group conclusions, which means cognitive sophistication is no defence against Motivated Reasoning.

## Confirmation Bias

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [judgement-and-decision-making, social-psychology]
- aliases: [confirmatory bias, my-side bias]
- broader: [cognitive-bias]
- related: [motivated-reasoning, [[selective-exposure], [[disconfirmation-bias], [[wason-task]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Confirmation Bias, named by Wason (1960), is the systematic tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information in ways that confirm pre-existing beliefs and to under-weight, dismiss, or fail to recall disconfirming information — a robust pattern observed across hypothesis testing, belief evaluation, and information consumption.

**key_claim**: Confirmation Bias is one of the most thoroughly replicated cognitive biases in the literature, operating across topic areas (politics, science, social judgement) and through multiple mechanisms (selective exposure, biased interpretation of mixed evidence, asymmetric scrutiny of supportive vs. contrary information, biased memory retrieval), making it a structural rather than incidental feature of human cognition.

**warning**: Confirmation Bias is frequently invoked as an all-purpose explanation for any belief one disagrees with, but the diagnosis "you have Confirmation Bias" tends to be itself an instance of Confirmation Bias if applied selectively to the other side; rigorous application requires looking for the same patterns in one's own reasoning, which is psychologically aversive enough that it is rarely done.

## Availability Heuristic

- domain: judgement-and-decision-making
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, behavioural-economics]
- aliases: [availability bias]
- broader: [heuristics-and-biases, cognitive-bias]
- related: [[representativeness-heuristic], [[anchoring-heuristic], [[tversky-and-kahneman], [[ease-of-recall]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Availability Heuristic, identified by Tversky and Kahneman (1973), is the cognitive shortcut in which probability or frequency judgements are based on how easily relevant instances come to mind — leading to systematic over-estimation of vivid, recent, or emotionally charged events (plane crashes, shark attacks, terrorism deaths) and under-estimation of routine, statistical events.

**key_claim**: The Availability Heuristic explains a wide range of risk-perception errors: news-driven attention to dramatic but rare hazards crowds out attention to mundane but high-frequency hazards, and the resulting risk priorities (security spending vs. road-safety spending, for example) systematically misallocate resources relative to underlying base rates.

**warning**: The Availability Heuristic is harder to debias than people typically expect: knowing about it does not eliminate it, and even decision-makers who are explicitly trained in base-rate reasoning continue to display the bias when judgements are made under time pressure — which means structural correctives (decision aids that surface base rates) typically outperform purely educational ones.

## System 1

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [judgement-and-decision-making, dual-process-theory]
- aliases: [Type 1 processing, intuitive system]
- broader: [dual-process-theory]
- related: [system-2, [[heuristics-and-biases], [[kahneman], [[fast-thinking]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: System 1, in Kahneman's *Thinking Fast and Slow* terminology drawing on Stanovich and Evans, refers to the family of fast, automatic, effortless, often emotional cognitive processes that produce intuitive judgements and rapid pattern recognition — and stands in contrast to System 2's slow, deliberate, effortful, rule-based processing.

**key_claim**: System 1 is responsible for the majority of moment-to-moment cognition and is highly competent in familiar, well-learned domains, but it produces systematic errors when its associative pattern matching is applied to problems that require statistical, base-rate, or counter-intuitive reasoning — which is the domain in which the heuristics-and-biases tradition has documented predictable departures from normative responses.

**warning**: System 1 vs. System 2 is a useful expository simplification rather than a clean neuroscientific division; current dual-process theorists (Evans, Stanovich) explicitly note that System 1 is not a single homogeneous system but a family of automatic processes with different mechanisms, and citing System 1 as a unitary cause of an error often obscures more than it explains.

## System 2

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [judgement-and-decision-making, dual-process-theory]
- aliases: [Type 2 processing, deliberative system]
- broader: [dual-process-theory]
- related: [system-1, [[working-memory], [[deliberation], [[slow-thinking]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: System 2, in Kahneman's terminology, refers to the family of slow, effortful, deliberate, working-memory-intensive cognitive processes that perform rule-based reasoning, controlled attention, and explicit calculation — and stands in contrast to System 1's fast, automatic, intuitive processing.

**key_claim**: System 2 is the locus of the cognitive operations that override System 1 intuitions when those intuitions are wrong — but System 2 is also expensive (slow, fatiguing, working-memory-limited) and lazy (defaulting to endorsing System 1 outputs unless a conflict is detected), which is why even well-trained reasoners frequently fail to deploy System 2 when it would correct System 1 errors.

**warning**: System 2 is not always more accurate than System 1; in domains where System 1 has accumulated extensive valid pattern-matching (expert chess intuition, native language comprehension, well-learned motor skill), forced System 2 deliberation can degrade rather than improve performance, which is why "always think more carefully" is bad advice in domains of genuine expertise.

## Inference

- domain: logic
- secondary_domains: [reasoning, philosophy]
- aliases: [inferential reasoning, drawing inferences]
- broader: [reasoning]
- narrower: [deductive-reasoning, inductive-reasoning, abductive-reasoning]
- related: [argument-analysis, [[validity], [[soundness]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Inference is the cognitive operation of moving from accepted premises to a conclusion by virtue of a logical or evidential relation between them — encompassing deductive Inference (where the conclusion necessarily follows), inductive Inference (where the conclusion is supported with some probability), and abductive Inference (where the conclusion is the best available explanation).

**key_claim**: Inference quality is what argument analysis and critical reasoning ultimately evaluate: a complete evaluation of an argument requires assessing both *premise truth* and *Inference strength*, and many real-world disagreements stem from disputes about Inference strength (does this evidence really support that conclusion?) rather than about the premises themselves.

**warning**: Inference is often taught as a purely formal matter of logical validity, but most real-world Inference is non-deductive — evidential, inductive, or abductive — and pedagogy that focuses on formal validity to the exclusion of inductive and abductive reasoning leaves learners unprepared for the actual Inference patterns they will encounter in news, science, and ordinary judgement.

## Disposition

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [psychology, virtue-epistemology]
- aliases: [thinking dispositions, intellectual dispositions]
- broader: [critical-thinking]
- related: [intellectual-humility, virtue-epistemology, [[need-for-cognition], [[actively-open-minded-thinking]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Disposition, in critical-thinking and virtue-epistemology research, is a stable inclination to engage one's cognitive abilities in particular ways — to seek out alternative views, to follow evidence where it leads, to revise beliefs when warranted, to think carefully under uncertainty — and stands in contrast to *abilities* (what one can do) by specifying what one is *inclined* to do.

**key_claim**: A Disposition is what determines whether available critical-thinking abilities actually get deployed; a learner with high analytic ability who is disinclined to use it on welcome conclusions, or who is disinclined to apply it under social pressure, will produce poor reasoning outcomes despite having the requisite skills, which is why disposition cultivation is an irreducible part of critical-thinking education.

**warning**: A Disposition is not directly observable and must be inferred from behaviour across situations; self-report measures of intellectual Disposition have well-documented social-desirability problems, and behavioural measures (actually-open-minded responses to disconfirming evidence under pressure) are needed to validate that claimed Dispositions are actually present.

## Epistemic Vigilance

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [evolutionary-psychology, social-cognition]
- aliases: [vigilance toward testimony]
- broader: [epistemology, social-cognition]
- related: [[sperber], [[mercier-and-sperber], [[testimony], [[deception-detection]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Epistemic Vigilance, theorised by Dan Sperber and colleagues (2010), is the suite of cognitive mechanisms that humans deploy to evaluate the trustworthiness of testimony and the plausibility of communicated content — calibrating belief against source competence and benevolence, and against the content's coherence with one's existing knowledge.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Vigilance, in Mercier and Sperber's argumentative theory of reasoning, is the evolutionary context for human reasoning capacities: reasoning evolved primarily for the social tasks of evaluating others' arguments and producing defensible ones, which explains why reasoning works well in dialectical contexts and so often produces motivated, biased outputs in solitary belief-formation.

**warning**: Epistemic Vigilance is well-tuned for face-to-face communication with known individuals but is poorly adapted for contemporary information environments — anonymous online sources, algorithmically curated feeds, deepfakes — where the cues Epistemic Vigilance evolved to use (social context, identifiable source, coherent reputation) are absent or simulated; productive deployment in these environments requires explicit augmentation rather than reliance on intuitive vigilance alone.

## Bloom's Taxonomy

- domain: education
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [Bloom taxonomy, revised Bloom's]
- broader: [educational-psychology]
- related: [[remembering], [[understanding], [[applying], [[analysing], [[evaluating], [[creating]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Bloom's Taxonomy is the hierarchical classification of cognitive learning objectives — published by Benjamin Bloom and colleagues in 1956 and revised by Anderson and Krathwohl in 2001 — that orders cognitive processes from lower-order (remember, understand, apply) to higher-order (analyse, evaluate, create), and serves as a planning framework for instructional design and assessment.

**key_claim**: Bloom's Taxonomy provides a vocabulary that exposes the gap between what curricula *say* they teach and what their assessments actually measure; the routine finding that learning objectives invoke higher-order verbs while assessments only test lower-order recall is one of the most consistently identified misalignments in instructional audits using the Taxonomy.

**warning**: Bloom's Taxonomy is widely treated as a strict hierarchy in which lower levels must be mastered before higher levels are addressed, but the empirical evidence for strict hierarchical dependency is weak; pedagogically, higher-order thinking can be productively engaged on partially-understood content, and waiting for "mastery" before allowing analysis or evaluation often defers the very engagement that consolidates lower-order knowledge.

## Paul-Elder Framework

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [education, philosophy]
- aliases: [Paul-Elder model]
- broader: [critical-thinking]
- related: [paul-elder-critical-thinking-framework, intellectual-standards, [[elements-of-thought], intellectual-humility]
- prerequisites: [critical-thinking]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Paul-Elder Framework is the integrated model of critical thinking developed by Richard Paul and Linda Elder consisting of *eight elements of thought* (purpose, question, information, inferences, concepts, assumptions, implications, point of view), *nine intellectual standards* by which those elements are evaluated, and *intellectual virtues* that govern their use over time.

**key_claim**: The Paul-Elder Framework is among the most fully-elaborated practical critical-thinking frameworks available, providing a working vocabulary that can be applied to virtually any reasoning task and that has been empirically associated with improved disciplined thinking when systematically taught and practised across multiple courses.

**warning**: The Paul-Elder Framework requires substantial sustained instruction and practice to internalise; brief workshop introductions reliably produce learners who can recite the elements and standards without using them in their actual reasoning, which is why the Foundation for Critical Thinking explicitly recommends multi-course integration rather than one-off deployment.

## Paul-Elder Critical Thinking Framework

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [education, philosophy]
- aliases: [Paul-Elder critical thinking model]
- broader: [paul-elder-framework, critical-thinking]
- related: [paul-elder-framework, intellectual-standards, [[elements-of-reasoning]]]
- prerequisites: [paul-elder-framework]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Paul-Elder Critical Thinking Framework is the canonical full name for the Paul-Elder Framework when the focus is specifically its application to critical thinking rather than to its component pieces — comprising the elements of thought, the universal intellectual standards, and the intellectual virtues — and is the version most cited in educational standards and assessment instruments.

**key_claim**: The Paul-Elder Critical Thinking Framework operationalises critical thinking as the *sustained application of intellectual standards to the elements of thought*: a critical thinker takes any piece of reasoning, identifies its elements (what's the purpose? the question? the assumptions?), and evaluates each against the intellectual standards (clear? accurate? relevant? deep?), making the framework concrete and trainable.

**warning**: The Paul-Elder Critical Thinking Framework can be misapplied as a checklist that learners run through mechanically, producing documentation of elements and standards without genuine evaluative engagement; the framework is intended as scaffolding for *internalised* critical disposition, and instruction that stops at the checklist stage produces compliance rather than capacity.

## 2x2 Framework

- domain: analytical-frameworks
- secondary_domains: [decision-making, business-strategy]
- aliases: [2x2 matrix]
- broader: [analytical-frameworks]
- related: [[four-quadrant-analysis], [[eisenhower-matrix], [[swot-analysis], [[boston-matrix]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: medium

**definition**: A 2x2 Framework is the analytical pattern of crossing two binary or continuous dimensions to produce four quadrants that organise cases, options, or strategies — exemplified by the Eisenhower urgent/important matrix, the BCG growth/share matrix, and many strategic-management tools — and is widely used because it makes trade-offs visible at a glance.

**key_claim**: A 2x2 Framework is most useful when the two dimensions are genuinely orthogonal (independent) and exhaustive (jointly cover the space), in which case the four quadrants reveal the strategically distinct types of cases or options and recommend differentiated responses; the simplicity is the value when those conditions are met.

**warning**: A 2x2 Framework is regularly applied in domains where the chosen dimensions are not orthogonal or where two dimensions are insufficient, producing forced classifications and false strategic clarity; consumers should ask "are these dimensions really independent?" and "do four cells really cover the space?" before accepting the framework's recommendations as decision-relevant.

## Argument Analysis

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [logic, philosophy]
- aliases: [argument mapping, argument reconstruction]
- broader: [critical-thinking, critical-reasoning]
- related: [critical-reasoning, inference, [[premise], [[conclusion], [[argument-mapping]]]
- prerequisites: [critical-reasoning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Argument Analysis is the disciplined practice of identifying the structure of an argument — separating premises from conclusions, identifying intermediate inferences, surfacing unstated assumptions, and distinguishing main argument from supporting and qualifying material — as a precondition for evaluating the argument's strength.

**key_claim**: Argument Analysis often reveals that real-world arguments are different from what they appear to be on the surface: the stated conclusion is not the operative conclusion, the apparent premises depend on unstated assumptions doing the heavy lifting, and the persuasive force comes from rhetorical scaffolding rather than from logical structure — making Argument Analysis a substantive contribution to evaluation, not a clerical preliminary.

**warning**: Argument Analysis is sometimes practised as adversarial demolition — finding any flaw to discredit the argument — rather than as charitable reconstruction — finding the strongest version of the argument before evaluating it; the intellectually serious form is the latter, and analysts whose Argument Analysis only criticises arguments they disagreed with already are doing rhetoric, not analysis.

## Socratic Method

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, pedagogy]
- aliases: [Socratic questioning, elenchus]
- broader: [pedagogy, critical-thinking]
- related: [[plato], paul-elder-framework, intellectual-standards, [[productive-questioning]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Socratic Method is the dialectical pedagogical practice — modelled on Socrates as represented in the Platonic dialogues — of advancing inquiry through systematic questioning that elicits the interlocutor's beliefs, surfaces their assumptions and implications, exposes inconsistencies, and motivates revision toward more defensible positions.

**key_claim**: The Socratic Method, in its productive form, treats the interlocutor as a partner in inquiry whose existing beliefs are to be examined collaboratively rather than refuted; the questioning is structured to make the interlocutor's own reasoning visible to them, which produces more durable revision than direct argumentation does.

**warning**: The Socratic Method is widely degraded into "Socratic ambush" — a teacher uses leading questions to guide students to a predetermined conclusion while preserving the appearance of inquiry — and in this degraded form it loses its developmental value while actively training learners in performative compliance rather than in genuine inquiry; the genuine Socratic Method is open to the possibility that the inquiry will go somewhere the questioner did not anticipate.

## Delphi Report

- domain: critical-thinking
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, education]
- aliases: [APA Delphi Report, 1990 Delphi Report]
- broader: [critical-thinking]
- related: [critical-thinking, [[facione], [[expert-consensus], [[ccss-test]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Delphi Report is the 1990 American Philosophical Association consensus statement, edited by Peter Facione, in which a panel of forty-six critical-thinking experts produced a Delphi-method consensus definition of critical thinking — analytic and dispositional — that has shaped subsequent assessment instruments (the California Critical Thinking Skills Test) and curricular standards.

**key_claim**: The Delphi Report is significant because it demonstrated substantial expert consensus on the operative components of critical thinking — six core cognitive skills (interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, explanation, self-regulation) and a list of dispositional habits — providing an empirical basis for an otherwise contested concept and making subsequent assessment work tractable.

**warning**: The Delphi Report represents a snapshot of expert consensus from a specific community at a specific time and should not be treated as the final word on critical thinking; later work has questioned the universality of its dispositional list across cultures, the assessability of self-regulation as defined, and the boundary between "critical thinking" skills and adjacent constructs (problem-solving, scientific reasoning, decision-making).
