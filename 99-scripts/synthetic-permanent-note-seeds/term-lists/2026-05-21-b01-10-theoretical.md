# Batch: B01-10 Theoretical Foundations

## LLM Scaling Laws

- secondary_domains: [machine-learning-theory, empirical-ml, neural-scaling]
- aliases: [neural scaling laws, Chinchilla scaling, Kaplan scaling laws, compute-optimal scaling]
- broader: [llm-theory, empirical-machine-learning]
- narrower: [chinchilla-scaling-law, kaplan-scaling-law, compute-optimal-training]
- related: [emergent-abilities-in-llms, phase-transitions-in-llms, parameter-efficient-fine-tuning]
- prerequisites: [large-language-models, neural-network-training, empirical-machine-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: LLM scaling laws are empirical power-law relationships between model performance (measured as test loss), model scale (parameters N, training tokens D, and compute budget C), discovered through systematic experimentation on language models. The seminal Kaplan et al. (2020) scaling laws showed that loss follows a power law in N and D independently, with compute-efficient training allocating most compute to model size. Hoffmann et al. (2022) (Chinchilla) revised this, finding that optimal performance for a given compute budget requires equal scaling of model size and training tokens — a model should be trained on approximately 20 tokens per parameter — substantially reducing the optimal model size and increasing training data relative to Kaplan's prescriptions.

**key_claim**: Chinchilla scaling laws revealed that the field's prior practice of undertraining large models (training GPT-3-scale models on far fewer tokens than optimal) was systematically compute-inefficient — the Chinchilla model (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) outperformed the much larger Gopher model (280B parameters, 300B tokens) by using the same compute budget more efficiently, demonstrating that training data scaling is at least as important as parameter scaling for compute-optimal performance.

**warning**: LLM scaling laws are empirical observations with bounded domain validity — they are measured on specific model architectures, tokenizers, data mixes, and training procedures, and their extrapolation beyond the measured range is uncertain; the discovery of emergent capabilities (qualitative capability jumps at certain scales) suggests that power-law extrapolation may miss qualitative transitions, and architectural innovations can shift the scaling curves substantially.

## Emergent Abilities in LLMs

- secondary_domains: [llm-theory, ai-safety, empirical-ml]
- aliases: [emergent capabilities, LLM emergence, sharp capability transitions, emergent skills]
- broader: [llm-theory, llm-scaling]
- narrower: []
- related: [large-language-model-scaling-laws, phase-transitions-in-llms, grokking-phenomenon, mechanistic-interpretability]
- prerequisites: [large-language-models, scaling-laws, few-shot-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Emergent abilities in LLMs are capabilities that appear to be absent in smaller models and to arise sharply (without gradual transition) as model scale increases, first documented systematically by Wei et al. (2022) in the BIG-Bench benchmark. Examples include multi-step arithmetic, chain-of-thought reasoning, analogical reasoning, and certain few-shot learning tasks — skills that appear near-random below a scale threshold and then jump sharply to above-random performance. Emergence as a phenomenon is debated: Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued that emergence is partly an artefact of nonlinear metric choice, appearing smooth on continuous metrics but sharp on pass/fail benchmarks.

**key_claim**: The emergence vs. smooth-scaling debate reveals a fundamental measurement challenge in evaluating LLM capabilities — whether a capability appears to emerge sharply or to improve smoothly depends critically on the evaluation metric chosen; pass/fail metrics produce sharp apparent transitions from smooth underlying capability curves, which means that "emergent" abilities may reflect metric choice as much as genuine discontinuous capability acquisition.

**warning**: Emergence creates safety uncertainty that scaling laws cannot resolve — if capabilities genuinely emerge discontinuously at scale thresholds, a model trained just below an emergence threshold and scaled incrementally may acquire dangerous capabilities without the gradual safety monitoring that continuous capability growth would allow; this possibility motivates careful safety evaluation at each scale increase rather than assuming linear extrapolation of safety properties.

## Grokking Phenomenon

- secondary_domains: [neural-network-theory, deep-learning, overfitting]
- aliases: [delayed generalisation, grokking, slow generalisation after memorisation, phase transition in learning]
- broader: [neural-network-learning-dynamics, llm-theory]
- narrower: []
- related: [double-descent-in-neural-networks, phase-transitions-in-llms, mechanistic-interpretability, circuit-analysis-in-transformers]
- prerequisites: [neural-network-training, generalisation-theory, deep-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Grokking is a neural network learning phenomenon where a model first memorises training data (achieving near-zero training loss but poor generalisation) and then, long after apparent convergence, suddenly transitions to true generalisation (good test performance) without any change in training procedure. First documented by Power et al. (2022) at OpenAI on small modular arithmetic tasks, grokking shows that the traditional heuristic of stopping training when training loss plateaus may be premature — models can achieve dramatically better generalisation with additional training beyond apparent convergence. The phenomenon is most pronounced in regularised models training on algorithmic tasks.

**key_claim**: Grokking challenges the conventional understanding of neural network training dynamics by demonstrating that apparent convergence does not imply completion — the network is undergoing a slow, internally reorganising process during the apparent plateau phase where it transitions from a memorisation-based solution to a generalisation-based solution, and stopping training at apparent convergence misses this reorganisation; interpretability studies show that grokking corresponds to the emergence of clean algorithmic circuits in the network.

**warning**: Grokking phenomena in real-world LLMs are not as clear-cut as in the controlled toy settings where they were first documented — the training conditions (large data, diverse tasks, complex architectures) of practical LLMs may obscure or suppress the sharp grokking transition, and drawing direct operational implications from toy grokking experiments for large model training requires careful empirical validation.

## Double Descent in Neural Networks

- secondary_domains: [neural-network-theory, statistical-learning-theory, overfitting]
- aliases: [double descent, modern bias-variance tradeoff, interpolation threshold, epoch-wise double descent]
- broader: [neural-network-learning-dynamics, statistical-learning-theory]
- narrower: []
- related: [grokking-phenomenon, large-language-model-scaling-laws, phase-transitions-in-llms]
- prerequisites: [statistical-learning-theory, neural-network-training, bias-variance-tradeoff]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Double descent is a phenomenon in neural network training and statistical learning where the test error follows a non-monotonic path as model capacity increases: error first decreases (classical regime), then increases sharply to a peak near the interpolation threshold (where the model exactly fits training data), and then decreases again into the over-parameterised regime as model size grows further. Described by Belkin et al. (2019) and Nakkiran et al. (2021), double descent challenges the classical bias-variance tradeoff intuition (more capacity → worse generalisation after the sweet spot) by showing that sufficiently overparameterised models can generalise well despite memorising training data. The phenomenon applies to both model size (more parameters) and training epochs (more training).

**key_claim**: Double descent resolves the paradox of why larger neural networks often generalise better than classical statistical theory predicts — the interpolation threshold is a critical transition where models generalise poorly by memorising without structure, but models that far exceed this threshold are implicitly regularised by the optimisation dynamics toward solutions that generalise better; this explains why training the largest available models is often optimal even when smaller models seem sufficient by capacity-based intuitions.

**warning**: The epoch-wise double descent (where test loss spikes at intermediate training times before decreasing again) implies that early stopping based on validation performance can prematurely stop training during the peak of the double descent curve, settling at a worse-generalising solution than would be achieved by continuing through the peak; validation-based early stopping should be applied carefully and with awareness that temporary validation loss increases may precede better final generalisation.

## Phase Transitions in LLMs

- secondary_domains: [llm-theory, statistical-physics, empirical-ml]
- aliases: [phase transitions, capability discontinuities, sharp learning transitions, LLM phase changes]
- broader: [llm-theory, scaling-laws]
- narrower: []
- related: [large-language-model-scaling-laws, emergent-abilities-in-llms, grokking-phenomenon, double-descent-in-neural-networks]
- prerequisites: [large-language-models, scaling-laws, statistical-physics-analogies]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Phase transitions in LLMs refer to sharp, nonlinear capability changes that occur at specific scale thresholds — analogous to physical phase transitions (ice melting, magnetisation onset) — where small additional increases in model scale, training data, or compute produce qualitative rather than quantitative changes in model behaviour. Observed phase transitions include: sudden acquisition of in-context learning capability, sharp onset of chain-of-thought reasoning ability, and emergence of calibrated uncertainty estimates. The physical analogy is apt: just as water at 99°C and 101°C behave qualitatively differently despite a small temperature difference, language models at slightly different scales can exhibit qualitatively different capabilities.

**key_claim**: Phase transitions in LLMs represent a fundamental challenge to smooth extrapolation-based capability forecasting — if capability acquisition is discontinuous, then projections from lower-scale performance measurements will systematically underestimate capabilities at higher scale, and safety evaluations performed at intermediate scales may not capture the full capability profile of production-scale models.

**warning**: The physical phase transition analogy, while instructive, may be misleading if taken too literally — unlike physical systems where phase transitions are thermodynamic properties of the material, LLM phase transitions depend on evaluation methodology, training procedure, architecture, and data distribution; small changes in these variables can shift the apparent threshold, making phase transitions less predictable and more context-dependent than the analogy suggests.

## Mechanistic Interpretability

- secondary_domains: [ai-interpretability, deep-learning, ai-safety]
- aliases: [mech interp, mechanistic understanding, reverse engineering neural networks, circuit-level interpretability]
- broader: [ai-interpretability, ai-safety]
- narrower: [circuit-analysis-in-transformers, superposition-hypothesis, activation-patching, sparse-autoencoders-for-interpretability]
- related: [circuit-analysis-in-transformers, superposition-hypothesis, activation-patching, sparse-autoencoders-for-interpretability, grokking-phenomenon]
- prerequisites: [neural-networks, transformer-architecture, linear-algebra, ai-interpretability]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Mechanistic interpretability is the research programme that aims to reverse-engineer the algorithms implemented by neural networks — understanding not just what a model can do, but how it does it, by identifying specific computational structures (circuits, features, attention heads) that implement particular behaviours. Pioneered by Chris Olah at Google Brain/Anthropic and operationalised via the circuits hypothesis, mechanistic interpretability attempts to decompose model computations into human-understandable functional units, akin to understanding a program by reading its source code rather than by observing its input-output behaviour. Key techniques include activation patching, probing, sparse autoencoder feature extraction, and attention head analysis.

**key_claim**: Mechanistic interpretability is the only interpretability approach with a plausible path to providing safety-relevant understanding — post-hoc explanation methods (LIME, SHAP, saliency maps) describe correlations in model behaviour without revealing the underlying computational mechanism, making them unable to predict behaviour in novel contexts; mechanistic interpretability, by identifying the actual algorithm, can predict model behaviour in situations not seen during evaluation, including adversarial scenarios.

**warning**: Mechanistic interpretability currently scales poorly to frontier-sized models — the techniques that successfully identify clean circuits in toy models and small transformers (GPT-2 scale) become intractable for models with hundreds of billions of parameters and trillions of features; current mechanistic insights come primarily from small, controlled models, and the extent to which those insights generalise to production-scale models is an open empirical question.

## Superposition Hypothesis

- secondary_domains: [mechanistic-interpretability, ai-interpretability, neural-network-theory]
- aliases: [feature superposition, polysemanticity, superposition in neural networks, compressed feature representation]
- broader: [mechanistic-interpretability]
- narrower: []
- related: [mechanistic-interpretability, sparse-autoencoders-for-interpretability, circuit-analysis-in-transformers, activation-patching]
- prerequisites: [mechanistic-interpretability, neural-network-representations, linear-algebra]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The superposition hypothesis, developed by Elhage et al. (2022) at Anthropic, proposes that neural networks represent more features than they have neurons by storing multiple features in superposition — each neuron participates in representing multiple features, and each feature is distributed across multiple neurons — through sparse, nearly-orthogonal directions in the activation space. The hypothesis explains why neurons are polysemantic (responding to multiple unrelated inputs) and why model representations are difficult to interpret at the individual neuron level. Superposition is optimal when features are sparse (rarely active simultaneously), because the cost of occasional interference is outweighed by the benefit of representing exponentially more features.

**key_claim**: The superposition hypothesis explains why naive mechanistic interpretability (assigning a semantic meaning to individual neurons) fails for real models — neurons are not the correct unit of analysis because the model uses the full high-dimensional activation space to represent features via superposition; the practical implication is that interpretability techniques must operate in activation space (identifying feature directions) rather than neuron space.

**warning**: The superposition hypothesis is an empirical finding from small, idealised models and a theoretical framework, not a proven theorem about arbitrary neural networks — while the evidence is strong and the framework is influential, the extent to which frontier-scale models rely on superposition versus other representation strategies is not fully characterised; interpretability conclusions derived from the superposition framework should be held tentatively and validated empirically.

## Circuit Analysis in Transformers

- secondary_domains: [mechanistic-interpretability, transformer-architecture, ai-interpretability]
- aliases: [circuits analysis, attention circuit analysis, transformer circuit tracing, induction circuits]
- broader: [mechanistic-interpretability]
- narrower: [induction-circuits, indirect-object-identification-circuit]
- related: [mechanistic-interpretability, superposition-hypothesis, activation-patching, grokking-phenomenon]
- prerequisites: [mechanistic-interpretability, transformer-architecture, attention-mechanism]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Circuit analysis in transformers is the mechanistic interpretability approach of identifying and characterising the subgraphs of the transformer computation graph (involving specific attention heads, MLP neurons, and their interactions) that implement a particular input-output behaviour — called circuits. A circuit is a minimal set of model components that, working together, produce a specific capability; removing circuit components ablates the capability, while preserving them suffices to reproduce it. Identified circuits include the induction circuit (responsible for in-context pattern completion), the indirect object identification circuit (responsible for determining which name a pronoun refers to), and various factual recall circuits.

**key_claim**: Circuit analysis has revealed that specific, interpretable algorithms are actually implemented in transformer weights — the induction circuit, comprising a specific pair of attention head types working together to copy repeated patterns, is one of the cleanest examples of an identified algorithm that is simple enough to be fully understood and is found in essentially all transformer models above a minimal scale; this demonstrates that at least some transformer capabilities are implemented as structured, comprehensible algorithms rather than inscrutable numerical patterns.

**warning**: Circuit analysis is labour-intensive and currently relies on manual hypothesis formation and mechanistic validation — identifying a single clean circuit in a small model requires significant research effort, and circuits become increasingly complex and entangled at scale; the feasibility of circuit-level understanding for all safety-relevant capabilities in frontier models is a major open question in the interpretability research programme.

## Activation Patching

- secondary_domains: [mechanistic-interpretability, causal-inference, ai-interpretability]
- aliases: [causal tracing, activation intervention, causal patching, path patching]
- broader: [mechanistic-interpretability]
- narrower: [path-patching, iterative-activation-patching]
- related: [circuit-analysis-in-transformers, mechanistic-interpretability, superposition-hypothesis]
- prerequisites: [mechanistic-interpretability, causal-inference, transformer-architecture]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Activation patching (also called causal tracing or causal mediation analysis) is a mechanistic interpretability technique that identifies which model components are causally responsible for a specific output by interventionally replacing activation values from one forward pass into another. The technique runs a clean forward pass (correct behaviour) and a corrupted forward pass (incorrect behaviour created by altering the input), then systematically patches individual components' activations from the clean run into the corrupted run, measuring how much each patch restores the correct output; high restoration upon patching indicates the component is causally important. Used by Meng et al. (2022) in ROME to identify factual recall mechanisms in GPT-J.

**key_claim**: Activation patching provides causal evidence about model mechanisms that purely correlational probing techniques cannot — while probing identifies where information is encoded, patching demonstrates that the information at that location causally influences the output, distinguishing incidental encoding (information is present but not used for this computation) from functionally relevant encoding (information is actively used to produce the output).

**warning**: Activation patching results are sensitive to the choice of corruption mechanism — the corrupted baseline must produce meaningfully different behaviour to make the patching comparison informative, but overly aggressive corruption (destroying all information) can cause spurious attributions by creating activations that fall far outside the distribution on which the network was trained; the choice of corruption baseline should be theoretically motivated for each analysis task.

## Sparse Autoencoders for Interpretability

- secondary_domains: [mechanistic-interpretability, deep-learning, unsupervised-learning]
- aliases: [SAEs, dictionary learning for LLMs, sparse dictionary learning, mechanistic SAE, Anthropic sparse autoencoders]
- broader: [mechanistic-interpretability, sparse-representations]
- narrower: []
- related: [mechanistic-interpretability, superposition-hypothesis, circuit-analysis-in-transformers, activation-patching]
- prerequisites: [mechanistic-interpretability, sparse-autoencoders, superposition-hypothesis, dictionary-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) for interpretability are a mechanistic interpretability technique that trains a sparse autoencoder on LLM activation vectors to decompose them into a learned dictionary of sparse, monosemantic features — addressing the superposition problem by mapping polysemantic neuron activations to a larger set of sparse, interpretable feature directions. The SAE learns to reconstruct activations as sparse linear combinations of dictionary elements (features), each of which ideally corresponds to a human-interpretable concept (e.g., "references to DNA", "negative sentiment", "the word Python"). Developed and scaled by Anthropic's interpretability team, SAEs have been applied to identify millions of features in Claude Sonnet, including a famous "banana" feature and unexpected multimodal concept features.

**key_claim**: Sparse autoencoders for interpretability represent the most scalable current approach to the superposition problem — rather than requiring manual circuit analysis for each capability, SAEs can be trained to automatically extract the feature dictionary of an entire model layer, providing a principled and automated method to identify the semantic features that the model has learned to represent; the challenge is that millions of extracted features require automated interpretation methods rather than human review.

**warning**: SAE features may not correspond cleanly to the model's actual computational primitives — while SAE features are more monosemantic than raw neurons, there is no guarantee that the SAE decomposition reflects the model's true internal representation structure rather than a compressed approximation that captures correlations; features that appear monosemantic in isolated analysis may participate in circuits in ways that do not match their semantic label, and the theoretical relationship between SAE features and mechanistic circuits remains an active research question.
