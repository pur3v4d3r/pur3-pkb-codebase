# Batch: PKM, Pedagogy & Social-Science Gaps

## Knowledge Management

- domain: information-science
- secondary_domains: [organizational-psychology, personal-knowledge-management]
- aliases: [KM, organisational knowledge management]
- broader: [information-science]
- related: [personal-knowledge-management, zettelkasten, communities-of-practice, transactive-memory-systems, tacit-knowledge]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Knowledge Management is the field of practice and research concerned with how organisations and individuals capture, organise, retrieve, share, and apply knowledge so that it remains usable and improvable over time, integrating the people, processes, and technology dimensions of knowledge work.

**key_claim**: Knowledge Management distinguishes explicit from tacit knowledge and recognises that the most valuable organisational knowledge is tacit; this is why Knowledge Management programs that focus exclusively on document repositories systematically underperform programs that combine repositories with communities of practice and apprenticeship structures.

**warning**: Knowledge Management is frequently reduced to "buy a document management system", but the empirical track record of pure-tooling Knowledge Management initiatives is poor; serious Knowledge Management treats culture, incentives, and tacit-to-explicit conversion as first-class concerns alongside the tooling.

## Zettelkasten Workflow

- domain: personal-knowledge-management
- secondary_domains: [knowledge-management, learning-science]
- aliases: [Zettelkasten method, slip-box workflow]
- broader: [zettelkasten]
- related: [zettelkasten, atomic-notes, evergreen-notes, fleeting-notes, literature-notes, permanent-notes]
- prerequisites: [zettelkasten]
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Zettelkasten Workflow is the operational sequence by which raw inputs — readings, conversations, observations — are processed into atomic, linked, durable notes through the canonical stages of fleeting capture, literature notes, permanent atomic notes, and structural index notes.

**key_claim**: The Zettelkasten Workflow distinguishes itself from generic note-taking by enforcing atomicity (one idea per note), durability (notes are written in the author's own words), and connectivity (every new note must link into the existing network), which is what produces the compounding intellectual value Luhmann's slip-box famously demonstrated.

**warning**: A Zettelkasten Workflow is frequently confused with the use of a particular tool such as Obsidian or Roam; the tool is incidental, the workflow is what produces the effect, and a sophisticated tool used without the Zettelkasten Workflow discipline merely accelerates the production of low-quality notes.

## Cultural Psychology

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [cross-cultural-psychology, anthropology]
- aliases: [cultural psychology of mind]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [cross-cultural-psychology, social-cognition, self-construal, weird-samples-problem, individualism-collectivism]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cultural Psychology is the branch of psychology that treats culture and mind as mutually constitutive, holding that psychological processes are shaped by, and themselves shape, the cultural meanings, practices, and institutions in which they develop, contrasting with cross-cultural psychology's comparative stance.

**key_claim**: Cultural Psychology has documented that many phenomena once treated as universal — fundamental attribution error, dissonance reduction, self-enhancement, even some perceptual effects — vary systematically with cultural context, which is why the WEIRD-samples critique of psychology was largely articulated from within Cultural Psychology.

**warning**: Cultural Psychology is sometimes read as licensing a soft cultural relativism, but the field's stronger and more interesting claim is that cultural variation provides the comparative data needed to specify which mechanisms are truly universal; treating Cultural Psychology as "everything is just culture" misses its scientific contribution.

## Social Cognition

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, social-psychology]
- aliases: [social-cognitive psychology]
- broader: [cognitive-psychology]
- related: [theory-of-mind, attribution-theory, social-cognitive-theory, schema-theory, dual-process-theory]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Social Cognition is the branch of psychology that studies how people perceive, encode, store, retrieve, and use information about themselves, other people, and social situations, applying the conceptual and methodological tools of cognitive psychology to the social domain.

**key_claim**: Social Cognition has shown that ordinary social judgement relies heavily on schema-driven, dual-process inference — fast, automatic associations supplemented by slower deliberative correction — which is why Social Cognition has become the dominant theoretical lens in modern social psychology.

**warning**: Social Cognition is often equated with the study of cognitive biases in social judgement, but the field is much broader, and reducing Social Cognition to a catalogue of biases misses its central project of explaining the architecture that produces both ordinary accurate social inference and its characteristic failures.

## Theory of Planned Behavior

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [social-psychology, health-psychology]
- aliases: [TPB, Ajzen TPB]
- broader: [behaviour-change-theory]
- related: [theory-of-reasoned-action, implementation-intentions, stages-of-change-model, attitude-behaviour-gap, perceived-behavioural-control]
- prerequisites: [motivation-theory, attitude]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Theory of Planned Behavior is Icek Ajzen's social-cognitive model in which intention to perform a behaviour is determined by attitudes toward the behaviour, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control, with intention together with perceived behavioural control predicting actual behaviour.

**key_claim**: The Theory of Planned Behavior remains the most widely cited social-cognitive model of behaviour change because it accommodates volitional behaviours by adding perceived behavioural control to its predecessor (the theory of reasoned action), and it has produced thousands of empirical applications across health, environment, and consumer domains.

**warning**: The Theory of Planned Behavior predicts intention more strongly than it predicts behaviour, leaving an "intention-behaviour gap" that the Theory of Planned Behavior itself does not adequately model; using the Theory of Planned Behavior to design behaviour-change interventions without complementary models such as implementation intentions systematically underperforms.

## Stages of Change Model

- domain: clinical-psychology
- secondary_domains: [health-psychology, motivational-psychology]
- aliases: [transtheoretical model, TTM, Prochaska-DiClemente stages]
- broader: [behaviour-change-theory]
- related: [theory-of-planned-behavior, motivational-interviewing, decisional-balance, self-efficacy]
- prerequisites: [behaviour-change-theory]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: The Stages of Change Model — formally the Transtheoretical Model — is Prochaska and DiClemente's framework that classifies a person's progress toward behavioural change into five stages: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance, with characteristic processes of change at each stage.

**key_claim**: The Stages of Change Model has been highly influential in clinical and public-health practice because it suggests stage-matched interventions — different processes for someone in precontemplation than for someone in maintenance — and the Stages of Change Model thereby reframes "non-compliance" as stage-mismatched intervention design.

**warning**: The Stages of Change Model has been criticised on empirical grounds: the discrete stages may be a convenient classification rather than genuine qualitative shifts, and stage-matched interventions have produced inconsistent effects in randomised trials, so the Stages of Change Model is best treated as a heuristic organiser rather than a validated mechanistic theory.

## Task Analysis

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, human-factors]
- aliases: [cognitive task analysis, hierarchical task analysis]
- broader: [instructional-design]
- related: [cognitive-task-analysis, four-component-instructional-design-model-4cid, expert-knowledge-elicitation, scaffolding]
- prerequisites: [instructional-design]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Task Analysis is the systematic decomposition of a target performance into its constituent goals, sub-goals, knowledge requirements, and actions, performed prior to instructional design so that learning materials and assessments target the cognitive structures the performance actually requires.

**key_claim**: Task Analysis is the empirically essential precondition of effective instructional design — most prominently in the 4C/ID model — because instruction designed without Task Analysis tends to teach the surface features of the performance rather than the underlying cognitive operations.

**warning**: Task Analysis is most informative for routine, well-structured tasks; for ill-structured creative or strategic work the decomposition itself becomes a research project, and naive Task Analysis can produce instruction that drills atomic sub-skills the expert never decomposes that way in practice.

## Strategy Instruction

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [self-regulated-learning, metacognition]
- aliases: [learning-strategy instruction, cognitive strategy instruction]
- broader: [instructional-design]
- related: [self-regulated-learning, metacognitive-strategy, cognitive-strategy-use, scaffolding, reciprocal-teaching]
- prerequisites: [self-regulated-learning, metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Strategy Instruction is the explicit teaching of cognitive and metacognitive learning strategies — summarisation, self-questioning, elaborative interrogation, planning, monitoring, evaluating — together with the conditional knowledge of when and why to apply each one, so that learners can deploy the strategies on their own across tasks.

**key_claim**: Strategy Instruction produces durable transfer only when the conditional ("when and why") knowledge is taught alongside the procedural knowledge of how to execute the strategy; Strategy Instruction limited to procedure produces strategies the learner uses only in the original task context.

**warning**: Strategy Instruction is sometimes reduced to handing learners a strategy checklist, but the meta-analytic evidence shows that effective Strategy Instruction requires modelling, scaffolded practice, and metacognitive reflection on strategy fit, so checklist-only Strategy Instruction systematically under-delivers on its promise.

## Summarization

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [reading-research, learning-strategies]
- aliases: [summarisation, summary writing as a study strategy]
- broader: [learning-strategies]
- related: [self-explanation, elaborative-interrogation, retrieval-practice, comprehension-monitoring, reading-comprehension]
- prerequisites: [learning-strategies, reading-comprehension]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Summarization is the learning strategy in which a student produces a condensed restatement of studied material in their own words, requiring selection of main ideas, deletion of trivial detail, generalisation across specific instances, and integration into a coherent reduced text.

**key_claim**: Summarization can be a moderately effective study strategy when learners have been explicitly trained in the constituent operations, but in the Dunlosky et al. evidence-based ranking of study strategies Summarization receives only a "low utility" rating because untrained Summarization frequently produces verbatim copying or random extraction.

**warning**: Summarization is a deceptive case in the learning-strategies literature: students subjectively feel that Summarization is producing learning, while objective measures show weak gains compared with retrieval practice; conflating the felt productivity of Summarization with the actual learning gain is a textbook fluency illusion.

## Test Anxiety

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [clinical-psychology, achievement-emotions]
- aliases: [evaluative anxiety, exam anxiety]
- broader: [achievement-emotions]
- related: [cognitive-load-theory, working-memory, achievement-emotions, attentional-control-theory, control-value-theory]
- prerequisites: [achievement-emotions, working-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Test Anxiety is the multi-component aversive state — combining cognitive worry, physiological arousal, and behavioural avoidance — that some learners experience in evaluative situations, and it is one of the most studied negative achievement emotions in educational psychology.

**key_claim**: Test Anxiety harms performance primarily through its cognitive worry component, which consumes working-memory capacity that the task itself requires, so the well-documented Test Anxiety performance decrement is largest on cognitively demanding tasks and small or absent on low-load tasks.

**warning**: Test Anxiety is sometimes treated as a global personality trait, but contemporary work decomposes Test Anxiety into worry, emotionality, and avoidance components with different antecedents and different intervention targets; treating Test Anxiety as a single variable misses the design lever each component provides.

## Guided Discovery

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, cognitive-load-theory]
- aliases: [guided inquiry, scaffolded discovery]
- broader: [instructional-strategy]
- related: [discovery-learning, direct-instruction, scaffolding, worked-examples, expertise-reversal-effect, problem-based-learning]
- prerequisites: [instructional-design]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Guided Discovery is the instructional strategy in which learners are positioned to discover principles, patterns, or solutions for themselves but with deliberate instructional scaffolding — prompts, hints, partially worked examples, structured questioning — that channels their exploration toward the target understanding.

**key_claim**: Guided Discovery occupies the empirically defensible middle ground in the long-running discovery-vs-direct-instruction debate; pure discovery learning routinely underperforms because of cognitive overload, while Guided Discovery preserves the motivational and transfer benefits of discovery without imposing the load.

**warning**: Guided Discovery is a moving target whose effectiveness depends sensitively on prior knowledge: as learners' expertise grows, the optimal level of guidance drops (the expertise reversal effect), so an instructional design that is appropriate Guided Discovery for novices becomes counterproductive over-scaffolding for experts.

## Lifespan Development

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [psychology, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [lifespan developmental psychology, life-span development]
- broader: [developmental-psychology]
- related: [developmental-psychology, adult-development, baltes-lifespan-theory, gain-loss-dynamics, plasticity]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Lifespan Development is the branch of developmental psychology, articulated most influentially by Paul Baltes and colleagues, that studies psychological change across the entire lifespan from infancy to old age, treating development as a continuous gain-and-loss dynamic rather than a process that ends in early adulthood.

**key_claim**: Lifespan Development reframes adulthood and ageing as periods of genuine developmental change — not merely decline — by showing that across every life phase there is co-occurring growth, maintenance, and loss, and that the relative balance shifts but the developmental dynamic continues.

**warning**: Lifespan Development is sometimes flattened into either a "stages of life" narrative or a "decline-and-loss" narrative, but the field's distinctive contribution is exactly the rejection of both flattenings; treating Lifespan Development as a list of life stages obscures the multidirectional, multidimensional, and contextual nature of the actual evidence.
