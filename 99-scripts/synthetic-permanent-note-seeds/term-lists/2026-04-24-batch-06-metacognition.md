# Batch: Metacognition & Self-Regulation

## Metacognitive Self-Regulation

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [self-regulation, learning-science]
- aliases: [metacognitive SRL]
- broader: [self-regulated-learning, metacognition]
- related: [metacognitive-regulation, [[zimmerman-srl-model], [[forethought-phase], performance-phase, self-reflection-phase]
- prerequisites: [self-regulated-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Metacognitive Self-Regulation is the metacognition-driven subset of self-regulated learning — the deliberate planning, monitoring, controlling, and reflecting on one's own cognitive processes during learning — and is the executive arm through which knowledge of cognition (metacognitive knowledge) is converted into adjusted behaviour during study and performance.

**key_claim**: Metacognitive Self-Regulation is the engine that distinguishes effective self-directed learners from those who execute study-strategy lists mechanically: it is the moment-to-moment adjustment — re-reading the section that was confusing, switching strategies when one isn't working, allocating extra time to weak areas — that converts knowing about strategies into actually deploying them adaptively.

**warning**: Metacognitive Self-Regulation depends on accurate metacognitive monitoring, and learners who are systematically miscalibrated (over- or under-confident) self-regulate badly even when they intend well; pedagogy must therefore target *monitoring accuracy* before or alongside regulation strategies, because regulation built on bad monitoring directs effort to the wrong places.

## Metacognitive Regulation

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [self-regulation, learning-science]
- aliases: [regulation of cognition]
- broader: [metacognition]
- related: [[metacognitive-knowledge], metacognitive-self-regulation, [[planning-monitoring-evaluating]], metacognitive-calibration]
- prerequisites: [metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Metacognitive Regulation, in Schraw and Moshman's framework, is one of the two components of metacognition (alongside metacognitive knowledge) and consists of the active processes of *planning* (selecting strategies, allocating resources), *monitoring* (tracking comprehension and progress), and *evaluating* (judging the products and processes of learning).

**key_claim**: Metacognitive Regulation is more responsive to instruction than metacognitive knowledge — learners can be taught planning, monitoring, and evaluation routines through structured practice and explicit prompting — making it a higher-leverage instructional target than waiting for declarative metacognitive knowledge to translate spontaneously into regulated behaviour.

**warning**: Metacognitive Regulation can become routinised in ways that defeat its purpose: a learner who mechanically completes a planning template, a monitoring checklist, and an evaluation rubric without actually engaging with the questions has performed Metacognitive Regulation in form but not in substance, and the resulting practice can produce documentation without insight.

## Metacognitive Feelings

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, memory-research]
- aliases: [feelings of knowing, fluency feelings]
- broader: [metacognition, metacognitive-experience]
- related: [[feeling-of-knowing], [[tip-of-the-tongue], metacognitive-calibration, [[fluency-as-cue]]]
- prerequisites: [metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Metacognitive Feelings are the fast, often non-conscious affective signals that inform metacognitive judgements — the feeling of knowing, the feeling of familiarity, the feeling of difficulty, perceptual and processing fluency — and are the substrate from which deliberate metacognitive judgements are typically constructed.

**key_claim**: Metacognitive Feelings rest substantially on processing fluency: material that is processed easily produces feelings of knowing and confidence, regardless of whether the underlying knowledge is accurate, which is why presentation manipulations (clearer fonts, easier examples) can inflate confidence judgements without improving learning outcomes.

**warning**: Metacognitive Feelings are systematically misleading in pedagogically important ways: re-reading produces a feeling of knowing that does not predict retrieval success, fluent examples produce illusions of comprehension, and confidently-experienced familiarity can co-exist with no usable knowledge — so pedagogy must teach learners to triangulate Metacognitive Feelings against retrieval-based evidence rather than trusting them.

## Metacognitive Calibration

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [judgement-and-decision-making, learning-science]
- aliases: [confidence calibration, calibration of metacognition]
- broader: [metacognition]
- related: [metacognitive-judgments, [[overconfidence], [[underconfidence], dunning-kruger-effect]
- prerequisites: [metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Metacognitive Calibration is the alignment between a learner's confidence in their knowledge and the actual accuracy of that knowledge — well-calibrated learners are confident when correct and uncertain when wrong — and is measured through correlations between item-level confidence judgements and item-level accuracy.

**key_claim**: Metacognitive Calibration is the binding constraint on effective self-regulated learning: a learner cannot allocate study time efficiently to weak areas if they cannot tell which areas are weak, and the well-documented overconfidence patterns (especially among lower-performing learners) explain a large portion of why learners often fail to improve despite available study time.

**warning**: Metacognitive Calibration can be improved through specific practices — receiving accuracy feedback after confidence judgements, generating retrieval attempts before judging knowing, comparing predicted vs. actual performance — but these practices are rarely embedded in standard instruction; learners left to their own devices typically remain miscalibrated, so explicit calibration training is needed.

## Metacognitive Judgments

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [memory-research, learning-science]
- aliases: [JOLs, judgments of learning, ease-of-learning judgments]
- broader: [metacognition]
- related: [[ease-of-learning], [[judgements-of-learning], [[retrospective-confidence], metacognitive-feelings, metacognitive-calibration]
- prerequisites: [metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Metacognitive Judgments are the explicit assessments learners make about their own cognitive states — judgments of learning (will I remember this later?), feelings of knowing (could I retrieve this if cued?), retrospective confidence (was that answer right?), and ease-of-learning judgements (how hard will this be?) — and serve as inputs to metacognitive regulation.

**key_claim**: Metacognitive Judgments are constructed in real time from cues (fluency, familiarity, partial retrieval) rather than being read off some internal knowledge meter, which means their accuracy depends on the diagnostic value of the available cues — and explains why judgements made immediately after study (when fluency cues dominate) are typically less accurate than judgements made after a delay (when retrieval-based cues become available).

**warning**: Metacognitive Judgments are widely used in self-regulated learning research as proxies for metacognitive monitoring, but their predictive validity for actual recall is modest at best in many studies; consumers of this literature should recognise that "improving JOLs" is not the same as "improving learning outcomes," and the linkage between judgement accuracy and behavioural change remains the practical bottleneck.

## Metacognitive Experience

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [Flavell metacognitive experiences]
- broader: [metacognition]
- narrower: [metacognitive-feelings, metacognitive-judgments]
- related: [[metacognitive-knowledge], [[flavell], [[task-difficulty-experience]]]
- prerequisites: [metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Metacognitive Experience, in Flavell's foundational tripartite framework, is the conscious cognitive or affective experience that accompanies and pertains to an intellectual enterprise — feelings, judgements, perceptions of difficulty or fluency — and is one of the three components alongside metacognitive knowledge and metacognitive strategies.

**key_claim**: Metacognitive Experience is the most pedagogically tractable of Flavell's three components because it occurs in real time during task performance and can be directly probed and trained: prompting learners to report their experience of difficulty, confidence, or confusion at structured moments creates the substrate on which monitoring and regulation can be deliberately trained.

**warning**: Metacognitive Experience is often confused with Metacognitive Knowledge in casual writing about metacognition, but Flavell's distinction matters: a learner can have rich Metacognitive Knowledge ("I know I have trouble with statistics") without the in-the-moment Metacognitive Experience that flags a specific problem ("this current calculation feels wrong") — and behaviour change requires the latter, not just the former.

## Dunning-Kruger Effect

- domain: metacognition-research
- secondary_domains: [judgement-and-decision-making, social-psychology]
- aliases: [DK effect]
- broader: [metacognitive-calibration]
- related: [[overconfidence], [[unskilled-and-unaware], metacognitive-judgments, [[double-curse]]]
- prerequisites: [metacognitive-calibration]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Dunning-Kruger Effect, as published by Kruger and Dunning (1999), is the empirical finding that people in the lowest performance quartile of a domain systematically overestimate their performance, while top performers tend to slightly underestimate, producing a characteristic gap that the original authors framed as a "double curse" of incompetence and unawareness of incompetence.

**key_claim**: The Dunning-Kruger Effect's pedagogical implication is that the learners least equipped to identify their own errors are the ones most in need of doing so, which means external diagnostic feedback (well-designed assessments, criterion-referenced rubrics, peer comparison with proper calibration support) is structurally necessary — self-assessment cannot be the primary feedback channel for novices.

**warning**: The Dunning-Kruger Effect has been heavily over-stated and partly mis-explained in popular discourse — the original effect size is modest, much of the apparent pattern in the original data is driven by regression to the mean and ceiling/floor effects (Nuhfer et al., Gignac & Zajenkowski), and confident incompetents are not as systematically distinct from confident competents as the meme suggests; cite the underlying research rather than the popular caricature.

## Performance Phase

- domain: self-regulated-learning
- secondary_domains: [metacognition-research, learning-science]
- aliases: [Zimmerman performance phase, volitional control phase]
- broader: [self-regulated-learning, [[zimmerman-srl-model]]]
- related: [[forethought-phase], self-reflection-phase, [[self-control], [[self-observation], [[task-strategies]]]
- prerequisites: [self-regulated-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Performance Phase, in Zimmerman's three-phase model of self-regulated learning, is the phase in which the learner executes the planned approach while engaging in self-control (using attention focusing, task strategies, self-instruction, environmental structuring) and self-observation (monitoring performance through self-recording and metacognitive monitoring).

**key_claim**: The Performance Phase is where forethought either pays off or breaks down: well-formulated plans from the forethought phase still require deliberate enactment, monitoring, and adjustment in the Performance Phase, and learners with rich planning vocabularies but weak Performance Phase routines reliably fail to convert intentions into outcomes.

**warning**: The Performance Phase is the hardest phase to support remotely or asynchronously because it occurs *during* the work itself; instructional approaches that focus heavily on pre-task planning and post-task reflection but provide no scaffolding during the Performance Phase systematically miss the moment where most regulation actually happens or fails to happen.

## Self-Reflection Phase

- domain: self-regulated-learning
- secondary_domains: [metacognition-research, learning-science]
- aliases: [Zimmerman self-reflection phase]
- broader: [self-regulated-learning, [[zimmerman-srl-model]]]
- related: [[forethought-phase], performance-phase, [[self-judgment], [[self-reaction], [[causal-attribution]]]
- prerequisites: [self-regulated-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Self-Reflection Phase, in Zimmerman's three-phase SRL model, is the post-performance phase in which the learner makes self-judgments (evaluating performance against criteria, making causal attributions for outcomes) and self-reactions (affective response, adaptive vs. defensive inference for next-cycle planning), feeding back into the next forethought phase.

**key_claim**: The Self-Reflection Phase is the phase where the SRL cycle either closes productively or breaks: learners who attribute outcomes to controllable causes (effort, strategy) and respond adaptively (revising plan for next cycle) maintain self-regulation, while learners who attribute outcomes to stable uncontrollable causes (ability, luck) and respond defensively (avoidance, learned helplessness) exit the cycle.

**warning**: The Self-Reflection Phase is often reduced to a written reflection assignment that learners produce as performance for a teacher rather than as genuine self-evaluation; the structure that elicits authentic Self-Reflection is private, formative, and tied to actual next-cycle decisions, not graded reflective writing whose outputs predict grade rather than genuine reorganisation of the learner's approach.
