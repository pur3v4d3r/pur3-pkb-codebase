## Epistemic Internalism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, justification-theory]
- aliases: [internalism about justification]
- broader: [theories of justification]
- related: [epistemic externalism, internalism vs externalism epistemology]

**definition**: Epistemic Internalism is the family of views holding that whether a belief is justified is determined entirely by factors internal to the believer's mental life — typically the evidence, reasons, or reflectively accessible mental states the believer has.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Internalism preserves the intuition that justification is a matter the thinker can in principle assess from the inside, which makes justification an intellectually responsible status rather than something the believer might possess unknowingly.

**warning**: Epistemic Internalism is sometimes equated with Cartesian transparency about the mind; many internalist positions concede that the relevant internal states are not perfectly transparent, and the view does not require infallible self-knowledge.

## Epistemic Externalism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, justification-theory]
- aliases: [externalism about justification]
- broader: [theories of justification]
- related: [epistemic internalism, internalism vs externalism epistemology]

**definition**: Epistemic Externalism is the family of views holding that justification depends at least partly on factors external to the believer's mental life — for example, the reliability of the belief-forming process or the actual relation between the belief and its truth-maker.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Externalism allows for justified belief without reflective access to the justification, which is what enables externalist accounts to credit perceptual knowledge in animals, young children, and ordinary adults who cannot articulate why they know what they know.

**warning**: Epistemic Externalism is not the claim that internal factors are irrelevant; most externalist accounts include internal components, and the dispute concerns whether external factors are necessary or sufficient for justification, not whether they exhaust it.

## Internalism Vs Externalism Epistemology

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, justification-theory, meta-epistemology]
- aliases: [internalism-externalism debate, access debate]
- broader: [theories of justification]
- related: [epistemic internalism, epistemic externalism, epistemic responsibility]

**definition**: The Internalism Vs Externalism Epistemology debate concerns whether the factors that determine epistemic justification are confined to the believer's internal mental states or also include external conditions such as causal reliability.

**key_claim**: The Internalism Vs Externalism Epistemology debate is structurally important because it forces a choice between two attractive but incompatible commitments: that justification be reflectively available to the believer and that justification be reliably connected to truth.

**warning**: The Internalism Vs Externalism Epistemology debate is sometimes treated as a clean binary, but contemporary positions are typically hybrids; speaking as if every epistemologist must be a "pure" internalist or externalist misrepresents the actual landscape of the dispute.

## Epistemic Regress

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-justification, meta-epistemology]
- aliases: [regress of justification, justificatory regress]
- broader: [foundations of justification]
- related: [agrippa trilemma, epistemic closure]

**definition**: The Epistemic Regress is the problem that every justified belief seems to require justification by some further belief, which itself requires justification, and so on, raising the question of how the chain of reasons can ever terminate or be made non-vicious.

**key_claim**: The Epistemic Regress is the structural pressure point that organises the major theories of justificatory architecture; foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism are best understood as competing ways of refusing one or another of the regress's apparent options.

**warning**: The Epistemic Regress is sometimes presented as a sceptical argument, but the regress itself is neutral about scepticism; what determines the sceptical upshot is the further claim that none of the available responses to the regress succeeds, which is a separate and contestable thesis.

## Agrippa Trilemma

- secondary_domains: [history-of-philosophy, scepticism, meta-epistemology]
- aliases: [Agrippa's trilemma, Münchhausen trilemma, problem of the criterion]
- broader: [epistemic regress]
- related: [epistemic regress, epistemic relativism]
- prerequisites: [epistemic regress]

**definition**: The Agrippa Trilemma argues that any attempted justification of a belief must terminate in one of three unacceptable options: an infinite regress of further justifications, a circle in which premises ultimately depend on the conclusion, or an arbitrary stopping point asserted without further support.

**key_claim**: The Agrippa Trilemma exhausts the options for chain-structured justification, which means defending knowledge against scepticism requires either accepting one of the three horns as benign or rejecting the chain model of justification altogether.

**warning**: The Agrippa Trilemma should not be read as a knockdown sceptical argument; each horn has been defended by serious philosophers (infinitism, coherentism, foundationalism), and the trilemma's force is to constrain the form of any successful theory of justification, not to refute knowledge.

## Epistemic Closure

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, modal-epistemology]
- aliases: [closure principle, deductive closure of knowledge]
- broader: [structural principles of knowledge]
- related: [contextualism in epistemology, epistemic regress]

**definition**: Epistemic Closure is the principle that if one knows that p, and one competently deduces q from p, one is in a position to know that q; in stronger formulations, knowledge is closed under known logical entailment.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Closure is what makes knowledge transitive across reasoning steps; without it, even valid deductive inference from known premises would not extend knowledge, and the cognitive function of inference would be radically constrained.

**warning**: Epistemic Closure is the explicit target of several anti-sceptical strategies that deny it (e.g., Nozick's tracking account); the principle's intuitive appeal hides genuine technical costs, and accepting or rejecting it has substantial downstream consequences for sceptical arguments.

## Contextualism In Epistemology

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-language, semantics-of-knowledge-attributions]
- aliases: [epistemic contextualism]
- broader: [theories of knowledge attribution]
- related: [epistemic closure, epistemic relativism]

**definition**: Contextualism In Epistemology is the view that the truth-conditions of knowledge attributions vary with the context of the attributor — what counts as "knowing" in a low-stakes everyday context is more permissive than what counts as "knowing" in a high-stakes or sceptical context.

**key_claim**: Contextualism In Epistemology dissolves apparent contradictions between everyday knowledge claims and sceptical denials by relocating the variation: both speakers can be saying something true because the standards encoded in "knows" shift with context.

**warning**: Contextualism In Epistemology is frequently confused with subjectivism or relativism about knowledge itself; the view is semantic — about what knowledge attributions mean — and is consistent with knowledge being a fully objective relation once standards are fixed.

## Epistemic Relativism

- secondary_domains: [meta-epistemology, sociology-of-knowledge]
- aliases: [relativism about knowledge, framework relativism]
- broader: [meta-epistemological positions]
- related: [contextualism in epistemology, agrippa trilemma]

**definition**: Epistemic Relativism is the view that the standards by which beliefs are evaluated as justified or known are themselves relative to a framework, culture, paradigm, or perspective, with no framework-independent standpoint from which to adjudicate between them.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Relativism distinguishes itself from mere disagreement by denying that there is a neutral epistemic court of appeal; it therefore implicates not only what is known but the very standards by which knowing is assessed.

**warning**: Epistemic Relativism faces a well-known self-application problem; the thesis itself appears to be either framework-relative (in which case it has no claim on those who reject the framework) or framework-independent (in which case it refutes itself), and any defensible version must address this directly.

## A Posteriori Knowledge

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, modal-epistemology]
- aliases: [empirical knowledge, experience-dependent knowledge]
- broader: [kinds of knowledge]
- related: [analytic synthetic distinction, propositional knowledge]

**definition**: A Posteriori Knowledge is knowledge whose justification depends on experience — observation, perception, testimony, or empirical inference — as distinct from knowledge that can be justified independently of experience.

**key_claim**: A Posteriori Knowledge marks the dependence relation rather than the temporal sequence; a belief counts as a posteriori when its justification requires experiential input, even if the believer happens not to have consciously consulted experience in arriving at it.

**warning**: A Posteriori Knowledge is sometimes equated with empirical claims about the external world only, but a posteriori justification can also support claims about one's own mental states, mathematical regularities discovered by computation, and other content not normally classified as "external" empirical fact.

## Analytic Synthetic Distinction

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-language, history-of-philosophy]
- aliases: [analytic-synthetic distinction]
- broader: [classification of statements]
- related: [a posteriori knowledge, propositional knowledge]

**definition**: The Analytic Synthetic Distinction divides statements into those whose truth depends solely on the meanings of their constituent terms (analytic) and those whose truth depends on how the world is in addition to those meanings (synthetic).

**key_claim**: The Analytic Synthetic Distinction structured much of early twentieth-century philosophy by appearing to provide a sharp criterion for separating definitional from empirical claims; subsequent work, most famously Quine's, challenged whether the distinction can be drawn cleanly at all.

**warning**: The Analytic Synthetic Distinction should not be assumed to be uncontroversial; it has been defended, attacked, and reformulated repeatedly, and treating it as a neutral classification scheme obscures more than a century of dispute over its viability.

## Propositional Knowledge

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, semantics]
- aliases: [knowledge-that, declarative knowledge]
- broader: [kinds of knowledge]
- related: [a posteriori knowledge, analytic synthetic distinction, epistemic closure]

**definition**: Propositional Knowledge is knowledge of facts — knowledge that some proposition is the case — as distinct from knowledge how (skill) and knowledge by acquaintance (direct familiarity); it is the type of knowledge classically analysed as justified true belief.

**key_claim**: Propositional Knowledge is the central target of the analysis-of-knowledge tradition because it is the kind of knowledge expressible in declarative sentences, evaluable by truth, and amenable to articulated justification — the form on which most epistemic theorising operates.

**warning**: Propositional Knowledge is not the only or even the dominant form of human knowledge; treating it as the exhaustive category misses skill, perceptual familiarity, and tacit knowledge, all of which resist propositional reconstruction without significant loss.

## Epistemic Akrasia

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, virtue-epistemology]
- aliases: [epistemic weakness of will, doxastic akrasia]
- broader: [irrationality]
- related: [epistemic responsibility, epistemic humility]

**definition**: Epistemic Akrasia is the condition of believing that one ought, by one's own epistemic standards, to believe that p, and yet failing to believe p (or believing not-p), in apparent violation of one's higher-order judgement.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Akrasia is a structural challenge to the assumption that belief tracks reflective judgement; its existence shows that first-order belief and second-order assessment can come apart, even in the absence of any error at either level.

**warning**: Epistemic Akrasia is sometimes denied on the grounds that the akratic state is incoherent and therefore impossible; this is a substantive philosophical position, not an obvious truth, and dismissing the phenomenon a priori cuts off empirical and introspective evidence that the state actually occurs.

## Epistemic Trust

- secondary_domains: [social-epistemology, ethics-of-belief]
- aliases: [trust in testimony, doxastic trust]
- broader: [social epistemology]
- related: [intellectual autonomy, collective epistemology, epistemic responsibility]

**definition**: Epistemic Trust is the practice of accepting another agent's testimony as a basis for belief without independently verifying the asserted content, on the grounds of one's assessment of the testifier's reliability and sincerity.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Trust is not optional but constitutive of the human knowledge economy; almost everything any individual believes traces back through chains of testimony, so the question is not whether to trust but how to allocate trust calibratedly.

**warning**: Epistemic Trust can degrade into uncritical credulity or harden into blanket scepticism; both extremes evade the harder task of differential calibration, which is the only practice that actually distinguishes trustworthy from untrustworthy sources.

## Collective Epistemology

- secondary_domains: [social-epistemology, philosophy-of-science]
- aliases: [group epistemology, social epistemology of groups]
- broader: [social epistemology]
- related: [epistemic trust, epistemic responsibility]

**definition**: Collective Epistemology studies the epistemic states and practices of groups — what it means for a group to know, believe, or be justified in believing something — and how group-level epistemic properties relate to those of individual members.

**key_claim**: Collective Epistemology is necessary because group beliefs do not reduce straightforwardly to aggregations of individual beliefs; the same set of individual beliefs can produce different group-level positions depending on aggregation rules, which has substantive consequences for science, juries, and institutions.

**warning**: Collective Epistemology is sometimes dismissed as metaphorical talk about groups, but well-developed accounts treat group epistemic states as theoretically tractable in their own right, and refusing to do so makes large parts of scientific, judicial, and democratic practice opaque.

## Reflective Equilibrium

- secondary_domains: [methodology, ethics, political-philosophy]
- aliases: [Rawlsian reflective equilibrium]
- broader: [philosophical methodology]
- related: [coherence theory of truth, judiciousness]

**definition**: Reflective Equilibrium is a method of justification in which one moves back and forth between particular judgements, general principles, and background theories, adjusting each in light of the others until they form a mutually supporting coherent whole.

**key_claim**: Reflective Equilibrium replaces the foundationalist hope for a privileged starting point with a dynamic equilibrium between levels; justification becomes a property of the whole web of considered judgement and principle rather than a relation grounded in any fixed base.

**warning**: Reflective Equilibrium is sometimes mistaken for mere intuition-checking, but the method requires genuine willingness to revise particular judgements in the face of well-supported principles (and vice versa); used as a one-way filter that only adjusts principles to fit pre-existing intuitions, it collapses into rationalised intuitionism.
