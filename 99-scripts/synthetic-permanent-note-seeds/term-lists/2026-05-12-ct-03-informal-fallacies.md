## Hasty Generalization

- secondary_domains: [inductive-reasoning, statistics]
- aliases: [hasty generalisation, insufficient sample fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, inductive fallacies]
- related: [texas sharpshooter fallacy, neglect of probability]

**definition**: Hasty Generalization is the fallacy of drawing a sweeping conclusion about a population from a sample that is too small, unrepresentative, or biased to support the inference.

**key_claim**: Hasty Generalization fails not because generalisation is illegitimate — most useful knowledge is generalised — but because the inference outruns the evidential base; the question is always whether the sample warrants the scope of the conclusion.

**warning**: Hasty Generalization is sometimes diagnosed simply because the conclusion is general; the fallacy requires that the supporting sample be inadequate, so well-supported general claims are not hasty merely by virtue of being general.

## False Cause Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [causal-reasoning, scientific-reasoning]
- aliases: [non causa pro causa, post hoc ergo propter hoc, causal fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, causal fallacies]
- related: [hasty generalization, neglect of probability]

**definition**: The False Cause Fallacy is the error of inferring that one event caused another on the basis of insufficient evidence — typically mere temporal succession, correlation, or salience — rather than a genuine causal connection.

**key_claim**: The False Cause Fallacy is structurally common because the human mind treats salient temporal succession as causal evidence by default; the fallacy is the failure to discharge that default with the kind of comparative reasoning that actually distinguishes cause from coincidence.

**warning**: The False Cause Fallacy is not the claim that correlation never indicates causation; correlations frequently are causal evidence, and treating "correlation is not causation" as a refutation rather than as a prompt to investigate is itself a misuse of the fallacy label.

## Appeal To Ignorance

- secondary_domains: [epistemology, dialectic]
- aliases: [argumentum ad ignorantiam, ignorance fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, evidential fallacies]
- related: [burden of proof, false cause fallacy]

**definition**: The Appeal To Ignorance is the fallacy of treating the absence of evidence against a claim as evidence for it (or absence of evidence for as evidence against), thereby converting one's ignorance into a positive epistemic conclusion.

**key_claim**: The Appeal To Ignorance is parasitic on a misallocated burden of proof; the fallacy collapses once the dialectical question of who must produce evidence is asked, because non-evidence does not transfer epistemic weight to either party absent that allocation.

**warning**: The Appeal To Ignorance is not invariably a fallacy; in well-designed inquiries where evidence would have been found if the claim were true, the absence of such evidence is genuinely confirming, so the fallacy applies only when no such expectation has been established.

## Appeal To Emotion

- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, persuasion-research]
- aliases: [argumentum ad passiones]
- broader: [informal fallacies, irrelevance fallacies]
- related: [appeal to tradition, red herring fallacy]

**definition**: The Appeal To Emotion is the fallacy of substituting emotional response — fear, pity, anger, pride — for a rational ground in support of a conclusion, such that the emotional reaction is offered as if it constituted evidence.

**key_claim**: The Appeal To Emotion is fallacious not because emotions are irrelevant to reasoning but because the felt force of an emotion does not entail that the belief that triggered it is true; the fallacy occurs when emotional weight is treated as inferential weight.

**warning**: The Appeal To Emotion does not entail that emotionally charged arguments are bad arguments; many sound arguments concern matters that legitimately move people, and dismissing reasoning merely because it is moving is a tone-policing move rather than a logical critique.

## Appeal To Tradition

- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, social-epistemology]
- aliases: [argumentum ad antiquitatem, traditional wisdom fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, irrelevance fallacies]
- related: [appeal to nature, genetic fallacy]

**definition**: The Appeal To Tradition is the fallacy of inferring that a practice, belief, or institution is correct, good, or true on the grounds that it has been long-established or widely accepted historically.

**key_claim**: The Appeal To Tradition treats temporal persistence as evidential warrant, but persistence is consistent with both adaptive selection and inertia; without an independent argument that the persistence tracks correctness, the inference has no force.

**warning**: The Appeal To Tradition is not equivalent to citing tradition as relevant context; longstanding practices may carry useful information about what has worked, and noting this is not fallacious provided the inference does not collapse "old" into "right."

## Appeal To Nature

- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, philosophy-of-biology, ethics]
- aliases: [naturalistic fallacy variants, argumentum ad naturam]
- broader: [informal fallacies, irrelevance fallacies]
- related: [appeal to tradition, genetic fallacy]

**definition**: The Appeal To Nature is the fallacy of inferring that something is good, healthy, or correct because it is natural (or bad because it is unnatural), without independent reason to think the natural/unnatural distinction tracks the evaluative property in question.

**key_claim**: The Appeal To Nature collapses two questions that need to be kept apart: whether a thing occurs without human intervention, and whether it is desirable; many natural things are harmful and many beneficial things are engineered, so the inference from natural to good has no general support.

**warning**: The Appeal To Nature is not the claim that naturalness is never relevant; in some contexts (ecological, evolutionary, regulatory) the natural/artificial distinction does carry information, and the fallacy is the inference, not the descriptive use of the category.

## Genetic Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [logic, rhetoric, history-of-ideas]
- aliases: [origin fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, irrelevance fallacies]
- related: [appeal to tradition, ad hominem]

**definition**: The Genetic Fallacy is the error of evaluating a claim or argument on the basis of its origin — who said it, where it came from, what historical process produced it — rather than its current evidential merits.

**key_claim**: The Genetic Fallacy treats origin as if it were entailment, but a claim's truth or argument's validity is independent of how it came to be entertained; even claims with disreputable origins can turn out to be true on inspection.

**warning**: The Genetic Fallacy does not entail that origin information is always irrelevant; origin can defeasibly bear on credibility (e.g., known motivated sources, conflicts of interest), and the fallacy is the inference that origin settles the matter.

## Equivocation Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [logic, semantics, formal-logic]
- aliases: [equivocation, ambiguity fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, ambiguity fallacies]
- related: [argument structure, logical form]

**definition**: The Equivocation Fallacy occurs when a key term shifts meaning within a single argument, so that the inference appears valid only because two distinct concepts are being treated as one.

**key_claim**: The Equivocation Fallacy reveals that argument validity depends on the stability of terms across premises; once a term's reference shifts, the inferential link the argument relies on no longer holds even though the surface structure looks intact.

**warning**: The Equivocation Fallacy is not invoked merely because a term has multiple senses; the fallacy requires that the argument actually exploit a shift in sense, which can be tested by substituting consistent definitions and seeing whether the argument still goes through.

## Motte And Bailey Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, dialectic, social-epistemology]
- aliases: [motte-and-bailey doctrine]
- broader: [informal fallacies, dialectical fallacies]
- related: [equivocation fallacy, no true scotsman fallacy, steelmanning]

**definition**: The Motte And Bailey Fallacy occurs when an arguer advances an ambitious or controversial claim (the bailey), and when challenged retreats to a much weaker, easily defended claim (the motte), then reasserts the original once the challenge has subsided.

**key_claim**: The Motte And Bailey Fallacy exploits the asymmetry between two claims that share vocabulary but differ sharply in content; the move is fallacious because the defender treats successful defence of the weak claim as if it had vindicated the strong one.

**warning**: The Motte And Bailey Fallacy is sometimes attributed wherever a position has both strong and weak versions, but the fallacy specifically requires the strategic shift between them; honest acknowledgement of a weak fallback alongside a stronger preferred claim is not motte-and-bailey reasoning.

## No True Scotsman Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [logic, rhetoric, definitions]
- aliases: [no true scotsman, ad hoc redefinition]
- broader: [informal fallacies, definitional fallacies]
- related: [equivocation fallacy, motte and bailey fallacy]

**definition**: The No True Scotsman Fallacy is the move of dismissing a counterexample to a generalisation by redefining the relevant category mid-argument so that the counterexample is excluded by stipulation rather than refuted.

**key_claim**: The No True Scotsman Fallacy preserves a generalisation by trading its empirical content for definitional content; once "true Xs do not Y" becomes the criterion for being an X, the generalisation is no longer a claim about the world but a tautology.

**warning**: The No True Scotsman Fallacy is not invoked by every act of refining a definition; legitimate clarification of categories can be necessary, and the fallacy specifically applies when the redefinition is ad hoc, immunises the original claim, and is offered only after a counterexample appears.

## Nirvana Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [decision-making, public-policy, comparative-evaluation]
- aliases: [perfect solution fallacy, ideal-vs-actual fallacy]
- broader: [informal fallacies, comparison fallacies]
- related: [neglect of probability, false cause fallacy]

**definition**: The Nirvana Fallacy is the error of rejecting a proposal because it falls short of an idealised, unrealisable alternative, rather than comparing it against the realistic alternatives actually available.

**key_claim**: The Nirvana Fallacy substitutes an unattainable benchmark for the relevant comparison set, which guarantees that any feasible option will fail; honest evaluation requires comparing options to other available options, not to imagined perfection.

**warning**: The Nirvana Fallacy is not the same as holding high standards; specifying a stretch goal is legitimate, and the fallacy occurs only when the unattainable standard is used as a basis for rejecting otherwise dominant feasible options.

## Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [statistics, scientific-reasoning, data-analysis]
- aliases: [post hoc target selection, cherry-picking targets]
- broader: [informal fallacies, statistical fallacies]
- related: [hasty generalization, false cause fallacy, replication and reproducibility]

**definition**: The Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy is the error of identifying a pattern in data after the fact and treating its existence as evidence of a non-chance underlying mechanism, while ignoring the many pattern-targets that could have been picked instead.

**key_claim**: The Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy reveals that the strength of evidence depends on what hypotheses were available before the data were inspected; clusters and coincidences look impressive only when the multiple-comparison structure that generated them is hidden.

**warning**: The Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy does not imply that exploratory pattern-finding is illegitimate; it is the inference from a post hoc pattern to a substantive mechanism, without independent confirmation, that constitutes the fallacy.

## Red Herring Fallacy

- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, dialectic]
- aliases: [red herring, irrelevant conclusion, ignoratio elenchi (related)]
- broader: [informal fallacies, relevance fallacies]
- related: [appeal to emotion, motte and bailey fallacy]

**definition**: The Red Herring Fallacy is the rhetorical move of introducing an irrelevant topic or consideration in order to divert attention from the original issue and to substitute a different issue that is easier to discuss or win.

**key_claim**: The Red Herring Fallacy works by exploiting the conversational tendency to follow whatever new content is introduced; the fallacy is identified not by content but by the relevance gap between the diversion and the original question under dispute.

**warning**: The Red Herring Fallacy is not committed by every change of subject; legitimate reframings, clarifications, or preliminary issues may need to be addressed first, and the fallacy applies specifically when the diversion serves to evade rather than to advance the original question.

## Begging The Question

- secondary_domains: [logic, dialectic]
- aliases: [petitio principii, circular argument]
- broader: [informal fallacies, structural fallacies]
- related: [premise acceptability, equivocation fallacy]

**definition**: Begging The Question is the fallacy of constructing an argument whose conclusion is presupposed — explicitly or implicitly — by one of its premises, so that the argument provides no independent reason to accept the conclusion.

**key_claim**: Begging The Question is dialectically vacuous because the audience that already accepts the question-begging premise already accepts the conclusion, while the audience that rejects the conclusion has no reason to grant the premise; the argument moves no one between positions.

**warning**: Begging The Question is widely misused in popular discourse to mean "raises the question"; the technical sense concerns presupposition of the conclusion within the premises, and conflating the two flattens a precise logical concept into a generic discourse marker.

## Argument From Silence

- secondary_domains: [historiography, evidential-reasoning]
- aliases: [argumentum ex silentio]
- broader: [informal fallacies, evidential fallacies]
- related: [appeal to ignorance, burden of proof]

**definition**: The Argument From Silence is the inference that something did not happen, was not believed, or was not the case because no surviving source mentions it; it draws a positive conclusion from the absence of testimony.

**key_claim**: The Argument From Silence is only as strong as the prior expectation that the missing source would have mentioned the matter; without that expectation, silence is uninformative, and treating silence as evidence in its own right is a special case of the appeal to ignorance.

**warning**: The Argument From Silence is not always fallacious; in source-rich domains where comprehensive coverage is plausible, silence can carry real evidential weight, but in source-sparse domains (most of pre-modern history) it carries almost none and is routinely overweighted.
