## Testing Effect

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, memory]
- aliases: [retrieval-practice effect, test-enhanced learning]
- broader: [memory consolidation]
- narrower: [free recall, cued recall]
- related: [spaced repetition, generation effect, desirable difficulties]
- prerequisites: [long-term memory, encoding vs retrieval]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Testing Effect is the empirical finding that retrieving information from memory — actively producing the answer — strengthens long-term retention more than restudying the same material for an equal amount of time.

**key_claim**: The Testing Effect demonstrates that the act of retrieval is itself a memory-modifying event, not merely a neutral readout of what is stored; this overturns the intuitive "study = strong, test = neutral" model that dominates naive learning practice.

**warning**: The Testing Effect is often misread as endorsing high-stakes evaluation; the empirical effect refers to low-stakes self-testing as a study strategy, and conflating the two leads to test anxiety being mistaken for a learning intervention.

## Generation Effect

- secondary_domains: [learning-science, memory]
- aliases: [generation principle]
- broader: [active learning, encoding specificity]
- related: [testing effect, elaborative interrogation, desirable difficulties]
- prerequisites: [encoding vs retrieval]

**definition**: The Generation Effect is the finding that information a learner produces themselves — by completing, predicting, or constructing it — is remembered better than equivalent information that is simply read or copied.

**key_claim**: The Generation Effect implies that the depth of cognitive engagement at encoding is a stronger predictor of retention than the surface form or duration of exposure, which reframes "study time" as a misleading proxy for "study effort."

**warning**: The Generation Effect breaks down when generation produces incorrect answers without timely feedback; the encoded errors then compete with the correct trace at retrieval, so unguided generation drills can actively harm learning.

## Forgetting Curve

- secondary_domains: [learning-science, memory]
- aliases: [Ebbinghaus forgetting curve]
- broader: [memory decay]
- narrower: [savings, relearning]
- related: [spaced repetition, testing effect]
- prerequisites: [long-term memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Forgetting Curve is the empirical relationship — first quantified by Hermann Ebbinghaus in 1885 — between the time elapsed since learning and the proportion of material still retrievable, typically described as a roughly exponential decline that flattens with each successful relearning episode.

**key_claim**: The Forgetting Curve establishes that retention is governed by a predictable temporal function rather than by the learner's perceived familiarity, which is why "feeling like you know it" is a uniquely unreliable cue for scheduling future review.

**warning**: The Forgetting Curve is often treated as a fixed natural law, but its precise shape depends on material type, prior knowledge, and retrieval conditions; using a single canonical curve to schedule review across all subjects systematically over- or under-schedules large categories of content.
