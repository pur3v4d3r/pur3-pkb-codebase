# Batch: Positive Psychology and Well-Being Anchors

## Positive Psychology

- secondary_domains: [psychology, well-being]
- aliases: [Seligman positive psychology]
- broader: [psychology]
- narrower: [perma-model, broaden-and-build-theory, character-strengths]
- related: [eudaimonia, psychological-flourishing, hope-theory, learned-optimism, savoring, post-traumatic-growth, benefit-finding]
- prerequisites: [humanistic-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Positive Psychology is the scientific study of the conditions and processes that contribute to human flourishing — the strengths, virtues, positive emotions, engaged states, meaningful relationships, and accomplishment patterns that allow individuals and communities to thrive — formally inaugurated as a sub-discipline by Martin Seligman's 1998 American Psychological Association presidential address.

**key_claim**: Positive Psychology insists that the absence of pathology is not the same as the presence of well-being, and that mapping what makes life worth living requires its own empirical program rather than being treated as the residual category left over once disease has been removed.

**warning**: Positive Psychology is frequently confused with the popular self-help genre or with a normative demand to "stay positive"; the empirical program explicitly rejects forced positivity, takes negative emotions as functionally important, and warns that mandated cheerfulness is one of the well-documented routes to need-frustration and burnout.

## Optimism

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, motivational-psychology]
- aliases: [dispositional optimism]
- broader: [explanatory-style]
- related: [learned-optimism, hope-theory, self-efficacy, psychological-resilience, benefit-finding, optimism-bias]
- prerequisites: [attribution-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Optimism is a generalized expectancy that future outcomes will be favorable, operationalized in dispositional form by Scheier and Carver's Life Orientation Test and in attributional form by an explanatory style that treats negative events as external, unstable, and specific while treating positive events as internal, stable, and global.

**key_claim**: Optimism predicts persistence on goal-directed tasks, faster physiological recovery from stressors, and broader use of approach-coping rather than avoidance-coping, and these effects survive controls for trait neuroticism — indicating Optimism functions as more than the cosmetic flip-side of a depressive style.

**warning**: Optimism is not the same as overconfidence or wishful-thinking; unrealistic Optimism that ignores feedback signals (the optimism bias proper) is associated with under-preparation and can degrade calibration, so the construct's protective effects depend on its coexistence with accurate metacognitive monitoring rather than its replacement of it.

## Coping Strategies

- secondary_domains: [stress-research, clinical-psychology]
- aliases: [coping mechanisms, stress-coping]
- broader: [emotional-regulation]
- narrower: [cognitive-reappraisal, problem-focused-coping, emotion-focused-coping]
- related: [psychological-resilience, benefit-finding, post-traumatic-growth, self-compassion, savoring]
- prerequisites: [stress-and-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Coping Strategies are the cognitive and behavioral efforts a person deploys to manage demands appraised as taxing or exceeding their resources — classically partitioned by Lazarus and Folkman into problem-focused coping (acting on the stressor itself) and emotion-focused coping (regulating the affective response when the stressor is unalterable).

**key_claim**: Coping Strategies are not stable trait-like dispositions but situation-specific deployments whose adaptiveness is conditional on the controllability of the stressor, which is why the same strategy (for example, distraction) can be functional in an uncontrollable context and dysfunctional in a controllable one.

**warning**: Coping Strategies are routinely mis-taxonomized as "good" or "bad" coping in popular treatments, but the empirical literature consistently shows that any classification that ignores the appraised controllability of the stressor mis-predicts outcomes; rigid commitment to a single coping mode regardless of context is itself a robust predictor of poorer adjustment.

## Meaning-Making

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, narrative-psychology]
- aliases: [sense-making, meaning construction]
- broader: [narrative-cognition]
- related: [post-traumatic-growth, benefit-finding, eudaimonia, psychological-resilience, cognitive-reappraisal, transformative-learning]
- prerequisites: [autobiographical-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Meaning-Making is the cognitive and narrative process by which a person integrates discrepant or disruptive experiences — losses, traumas, transitions, ambiguous events — into a coherent autobiographical and conceptual framework, restoring a sense of comprehensibility, significance, and purpose that the experience had threatened.

**key_claim**: Meaning-Making operates on two distinct levels that the literature too often conflates: situational meaning (the appraisal of a particular event) and global meaning (the broader belief and goal system into which it is assimilated), and successful adaptation typically requires updating one or both rather than asserting the unchanged validity of either.

**warning**: Meaning-Making is sometimes treated as inherently restorative, but forced or premature Meaning-Making — particularly the imposition of redemptive narratives on losses that have not yet been fully appraised — predicts poorer outcomes than acknowledged disruption, so the timing and authenticity of the process matter at least as much as its occurrence.

## Hedonic Well-Being

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, philosophy-of-well-being]
- aliases: [hedonic happiness, subjective well-being]
- broader: [psychological-flourishing]
- related: [eudaimonia, perma-model, savoring, hedonic-adaptation, broaden-and-build-theory]
- prerequisites: [emotional-regulation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Hedonic Well-Being is the dimension of flourishing constituted by the balance of pleasant over unpleasant affect plus a person's global cognitive judgment of life satisfaction — the construct that Diener's tripartite Subjective Well-Being model operationalized and that contrasts in the philosophical tradition with eudaimonic conceptions of the good life.

**key_claim**: Hedonic Well-Being and eudaimonic well-being are empirically correlated but factorially distinct, and interventions that elevate one do not reliably elevate the other — indicating that the question "which conception of well-being to pursue" is not a definitional quibble but an actionable design choice with measurable downstream effects.

**warning**: Hedonic Well-Being is vulnerable to hedonic adaptation: the affective component drifts back toward a person's set-point as circumstances stabilize, so policies and personal projects that target only Hedonic Well-Being without also engaging meaning, mastery, or relational depth tend to produce diminishing returns over time.

## Signature Strengths

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, virtue-ethics]
- aliases: [top character strengths, signature virtues]
- broader: [character-strengths]
- related: [virtues, perma-model, eudaimonia, intrinsic-motivation, psychological-flourishing]
- prerequisites: [self-awareness]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Signature Strengths are the small subset of an individual's most central, energizing, and self-defining character strengths within the Values-In-Action classification — typically the top three to seven of the twenty-four strengths — whose deployment in everyday roles is hypothesized to produce greater authenticity, engagement, and life satisfaction than the cultivation of weaker strengths.

**key_claim**: Signature Strengths function as a leverage point for well-being interventions because acting from one's top strengths in a new way for a sustained period reliably increases life satisfaction and decreases depressive symptoms in randomized trials — an effect attributed to the alignment of activity with intrinsic identity rather than to the strengths themselves in isolation.

**warning**: Signature Strengths are commonly misread as fixed traits to discover and then express; the more defensible reading is that they are the strengths a person currently identifies with most readily, and treating them as immutable can entrench identity in ways that block the developmental growth the framework was originally meant to enable.

## Virtues

- secondary_domains: [virtue-ethics, moral-psychology]
- aliases: [moral virtues, character virtues]
- broader: [character-strengths]
- narrower: [signature-strengths]
- related: [eudaimonia, virtue-epistemology, intellectual-humility, wisdom, self-compassion, moral-development]
- prerequisites: [moral-development]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Virtues are stable, action-guiding excellences of character — dispositions to feel, judge, and act well across recurring situations of a given type — that are valued cross-culturally and that the Values-In-Action classification organizes into six superordinate categories (wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence) bridging Aristotelian and contemporary positive-psychology traditions.

**key_claim**: Virtues are best understood as cultivated rather than innate: they emerge from repeated practice in graded contexts that allows the relevant emotional, perceptual, and behavioral patterns to become habituated, which is why virtue cultivation programs that target single behaviors without reshaping the surrounding context produce weaker effects than those that restructure both.

**warning**: Virtues language is easily slid into moralistic prescription, but the empirical and philosophical core of the construct is descriptive — Virtues are the trait-like patterns that demonstrably enable flourishing — and using virtue vocabulary to enforce a single normative ideal ignores the well-documented pluralism of viable virtue profiles across cultures and developmental stages.

## Ryff's Six-Factor Model of Psychological Well-Being

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, eudaimonic-psychology]
- aliases: [Ryff PWB model, Scales of Psychological Well-Being]
- broader: [psychological-flourishing]
- related: [eudaimonia, perma-model, hedonic-well-being, autonomy, self-determination-theory, meaning-making]
- prerequisites: [eudaimonia]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Ryff's Six-Factor Model of Psychological Well-Being is Carol Ryff's eudaimonic-tradition operationalization of well-being into six distinct dimensions — autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance — assessed by the Scales of Psychological Well-Being and explicitly contrasted with hedonic subjective-well-being measures.

**key_claim**: Ryff's Six-Factor Model of Psychological Well-Being established that eudaimonic well-being is multi-dimensional rather than a single global construct, and the dimensions display distinct developmental trajectories across the lifespan — for example, environmental mastery and autonomy rise into midlife while purpose in life tends to decline in late adulthood.

**warning**: Ryff's Six-Factor Model of Psychological Well-Being is sometimes taken as a definitive map of eudaimonic well-being, but the six-factor structure has not been replicated in every cultural sample, and short-form versions of the scales lose factor distinctness; treating the six dimensions as cleanly orthogonal can therefore over-state how cleanly an intervention targets one without engaging the others.

## Wishful Thinking

- secondary_domains: [decision-science, social-cognition]
- aliases: [desirability bias, wishful belief]
- broader: [motivated-reasoning]
- related: [optimism, confirmation-bias, motivated-reasoning, self-serving-bias, self-deception, hope-theory]
- prerequisites: [motivated-reasoning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Wishful Thinking is the cognitive bias in which the perceived likelihood of an outcome is shifted toward the outcome a person desires, independent of the evidence — a desirability-driven distortion of probability judgment distinct from optimism (which is a future-expectancy disposition) and from confirmation bias (which is a search and weighting bias on evidence).

**key_claim**: Wishful Thinking is robust across forecasting domains as long as the desired outcome is sufficiently personally consequential, and it is amplified rather than corrected by greater domain expertise when accountability for the prediction is low — indicating that the bias targets the gap between desire and accountability rather than the gap between desire and information.

**warning**: Wishful Thinking is often diagnosed retrospectively in any failed prediction that turned out to favor the predictor's interest, but the methodologically sound diagnosis requires comparing the predictor's stated probability to a calibrated reference class; without that comparison, the label collapses into the post-hoc complaint that the person was hopeful and wrong.

## Volition

- secondary_domains: [motivation-science, philosophy-of-action]
- aliases: [will, willing, volitional control]
- broader: [self-regulation]
- narrower: [volitional-control, implementation-intention]
- related: [goal-intention, action-control-theory, willpower, self-control, autonomous-motivation, action-phase-model, rubicon]
- prerequisites: [forethought-phase]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Volition is the post-decisional set of psychological processes by which an intention is shielded against competing tendencies, translated into action, and maintained through interruption — the territory the Rubicon model of action phases assigns to the post-decisional space and that Kuhl's Action Control Theory partitions into attentional, motivational, emotional, environmental, and parsimonious-information-processing controls.

**key_claim**: Volition is theoretically and empirically separable from motivation: motivational processes determine which goal a person is committed to, while Volition determines whether that commitment is carried through given the inevitable temptations and disruptions of execution — a distinction that explains why high-motivation individuals can systematically under-implement their own goals.

**warning**: Volition is often collapsed into willpower or self-control in popular discourse, which licenses moralistic explanations of failure to act; the action-control literature instead treats Volition as a set of trainable strategies (implementation intentions, environment design, attentional control) whose absence is a skill deficit rather than a character defect.
