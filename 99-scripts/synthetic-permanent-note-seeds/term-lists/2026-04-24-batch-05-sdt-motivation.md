# Batch: Self-Determination Theory & Motivation

## Autonomy Need

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, psychology]
- aliases: [need for autonomy]
- broader: [basic-psychological-needs, self-determination-theory]
- related: [competence-need, relatedness-need, autonomous-regulation, [[deci-and-ryan]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Autonomy Need, in Self-Determination Theory, is the basic psychological requirement to experience one's actions as volitional and self-endorsed — as expressions of the integrated self rather than as compelled by external pressure or internal compulsion — and is one of the three needs Deci and Ryan identify as universal across cultures.

**key_claim**: The Autonomy Need is satisfied not by absence of structure but by the experience of *self-endorsement* of one's engagement: a learner can experience high autonomy within demanding curricula if the demands are perceived as personally meaningful, while a learner in a permissive environment can experience low autonomy if engagement feels arbitrary or unconnected to self.

**warning**: The Autonomy Need is widely confused with independence or freedom of choice; SDT explicitly distinguishes Autonomy Need satisfaction from independence (an interdependent person can be highly autonomous) and from sheer choice (token choices among trivial options do not satisfy the Autonomy Need), and pedagogical "autonomy support" that conflates these targets the wrong construct.

## Competence Need

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, psychology]
- aliases: [need for competence]
- broader: [basic-psychological-needs, self-determination-theory]
- related: [autonomy-need, relatedness-need, [[mastery-experience]], [[optimal-challenge]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Competence Need, in Self-Determination Theory, is the basic psychological requirement to feel effective in one's interactions with the environment — to experience oneself as capable of producing intended outcomes — and is satisfied by optimally challenging activities that yield clear progress and feedback.

**key_claim**: The Competence Need is supported by what SDT calls *structure* — clear expectations, scaffolded difficulty progression, informative feedback — and is undermined by both excessive ease (no efficacy signal) and excessive difficulty without scaffolding (efficacy signal of failure), which together specify the design space for environments that nourish the Competence Need.

**warning**: The Competence Need is sometimes pursued by lowering challenge to ensure "wins," but this strategy backfires: trivial successes do not produce genuine Competence Need satisfaction because the learner is not deceived about the difficulty of what was achieved; sustainable Competence Need satisfaction requires real challenge with appropriate scaffolding, not contrived success.

## Relatedness Need

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, psychology]
- aliases: [need for relatedness, need to belong]
- broader: [basic-psychological-needs, self-determination-theory]
- related: [autonomy-need, competence-need, attachment-theory, [[belongingness]], [[sense-of-community]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Relatedness Need, in Self-Determination Theory, is the basic psychological requirement to feel connected to others — to care and be cared for, to belong to a community — and is satisfied by warm, responsive relationships in which the person feels valued for who they are rather than for instrumental performance.

**key_claim**: The Relatedness Need is the most often-overlooked of the three SDT basic needs in instructional design, yet relatedness with teachers and peers is a strong predictor of engagement, persistence, and well-being, particularly for learners from groups for whom belonging in the educational setting cannot be taken for granted.

**warning**: The Relatedness Need is undermined by conditional regard — affection contingent on performance — even when the conditions are well-meant; teachers and parents who only show warmth following success or compliance compromise Relatedness Need satisfaction in ways that undermine the very autonomous motivation they hope to cultivate.

## Need Thwarting

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, well-being-research]
- aliases: [psychological-need thwarting]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- related: [need-support, [[ill-being], [[controlling-environment], [[psychological-need-frustration]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Need Thwarting, in Self-Determination Theory, refers to environmental conditions and interactions that *actively frustrate* the basic psychological needs — controlling pressure that thwarts autonomy, demeaning feedback that thwarts competence, rejecting or conditional treatment that thwarts relatedness — distinct from mere absence of support.

**key_claim**: Need Thwarting predicts ill-being, defensive disengagement, and psychopathology more strongly than mere need *non*-support predicts well-being; the asymmetry is empirically robust and has the practical implication that removing thwarting conditions is more urgent than adding supportive ones — bad environments harm more than good environments help.

**warning**: Need Thwarting is often invisible to the thwarting party: well-intentioned controlling pressure ("for your own good") thwarts autonomy regardless of intent, and well-meant comparative feedback ("you're doing better than X") can thwart competence by recasting performance as zero-sum; reducing Need Thwarting requires explicit attention to the *experience* of those affected, not the intent of those acting.

## Need Support

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, instructional-design]
- aliases: [autonomy-supportive teaching, need-supportive practice]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- related: [need-thwarting, autonomy-need, competence-need, relatedness-need, [[autonomy-supportive-teaching]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Need Support refers to interpersonal and environmental practices that nourish the three SDT basic psychological needs simultaneously — providing meaningful rationale and choice (autonomy), structured challenge with informative feedback (competence), and warm involvement with non-conditional regard (relatedness) — and is the practical operationalisation of SDT for parents, teachers, coaches, and managers.

**key_claim**: Need Support across hundreds of intervention studies produces moderate gains in autonomous motivation, engagement, and well-being, with effects that generalise across age, culture, and domain — making Need Support one of the most generalisable evidence-based interpersonal practices identified by motivation research.

**warning**: Need Support is often conflated with permissiveness or warmth alone, but SDT specifies that all three needs must be addressed; warmth without structure (Relatedness without Competence support) is as incomplete as structure without choice (Competence support without Autonomy support), and effective Need Support is the integrated practice, not any one component.

## Autonomous Regulation

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory]
- aliases: [autonomous motivation]
- broader: [self-determination-theory, [[organismic-integration-theory]]]
- related: [[controlled-regulation], [[identified-regulation], [[integrated-regulation], [[intrinsic-motivation]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Autonomous Regulation, in Self-Determination Theory's organismic integration subtheory, is the family of motivational regulations characterised by self-endorsement — including intrinsic motivation, integrated regulation, and identified regulation — in contrast to controlled regulations (introjected and external) that involve internal or external pressure.

**key_claim**: Autonomous Regulation predicts qualitatively better outcomes than controlled regulation across virtually every domain studied — deeper learning, greater persistence, more creative problem-solving, better mental and physical health — even when the absolute *amount* of motivation is matched, which is why SDT treats motivational *quality* as more important than motivational *quantity*.

**warning**: Autonomous Regulation cannot be installed by exhortation; one cannot simply tell a learner to find their work personally meaningful. The conditions that promote internalisation toward Autonomous Regulation are environmental — meaningful rationale, autonomy support, structure that supports competence — and pedagogy that demands autonomous motivation from learners without providing those conditions sets them up to fail.

## General Causality Orientations Scale

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, psychometrics]
- aliases: [GCOS, causality orientations scale]
- broader: [causality-orientations-theory]
- related: [[autonomy-orientation], [[control-orientation], [[impersonal-orientation], self-determination-theory]
- prerequisites: [causality-orientations-theory]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: The General Causality Orientations Scale (Deci & Ryan, 1985) is the self-report instrument that measures three relatively stable individual differences in motivational orientation — autonomy orientation (acting from interest and value), control orientation (acting from internal/external pressure), and impersonal orientation (experiencing action as ineffective) — by asking respondents to report likely reactions to vignettes.

**key_claim**: The General Causality Orientations Scale operationalises SDT's claim that internalisation produces stable individual differences in how people characteristically experience and regulate their actions; GCOS scores predict outcomes across health, work, education, and relationships that match SDT's theoretical predictions.

**warning**: The General Causality Orientations Scale is best treated as measuring *trait-level tendencies* that are nonetheless responsive to context: a person with a moderate autonomy orientation can be pushed into controlled regulation by a heavily contingent environment, and a control-oriented person can be drawn into autonomous regulation by sustained need-supportive conditions, so GCOS scores should not be reified as fixed personality.

## Causality Orientations Theory

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, personality-psychology]
- aliases: [COT, SDT causality orientations]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- related: [general-causality-orientations-scale, autonomous-regulation, [[locus-of-causality]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Causality Orientations Theory is the SDT subtheory addressing relatively stable individual differences in motivational orientation — the degree to which a person characteristically experiences action as autonomous, controlled, or impersonal — and bridges SDT's situational analysis with personality-level explanation of consistent motivational patterns.

**key_claim**: Causality Orientations Theory predicts that the same situation can produce systematically different motivational experiences depending on the person's orientation, and that orientations themselves develop over time through cumulative exposure to need-supportive vs. need-thwarting environments — connecting SDT's developmental and individual-difference levels of analysis.

**warning**: Causality Orientations Theory should not be used to explain away environmental effects: telling a controlled-oriented learner to become more autonomous is no more effective than telling a depressed person to cheer up; orientations are partly a downstream consequence of environment, and intervention should typically address need-thwarting conditions rather than the orientations they produced.

## Relationship Motivation Theory

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, relationship-research]
- aliases: [RMT]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- related: [relatedness-need, attachment-theory, [[interdependence], [[autonomy-in-relationships]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Relationship Motivation Theory is the SDT subtheory addressing how the basic psychological needs operate within close relationships — friendships, romantic partnerships, parent-child relationships — and explains why high-quality relationships are characterised by mutual need support and why need thwarting in relationships predicts distress and dissolution.

**key_claim**: Relationship Motivation Theory predicts that the *highest-quality* relationships are not those in which partners are merely warm but those in which partners actively support each other's autonomy as well as relatedness; relationships that maximise relatedness at the cost of autonomy (enmeshment, controlling closeness) are predicted to be less satisfying and less stable than those that integrate the two.

**warning**: Relationship Motivation Theory is sometimes critiqued as importing individualistic Western values about autonomy into relationship analysis; SDT responds that the autonomy at issue is *self-endorsement* (compatible with deeply interdependent relationships) rather than *independence*, but consumers of Relationship Motivation Theory should attend to this distinction to avoid misapplying the framework cross-culturally.

## Goal Contents Theory

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, well-being-research]
- aliases: [GCT, intrinsic vs extrinsic goals]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- related: [eudaimonia, [[intrinsic-aspirations], [[extrinsic-aspirations], [[aspirations-index]]]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Goal Contents Theory is the SDT subtheory distinguishing *intrinsic* life goals (personal growth, close relationships, community contribution, health) from *extrinsic* life goals (wealth, fame, image), and predicting that pursuit of intrinsic goals yields greater well-being while pursuit of extrinsic goals — even successful pursuit — does not.

**key_claim**: Goal Contents Theory is supported by a robust cross-cultural evidence base showing that placing relative emphasis on extrinsic over intrinsic aspirations predicts lower well-being, more mental-health symptoms, and worse interpersonal functioning, even after controlling for absolute attainment levels — a finding with serious implications for cultures that valorise extrinsic success.

**warning**: Goal Contents Theory should not be misread as a moral injunction against ever pursuing extrinsic goals; income, recognition, and reputation matter for survival and security, and the empirical claim is about the *relative weight* given to extrinsic vs. intrinsic goals, not about renouncing the former altogether — a distinction lost in popularisations that treat the theory as ascetic.

## Overjustification Effect

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [psychology, behavioural-economics]
- aliases: [crowding out of intrinsic motivation]
- broader: [intrinsic-motivation-research]
- related: [self-determination-theory, [[lepper-greene-nisbett], [[external-rewards], [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Overjustification Effect, demonstrated by Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett (1973), is the empirical finding that introducing tangible extrinsic rewards for an already intrinsically motivating activity reduces subsequent intrinsic motivation when the reward is removed, because the activity becomes cognitively reframed as instrumental rather than self-endorsed.

**key_claim**: The Overjustification Effect undermines a naive behaviourist assumption that reward always strengthens behaviour: when the target behaviour is already intrinsically motivated, contingent tangible rewards can systematically undermine subsequent autonomous engagement, with the strongest effects for expected, salient, controlling rewards.

**warning**: The Overjustification Effect is sometimes overgeneralised into a blanket prohibition on rewards in education and work, but the meta-analytic evidence (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999) shows that rewards delivered as informational feedback for high-quality performance do not produce the effect; the practical lesson is to deliver rewards informationally rather than controllingly, not to abandon recognition altogether.

## Motivational Interviewing

- domain: clinical-psychology
- secondary_domains: [behaviour-change, counselling]
- aliases: [MI]
- broader: [counselling, behaviour-change-techniques]
- related: [self-determination-theory, [[stages-of-change], [[change-talk], [[ambivalence]], autonomy-need]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Motivational Interviewing, developed by Miller and Rollnick, is a person-centred counselling approach that elicits and strengthens the client's own motivation for change by exploring and resolving ambivalence rather than imposing change from outside, using core skills of open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summaries.

**key_claim**: Motivational Interviewing is one of the most extensively evidence-based behaviour-change approaches in clinical practice, with meta-analytic support across substance use, health behaviour, and adherence — and its mechanism aligns closely with SDT: MI's autonomy-supportive practices reliably increase autonomous regulation for change, which then mediates behavioural outcomes.

**warning**: Motivational Interviewing has a high "lethal dose" of training — practitioners exposed to brief workshops without supervised practice routinely believe they are doing MI when audio-coding shows they are doing directive counselling; competence in Motivational Interviewing requires sustained training with feedback rather than weekend workshops, which limits its real-world implementation despite the strong outcome evidence.

## Attachment Theory

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [clinical-psychology, relationship-research]
- aliases: [Bowlby attachment theory]
- broader: [developmental-psychology]
- related: [relatedness-need, [[bowlby], [[ainsworth], [[secure-attachment], [[insecure-attachment]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Attachment Theory, founded by John Bowlby and elaborated by Mary Ainsworth, is the developmental and clinical framework that explains how patterns of early caregiver responsiveness shape internal working models of self, other, and relationships that organise emotional regulation and interpersonal behaviour across the lifespan.

**key_claim**: Attachment Theory predicts that secure-base experiences in childhood — caregivers who are responsive, predictable, and emotionally available — produce internal working models supportive of confident exploration and trustful relationships, while patterns of unresponsive, inconsistent, or frightening caregiving produce insecure attachment patterns associated with measurable interpersonal and regulatory difficulties.

**warning**: Attachment Theory's classifications (secure, avoidant, anxious, disorganised) are often misused as diagnostic labels that overstate stability and underestimate context; attachment patterns are more responsive to current relationships than the popular literature suggests, and treating someone as "an avoidant" obscures both the developmental conditions that produced the pattern and the relational conditions that could revise it.

## Flow

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, performance-psychology]
- aliases: [flow state, optimal experience]
- broader: [intrinsic-motivation-research]
- related: [[csikszentmihalyi], [[autotelic-experience], [[challenge-skill-balance], [[deep-work]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Flow, as defined by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, is the autotelic experience of complete absorption in an activity that occurs when challenge and skill are both high and roughly matched, characterised by merged action and awareness, intense concentration, distorted time perception, and intrinsic reward derived from the activity itself.

**key_claim**: Flow predicts high engagement, intrinsic motivation, and skill development because the conditions that produce it — clear goals, immediate feedback, optimal challenge — are also the conditions that produce sustained deliberate practice; designing environments that reliably afford Flow is therefore a high-leverage intervention for both performance and well-being.

**warning**: Flow is sometimes treated as a universal good, but it can occur during ethically problematic activities (skilled gambling, manipulative work, violent games) and can become addictive in ways that crowd out other valued activities; the experience of Flow is content-neutral, and its goodness depends on the goodness of what one is absorbed in.

## Amotivation

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory]
- aliases: [absence of motivation]
- broader: [self-determination-theory, [[organismic-integration-theory]]]
- related: [[learned-helplessness], [[demoralisation], autonomous-regulation, controlled-regulation]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Amotivation, in Self-Determination Theory, is the state of acting without intentionality or with a sense that the action is meaningless or beyond one's control — distinct from intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in that it represents the absence of regulation rather than a particular regulatory style — and is predicted by chronic need thwarting and helplessness experiences.

**key_claim**: Amotivation predicts disengagement, withdrawal, and poor performance more reliably than any single deficit in autonomous regulation, and is the typical end-state of sustained exposure to controlling, incompetence-inducing, or rejecting environments — which is why SDT treats Amotivation as a clinical-significance signal rather than as a mere motivation deficit.

**warning**: Amotivation is often misread as laziness or character defect, but SDT's analysis points to environmental causation: a learner who has been chronically need-thwarted will appear amotivated even in subsequently supportive environments because the working model that "effort doesn't matter" persists; rebuilding requires sustained need support, not exhortation.

## Eudaimonia

- domain: well-being-research
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, positive-psychology]
- aliases: [eudaimonic well-being, flourishing]
- broader: [well-being-research]
- related: [[hedonic-well-being], [[aristotle], [[virtue-ethics], goal-contents-theory, [[meaning-in-life]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Eudaimonia is the Aristotelian conception of well-being as living in accord with virtue and one's true self — translated as "flourishing" rather than mere "happiness" — and contrasts in well-being research with hedonic conceptions that equate well-being with subjective pleasure or life satisfaction.

**key_claim**: Eudaimonia in contemporary research (Ryff, Deci & Ryan, Waterman) operationalises flourishing as a profile including autonomy, competence, relatedness, purpose, growth, and self-acceptance, and predicts outcomes — physical health, longevity, resilience — that hedonic well-being alone does not, suggesting that the two traditions track partly distinct psychological realities.

**warning**: Eudaimonia is normatively loaded in ways that hedonic measures are not — to operationalise flourishing one must take a stance on what counts as virtue and meaningful living — and consumers should be aware that "eudaimonic well-being" measures encode philosophical commitments that might not transfer cleanly across cultural traditions, despite their authors' universalist intent.

## Mastery Goal Orientation

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [achievement-goal-theory, education]
- aliases: [learning goal orientation, mastery orientation]
- broader: [achievement-goal-theory]
- related: [performance-goal-orientation, [[intrinsic-motivation], [[deep-learning], [[task-involvement]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Mastery Goal Orientation is the tendency to construe achievement situations as opportunities to develop competence — focusing on learning, improvement, and task mastery — rather than as opportunities to demonstrate competence relative to others, and is associated with adaptive patterns of effort, persistence, and deep processing.

**key_claim**: A Mastery Goal Orientation predicts more adaptive responses to setback (effort attribution, strategy revision) and more durable learning (deep processing, conceptual integration) than performance orientations, particularly in challenging domains where short-term performance comparisons can be discouraging.

**warning**: A Mastery Goal Orientation is often promoted in education without recognising that institutional structures (grading curves, comparative ranking, scholarship competition) actively reward performance-orientation responses; exhorting Mastery Goal Orientation in environments that systematically reinforce performance comparisons is asking learners to swim against the institutional current with predictable outcomes.

## Performance Goal Orientation

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [achievement-goal-theory, education]
- aliases: [ego-goal orientation, ability-goal orientation]
- broader: [achievement-goal-theory]
- related: [mastery-goal-orientation, ego-involvement, [[performance-approach], [[performance-avoidance]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Performance Goal Orientation is the tendency to construe achievement situations as opportunities to demonstrate competence relative to others — through outperforming peers (performance-approach) or avoiding being outperformed (performance-avoidance) — with social comparison rather than personal mastery as the criterion of success.

**key_claim**: A Performance Goal Orientation produces a mixed pattern: performance-approach can support effort and engagement when challenges are manageable, but performance-avoidance reliably predicts maladaptive responses (anxiety, surface processing, self-handicapping), and Performance Goal Orientations as a class shift attention from learning to looking competent in ways that compromise transfer.

**warning**: A Performance Goal Orientation is often inadvertently induced by teaching practices that are intended as mastery-supportive — public recognition of top scorers, comparative grade displays, "exemplary work" boards — which signal to learners that comparative performance is what is valued, regardless of stated mastery emphasis; design must align signals with stated goals.

## Ego Involvement

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, achievement-goal-theory]
- aliases: [ego involvement in tasks, contingent self-worth in tasks]
- broader: [introjected-regulation]
- related: [performance-goal-orientation, [[task-involvement], controlled-regulation, [[self-esteem]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Ego Involvement, in SDT and achievement-goal frameworks, is the state in which a person's self-worth becomes contingent on task performance — succeeding feels like proving the self, failing feels like the self is deficient — and is a form of internalised pressure characteristic of introjected regulation.

**key_claim**: Ego Involvement produces high short-term effort but at substantial cost: anxiety, self-handicapping when failure looms, withdrawal when failure occurs, and reduced creativity due to the narrowing effects of self-protective focus, which is why ego-involving practices that boost grades in the short term often degrade learning and well-being in the long term.

**warning**: Ego Involvement is particularly easily induced by adults who praise *the person* rather than *the work* ("you're so smart" rather than "you found a productive approach"); person-praise has been shown by Mueller and Dweck (1998) and replicated since to induce Ego Involvement and the maladaptive responses to challenge that follow, even when intended as encouragement.

## Feedback Design for Autonomy and Mastery

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [self-determination-theory, learning-science]
- aliases: [autonomy-supportive feedback, mastery-supportive feedback]
- broader: [feedback, instructional-design]
- related: [need-support, [[informational-feedback], [[controlling-feedback], mastery-goal-orientation]
- prerequisites: [need-support]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Feedback Design for Autonomy and Mastery is the family of practices that deliver evaluative information in ways that simultaneously support competence (informative about quality and progress), autonomy (non-controlling, respectful of choice), and mastery focus (oriented to learning rather than to comparative ranking) — operationalising SDT and achievement-goal principles in concrete instructional moves.

**key_claim**: Feedback Design for Autonomy and Mastery emphasises specific moves: feedback on the work rather than the person, clear progress information against task criteria rather than against peers, suggestions framed as options rather than demands, and explicit acknowledgement of the learner's perspective — practices that consistently produce better learning and well-being than evaluative-controlling feedback.

**warning**: Feedback Design for Autonomy and Mastery can degrade into vacuous praise or non-evaluative warmth that fails to provide the informational signal learners need; the autonomy-support and mastery focus do not require *withholding* judgement but rather *delivering* judgement in informational rather than controlling form, a distinction that is hard to maintain under time pressure.

## Academic Emotions

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [emotion-research, learning-science]
- aliases: [achievement emotions]
- broader: [educational-psychology, emotion-research]
- related: [emotional-regulation, [[control-value-theory], [[pekrun], [[test-anxiety], [[boredom]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Academic Emotions, as theorised by Reinhard Pekrun's control-value theory, are the affective experiences specifically tied to academic activities and outcomes — enjoyment, hope, pride, anxiety, anger, shame, hopelessness, boredom — and are predicted by learners' appraisals of control over and value of academic outcomes.

**key_claim**: Academic Emotions exert measurable effects on learning processes and outcomes — positive activating emotions support engagement and deep processing, while negative deactivating emotions like hopelessness and boredom produce withdrawal — making attention to Academic Emotions a non-optional component of pedagogy aimed at sustained engagement.

**warning**: Academic Emotions are often treated as side-effects to be managed rather than as central design targets, but in the control-value theory they are downstream of curricular and pedagogical choices about control and value; reducing test anxiety, for instance, is more durably accomplished by reshaping the structures that produce uncontrollable high-stakes evaluation than by teaching individual coping strategies.

## Emotional Regulation

- domain: emotion-research
- secondary_domains: [clinical-psychology, developmental-psychology]
- aliases: [emotion regulation, affect regulation]
- broader: [self-regulation]
- related: [academic-emotions, [[gross-process-model], [[reappraisal], [[suppression], metacognitive-regulation]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Emotional Regulation is the set of processes — automatic and controlled, conscious and unconscious — by which people influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express them, formalised by Gross's process model into antecedent-focused (situation selection, situation modification, attention deployment, cognitive change) and response-focused (response modulation) strategies.

**key_claim**: Emotional Regulation strategy choice has substantial consequences for outcomes: cognitive reappraisal (reframing the meaning of emotion-eliciting situations) is associated with better well-being and performance than expressive suppression (inhibiting emotion display while continuing to feel it), making strategy training a tractable target for intervention.

**warning**: Emotional Regulation is often mistakenly equated with emotion suppression — "don't let it show" — which is the strategy with the worst outcomes in the empirical literature; effective Emotional Regulation pedagogy must distinguish among strategies and explicitly teach the more adaptive ones, rather than promoting the implicit suppression model that schools often reinforce.
