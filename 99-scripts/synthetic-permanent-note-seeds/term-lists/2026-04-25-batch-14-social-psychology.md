# Batch: Social Psychology

## Social Identity Theory

- secondary_domains: [intergroup-relations, social-cognition]
- aliases: [SIT, Tajfel-Turner social identity theory]
- broader: [self-concept-theories]
- related: [in-group-out-group-bias, self-categorization-theory, stereotype-threat, tajfel-henri, turner-john, intergroup-conflict]
- prerequisites: [self-concept-theories]

**definition**: Social Identity Theory, developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, holds that a substantial portion of self-concept derives from membership in social groups (nationality, profession, fandom, ideology), that individuals are motivated to maintain a positive social identity, and that this motive produces in-group favoritism and inter-group differentiation even in the absence of any material conflict, as demonstrated in the minimal-group paradigm.

**key_claim**: Social Identity Theory established that intergroup bias is not solely a product of resource competition or personality pathology: arbitrary categorization alone (the minimal-group experiments) suffices to produce in-group favoritism, which forces explanations of prejudice and intergroup conflict to take *categorization itself* as a primary causal variable rather than a downstream consequence.

**warning**: Social Identity Theory is sometimes used to argue that intergroup hostility is biologically inevitable; the theory's actual claim is more limited — that categorization plus identity motives produce *favoritism*, not necessarily out-group hostility — and conflating favoritism with hostility licenses fatalist conclusions about prejudice that the original framework does not support.

## Cognitive Dissonance

- secondary_domains: [attitude-change, motivation]
- aliases: [Festinger cognitive dissonance theory]
- broader: [consistency-theories]
- related: [attitude-formation, self-perception-theory, festinger-leon, induced-compliance-paradigm, post-decisional-dissonance]
- prerequisites: [consistency-theories]

**definition**: Cognitive Dissonance, formalized by Leon Festinger in 1957, is the aversive psychological state arising when an individual holds simultaneously inconsistent cognitions — an attitude and a behavior that contradict it, two contradictory beliefs, a choice and the foregone alternative — and that motivates dissonance-reduction efforts (attitude change, behavior change, trivialization of one cognition, or addition of consonant cognitions).

**key_claim**: Cognitive Dissonance reframed attitude change as a *motivated* process rather than a passive response to information: the classic Festinger-Carlsmith induced-compliance experiment showed that participants paid less to lie about a boring task subsequently rated it as more interesting, demonstrating that insufficient external justification forces internal attitude change to restore consistency.

**warning**: Cognitive Dissonance is often invoked loosely to describe any psychological discomfort with conflicting ideas; the original construct requires *self-relevant* inconsistency producing arousal, and modern accounts (Cooper-Fazio, self-affirmation theory) further restrict the conditions under which dissonance produces attitude change, so casual "dissonance" attributions usually fail to satisfy the theory's actual conditions.

## Attitude Formation

- secondary_domains: [social-cognition, attitude-research]
- broader: [attitude-research]
- related: [persuasion-theory, classical-conditioning, mere-exposure-effect, self-perception-theory, attitude-strength, implicit-attitudes]
- prerequisites: [attitude-research]

**definition**: Attitude Formation is the process by which evaluative dispositions toward objects, people, or issues are acquired through three principal routes — affective conditioning (object becomes associated with positive or negative affect), behavioral inference (attitude inferred from one's own behavior, per self-perception theory), and cognitive evaluation (attitude derived from beliefs about object attributes weighted by evaluation).

**key_claim**: Attitude Formation processes operate concurrently and produce attitudes that vary in *strength* — accessibility, certainty, importance, behavioral predictiveness — and the strength dimension matters more than the direction for predicting whether an attitude will resist persuasion, guide behavior, or remain stable over time, a fact that early attitude-direction-only research systematically obscured.

**warning**: Attitude Formation models often imply that attitudes determine behavior; the attitude-behavior correlation is reliably moderate (around r ≈ 0.3 in early reviews, higher only under specific conditions of attitude-behavior measurement compatibility), so behavior prediction from attitudes alone is a weaker inferential tool than introductory texts often suggest.

## Persuasion Theory

- secondary_domains: [attitude-change, communication]
- aliases: [elaboration likelihood model, ELM]
- broader: [attitude-change]
- related: [attitude-formation, cognitive-dissonance, central-vs-peripheral-route, petty-richard, cacioppo-john, heuristic-systematic-model]
- prerequisites: [attitude-research]

**definition**: Persuasion Theory in its dominant modern formulation (Petty and Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model, with Chaiken's parallel Heuristic-Systematic Model) holds that attitude change occurs via two routes: a *central* route in which recipients carefully evaluate argument quality (when motivation and ability to process are high), and a *peripheral* route in which they rely on heuristic cues (source credibility, message length, communicator attractiveness) when motivation or ability is low.

**key_claim**: Persuasion Theory's dual-route framework predicts a critical moderation: high-quality arguments outperform low-quality arguments only under high-elaboration conditions, while peripheral cues (e.g., expert source) move attitudes only under low-elaboration conditions — explaining why the same persuasive ingredient succeeds in one context and fails in another, and why "stronger arguments" do not uniformly produce stronger persuasion.

**warning**: Persuasion Theory's two routes are often described as discrete; the original ELM frames them as poles of a continuum, and the simplistic "central good / peripheral bad" gloss misses that peripheral processing is normatively appropriate when ability to process is genuinely limited — a nuance that "high-road persuasion" advocacy in applied settings frequently elides.

## Social Proof

- secondary_domains: [social-influence, behavioral-economics]
- aliases: [informational social influence]
- broader: [social-influence]
- related: [conformity, herd-behavior, descriptive-norms, cialdini-robert, nudge-theory, choice-architecture]
- prerequisites: [social-influence]

**definition**: Social Proof, as catalogued by Robert Cialdini, is the principle that individuals look to the behavior of others — particularly similar others, and particularly under uncertainty — as evidence about the appropriate or correct course of action, producing conformity that is informational (others know something I don't) rather than normative (I want to fit in).

**key_claim**: Social Proof produces measurable behavior shifts at population scale: descriptive-norm interventions ("most people in your neighborhood conserve water") routinely outperform informational appeals about the right thing to do, which has made social-proof messaging a workhorse of behavioral public-policy practice from energy conservation to tax compliance.

**warning**: Social Proof can backfire through *boomerang effects* when descriptive norms are used to discourage common behaviors — telling people most others over-consume signals that over-consumption is normal — so applied social-proof interventions require pairing the descriptive norm with an injunctive norm (approval/disapproval signal) to avoid licensing the very behavior they intend to suppress.

## In Group Out Group Bias

- secondary_domains: [intergroup-relations, social-cognition]
- aliases: [in-group favoritism, intergroup bias]
- broader: [social-identity-theory]
- related: [social-identity-theory, self-categorization-theory, minimal-group-paradigm, prejudice, stereotype-formation, contact-hypothesis]
- prerequisites: [social-identity-theory]

**definition**: In Group Out Group Bias is the systematic tendency to evaluate, allocate resources to, and trust members of one's own social category more favorably than members of other categories — emerging robustly even when group membership is arbitrary and short-lived, as in Tajfel's minimal-group experiments — and forming the proximal mechanism behind much intergroup discrimination and conflict.

**key_claim**: In Group Out Group Bias is asymmetric in an important way: in-group favoritism appears more readily than out-group derogation, meaning that intergroup discrimination is more often a consequence of *preferential treatment of one's own* than of *active hostility toward others* — a distinction that has consequences for which interventions (resource-allocation rules vs. attitude-change campaigns) are appropriately targeted.

**warning**: In Group Out Group Bias is sometimes invoked to explain any intergroup difference in outcomes; structural and historical factors typically dominate population-level inequalities, and attributing disparities primarily to dyadic intergroup bias risks misallocating intervention effort away from the structural mechanisms that account for most variance.

## Social Facilitation

- secondary_domains: [social-influence, performance-research]
- aliases: [audience effects, Zajonc social facilitation]
- broader: [social-influence]
- related: [evaluation-apprehension, drive-theory, mere-presence, zajonc-robert, choking-under-pressure]
- prerequisites: [social-influence]

**definition**: Social Facilitation is the empirical pattern, formalized by Robert Zajonc, in which the mere presence of others enhances performance on simple or well-learned tasks (where the dominant response is correct) but impairs performance on complex or novel tasks (where the dominant response is likely incorrect), an effect attributed to physiological arousal that strengthens dominant responses.

**key_claim**: Social Facilitation links a robust performance effect (audience presence shifts outcomes) to a specific moderator (task dominance) that predicts the *direction* of the effect — making it one of the few social-influence findings with a quantitatively specified theoretical structure rather than a directional but unmoderated description, and giving practical implications for when audiences help vs. harm performance.

**warning**: Social Facilitation effects are reliable but small in modern meta-analyses, and the audience-effect literature has not cleanly resolved among competing mechanisms (mere-presence vs. evaluation apprehension vs. distraction-conflict); citing social facilitation as a clean confirmation of any single mechanism overreads the evidence.

## Stereotype Threat

- secondary_domains: [social-cognition, performance-research, education]
- aliases: [Steele-Aronson stereotype threat]
- broader: [stereotype-effects]
- related: [stereotype-formation, working-memory, self-efficacy, identity-safety, steele-claude]
- prerequisites: [stereotype-effects]

**definition**: Stereotype Threat, demonstrated by Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson, is the performance decrement that occurs when members of a negatively-stereotyped group are placed in a setting where the stereotype is salient and applicable to the task, attributed to working-memory consumption by stereotype-monitoring and emotion-regulation processes that compete with task-relevant cognition.

**key_claim**: Stereotype Threat provides a non-essentialist explanation for portions of the achievement gap on standardized tests — performance differences that disappear or shrink when the stereotype is rendered non-applicable (e.g., framing the test as non-diagnostic) — and shifts the explanatory burden from stable group differences to context-dependent situational factors.

**warning**: Stereotype Threat has been a focal target of the replication crisis: meta-analyses suggest publication-bias-corrected effect sizes substantially smaller than the original literature implied, and several pre-registered replications have failed to find the effect; treating stereotype threat as a settled, large-effect phenomenon overstates the current evidential consensus.

## Bystander Effect

- secondary_domains: [social-influence, prosocial-behavior]
- aliases: [Latane-Darley bystander effect, diffusion of responsibility]
- broader: [prosocial-behavior]
- related: [diffusion-of-responsibility, pluralistic-ignorance, latane-bibb, darley-john, prosocial-intervention]
- prerequisites: [prosocial-behavior]

**definition**: The Bystander Effect, demonstrated by Latane and Darley following the Kitty Genovese case, is the empirical regularity that the likelihood of any single bystander intervening in an emergency *decreases* as the number of co-present bystanders increases, attributed to diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance about the seriousness of the event, and audience inhibition.

**key_claim**: The Bystander Effect demonstrated that prosocial action is not adequately predicted by individual moral character: situational factors — specifically the presence of other potential responders — exert effects that frequently dominate dispositional differences, which made the construct foundational to the situationist turn in social psychology.

**warning**: The Bystander Effect's most-cited illustrative case (the Kitty Genovese murder reported as having 38 witnesses who did nothing) has been substantially revised by historians; later evidence shows fewer witnesses and at least one intervention call, so the case study commonly used to motivate the effect is misleading even though the experimental evidence for the effect is genuine.

## Self Perception Theory

- secondary_domains: [attitude-formation, self-knowledge]
- aliases: [Bem self-perception theory]
- broader: [attitude-formation]
- related: [attitude-formation, cognitive-dissonance, bem-daryl, overjustification-effect, embodied-cognition]
- prerequisites: [attitude-formation]

**definition**: Self Perception Theory, proposed by Daryl Bem, holds that individuals come to know their own attitudes, emotions, and internal states partly by *observing their own behavior and the context in which it occurs* — making self-knowledge an inferential process much like other-perception, rather than direct introspective access to internal states.

**key_claim**: Self Perception Theory offered an alternative explanation for the dissonance-reduction findings without invoking aversive arousal: when external justification for a counter-attitudinal behavior is insufficient, individuals infer that they must hold the attitude consistent with the behavior — an inference, not a motivated reduction of discomfort. The central paradigms (e.g., Festinger-Carlsmith) are explained by either theory, and decades of research have shown the two operate in different conditions rather than one supplanting the other.

**warning**: Self Perception Theory is frequently overgeneralized to claim that all self-knowledge is post-hoc inference; the theory's strongest evidential domain is *attitudes and judgments about which the person has weak prior knowledge* — extending the framework to claim that introspection is generally illusory overreaches what the original work supports.
