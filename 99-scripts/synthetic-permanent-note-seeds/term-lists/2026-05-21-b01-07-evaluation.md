# Batch: B01-07 Evaluation Frameworks

## LLM Evaluation Benchmarks

- secondary_domains: [ml-benchmarking, nlp-evaluation, ai-safety]
- aliases: [LLM benchmarks, language model evaluation suites, NLP benchmarks for LLMs]
- broader: [llm-evaluation, ml-benchmarking]
- narrower: [mmlu-benchmark, hellaswag, truthfulqa, big-bench]
- related: [model-graded-evaluation, human-preference-evaluation, mt-bench-evaluation, arena-elo-ranking, promptbench-robustness]
- prerequisites: [large-language-models, machine-learning-evaluation, natural-language-processing]
- confidence: high

**definition**: LLM evaluation benchmarks are standardised datasets, tasks, and scoring protocols designed to measure specific capabilities of large language models — including reasoning (MMLU, BIG-Bench), commonsense knowledge (HellaSwag, WinoGrande), factual accuracy (TruthfulQA, Natural Questions), coding ability (HumanEval, SWE-Bench), mathematical reasoning (MATH, GSM8K), instruction following (IFEval), and safety (HarmBench, BeaverTails). Benchmarks provide reproducible, comparable measurements that track capability progress across model versions and facilitate research comparisons, though their relationship to real-world utility is imperfect and contested.

**key_claim**: Benchmark saturation is an accelerating problem in LLM evaluation — as models improve, state-of-the-art performance on widely used benchmarks approaches ceiling (100%), making discriminative comparison impossible; MMLU, which distinguishes well between models at the 50–85% accuracy range, becomes uninformative as frontier models exceed 90%, requiring continuous creation of harder benchmarks that stay ahead of model capability, raising the risk of benchmark overfitting in the creation process itself.

**warning**: LLM benchmark scores are contamination-prone — models are trained on web-crawled text that may include benchmark questions and answers, artificially inflating scores beyond what would be achieved on genuinely unseen test data; benchmark contamination is difficult to detect reliably and may systematically bias leaderboard rankings, particularly for benchmarks released before major training runs.

## Model-Graded Evaluation

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, ai-judges, automated-assessment]
- aliases: [LLM-as-judge, LLM judge evaluation, automated LLM evaluation, AI judge]
- broader: [llm-evaluation]
- narrower: [g-eval-framework, mt-bench-evaluation, arena-elo-ranking]
- related: [human-preference-evaluation, g-eval-framework, mt-bench-evaluation, self-evaluation-bias, evals-as-a-product]
- prerequisites: [llm-evaluation, large-language-models, evaluation-methodology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Model-graded evaluation (also called LLM-as-judge) is the practice of using a language model to automatically evaluate the quality of other language models' outputs, typically by providing the evaluating model with a rubric, a query, one or more candidate responses, and instructions to score or rank the responses. Pioneered as a scalable alternative to human evaluation, model-graded evaluation leverages frontier LLMs' broad knowledge and instruction-following capability to approximate human expert judgment at much lower cost and higher throughput. Evaluation formats include pairwise comparison (which of A or B is better?), absolute scoring (rate this response 1–10), and rubric-based assessment (score on specific criteria).

**key_claim**: Model-graded evaluation is now sufficiently reliable to substitute for human evaluation in many development contexts — frontier model judges achieve agreement with human preference labels at rates comparable to inter-human agreement on many tasks, making LLM-as-judge viable for rapid, scalable evaluation during development; however, the judge model's biases directly transfer to the evaluation results, making judge model selection and bias characterisation as important as evaluation design.

**warning**: Model-graded evaluation has systematic biases that are well-documented but difficult to fully mitigate: positional bias (preferring the first response in a pairwise comparison), verbosity bias (preferring longer responses regardless of quality), self-preference bias (models favouring outputs similar to their own generation style), and sycophancy bias (favouring responses that agree with the judge model's views); these biases can produce systematically misleading comparisons, particularly for model families the judge was not trained to evaluate fairly.

## Human Preference Evaluation

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, user-research, annotation]
- aliases: [human evaluation, preference annotation, crowdsourced evaluation, human rater evaluation]
- broader: [llm-evaluation]
- narrower: [arena-elo-ranking, comparative-human-evaluation]
- related: [model-graded-evaluation, arena-elo-ranking, reward-model-training, evals-as-a-product]
- prerequisites: [llm-evaluation, annotation-methodology, human-computer-interaction]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Human preference evaluation is the evaluation methodology in which human annotators assess language model outputs by comparing pairs of responses (which is better?) or rating individual responses against defined quality criteria (helpfulness, accuracy, harmlessness, coherence). Human preference evaluation is the gold standard for assessing the dimensions of output quality that are most important for end-user satisfaction — helpfulness, tone, clarity, and appropriateness — which are difficult to capture in automated metrics. Collected human preferences are used directly (to rank models or measure improvement) and indirectly (as training data for reward models in RLHF).

**key_claim**: Human preference evaluation is the most ecologically valid measure of production deployment quality but suffers from systematic annotator biases that are poorly controlled in practice — annotators consistently prefer confident-sounding answers over hedged accurate ones, longer responses over shorter ones, and responses that match their own prior beliefs over accurate corrections; these biases are the direct cause of many observed failures in RLHF-trained models, making annotator training and bias mitigation as important as annotation scale.

**warning**: Human preference evaluation results are highly sensitive to annotator pool composition — the same pair of responses can yield different preference rates across annotators from different educational, linguistic, and cultural backgrounds; this makes cross-study comparisons unreliable and means that preference evaluation results from one demographic context should not be assumed to generalise to others.

## Evals as a Product

- secondary_domains: [mlops, ai-safety, software-engineering]
- aliases: [eval framework design, evaluation infrastructure, eval pipelines as product]
- broader: [llm-evaluation, mlops]
- narrower: []
- related: [model-graded-evaluation, human-preference-evaluation, llm-evaluation-benchmarks, ab-testing-prompts, prompt-observability]
- prerequisites: [llm-evaluation, software-engineering, mlops]
- confidence: high

**definition**: "Evals as a product" is an AI development philosophy and organisational practice in which evaluation infrastructure is treated as a first-class software product — with dedicated engineering resources, version control, reproducibility guarantees, and continuous improvement — rather than as an ad-hoc annotation exercise performed once before deployment. Popularised by OpenAI's evals repository and Anthropic's model card evaluation methodology, the approach advocates for building eval suites that are maintained in parallel with model development, that cover a comprehensive set of capability and safety dimensions, and that provide fast, automated feedback loops during training and deployment.

**key_claim**: The quality of an AI system's evaluation infrastructure is a leading indicator of its safety and capability ceiling — teams that treat evals as a product systematically catch regressions earlier, measure safety improvements more precisely, and make better-informed model selection decisions than teams that rely on informal or ad-hoc evaluation; the practical implication is that investment in eval infrastructure yields returns throughout the model development lifecycle, not just at deployment time.

**warning**: Treating evals as a product creates an optimisation pressure against the evals themselves — when evaluation results are used to make product and deployment decisions, there is a systematic incentive to make evals easier to pass, to exclude problematic test cases, and to over-index on dimensions that the current model handles well; independent eval teams, external red-teaming, and public benchmark validation are necessary checks on internal eval system capture.

## G-Eval Framework

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, automated-assessment, nlp-research]
- aliases: [G-Eval, chain-of-thought evaluation, LLM chain-of-thought judge]
- broader: [model-graded-evaluation, llm-evaluation]
- narrower: []
- related: [model-graded-evaluation, human-preference-evaluation, mt-bench-evaluation, self-evaluation-bias]
- prerequisites: [model-graded-evaluation, chain-of-thought-prompting, llm-evaluation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: G-Eval is a framework for LLM-based evaluation of natural language generation (NLG) tasks that uses chain-of-thought to decompose the evaluation into explicit scoring steps before producing a final score, improving correlation with human judgements compared to direct rating. In G-Eval, the evaluating model is given a task definition, evaluation criteria, and one or more candidate texts; it first generates evaluation steps based on the criteria, then produces a probability-weighted score by computing the probability the model assigns to each score token (1–5) rather than sampling a single score, reducing the high variance of direct sampling-based evaluation. G-Eval was proposed by Liu et al. (2023) and showed stronger human correlation than existing automated metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore) on summarisation and dialogue tasks.

**key_claim**: G-Eval's probability weighting of score tokens addresses a fundamental variance problem in sampled LLM judge outputs — sampled scores from LLM judges have high variance because they are single samples from a complex distribution, while G-Eval's expected value over the scoring distribution produces a more stable and consistent numerical score that correlates better with human assessments; the CoT step further reduces variance by providing intermediate reasoning that anchors the score.

**warning**: G-Eval's correlation with human judgements is task-dependent and does not generalise uniformly — it shows stronger correlation with human judgements on summarisation and coherence dimensions than on factuality and grounding, precisely the dimensions where accurate evaluation is most critical for safety; using G-Eval as a proxy for human evaluation on factuality-sensitive tasks may provide false confidence in output quality.

## MT-Bench Evaluation

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, instruction-following-evaluation, conversational-ai]
- aliases: [MT-Bench, multi-turn benchmark, MT-bench LLM eval]
- broader: [model-graded-evaluation, llm-evaluation-benchmarks]
- narrower: []
- related: [model-graded-evaluation, g-eval-framework, arena-elo-ranking, human-preference-evaluation]
- prerequisites: [model-graded-evaluation, llm-evaluation, instruction-following]
- confidence: high

**definition**: MT-Bench is a multi-turn conversational evaluation benchmark developed by Zheng et al. (2023) at LMSYS that assesses language models' instruction-following and reasoning capabilities across 80 multi-turn question sets spanning eight categories: writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math, coding, knowledge, and STEM. Each question set consists of a first-turn question followed by a second-turn follow-up that tests the model's ability to maintain context, follow complex instructions, and refine or build on its first-turn response. MT-Bench uses GPT-4 as the judge model, with per-category scoring on a 1–10 scale, and results correlate well with Chatbot Arena human preference rankings.

**key_claim**: MT-Bench's multi-turn structure is its key discriminative advantage over single-turn benchmarks — many models that perform well on single-turn instruction following degrade significantly when required to maintain context across turns or follow complex turn-2 instructions that presuppose the first-turn response, making multi-turn performance a better predictor of real-world assistant utility than single-turn performance.

**warning**: MT-Bench is a relatively small benchmark (80 questions) with GPT-4 as the sole judge, which creates two limitations: the small question set means results can vary substantially between runs (high variance), and using GPT-4 as judge introduces GPT-4's specific biases (positional bias, verbosity preference, and self-preference relative to OpenAI-family models) that may disadvantage non-OpenAI-family models.

## Arena Elo Ranking

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, crowdsourced-evaluation, competitive-ranking]
- aliases: [Chatbot Arena, Elo ranking for LLMs, LMSYS Arena, crowdsourced LLM ranking]
- broader: [human-preference-evaluation, llm-evaluation]
- narrower: []
- related: [human-preference-evaluation, model-graded-evaluation, mt-bench-evaluation, evals-as-a-product]
- prerequisites: [elo-rating-system, llm-evaluation, human-preference-evaluation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Arena Elo ranking is a crowdsourced evaluation methodology for ranking large language models using the Elo rating system, implemented in the LMSYS Chatbot Arena platform. Users interact with two anonymous models simultaneously on their own queries and vote for the better response; Elo scores are computed from accumulated pairwise preference votes, updating both models' ratings after each comparison using the Elo update formula. With millions of human preference votes across thousands of model pairs, the Arena produces Elo rankings that are among the most ecologically valid comparative assessments of LLM quality, since they reflect real user preferences on user-generated queries rather than designed benchmarks.

**key_claim**: Arena Elo is the most ecologically valid large-scale comparative evaluation of LLMs available because it captures real user preferences on real user queries rather than task-designed benchmark questions — the Elo ranking reflects the aggregate of millions of genuine utility judgements rather than performance on a fixed test set, making it a better predictor of deployment success than most automated benchmarks.

**warning**: Arena Elo rankings are systematically biased toward models that are good at making favourable impressions on short interactions — models optimised for first-impression quality (confident tone, immediate helpfulness, engaging style) rank higher than models optimised for correctness on complex tasks, because the Arena's anonymous voting format encourages quick judgements rather than careful accuracy assessment; this bias means Elo is a poor ranking method for specialised or expert-use-case model selection.

## PromptBench Robustness

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, adversarial-nlp, prompt-engineering]
- aliases: [PromptBench, adversarial prompt robustness, prompt perturbation evaluation]
- broader: [llm-evaluation-benchmarks, llm-evaluation]
- narrower: []
- related: [llm-evaluation-benchmarks, red-teaming-llms, adversarial-suffix-attacks, prompt-engineering]
- prerequisites: [llm-evaluation, adversarial-nlp, prompt-engineering]
- confidence: high

**definition**: PromptBench is an adversarial robustness evaluation framework for large language models introduced by Zhu et al. (2023) that measures the sensitivity of LLM performance to adversarial perturbations of input prompts at multiple linguistic levels: character level (typos, swaps), word level (synonym substitution, word deletion), sentence level (paraphrase, back-translation), and semantic level (meaning-preserving reformulation). By evaluating the same benchmark tasks under clean versus perturbed prompts, PromptBench quantifies how much prompt wording affects model performance — high sensitivity indicates low robustness and suggests that benchmark scores may be overly dependent on the specific phrasing used.

**key_claim**: PromptBench reveals that LLM benchmark performance is substantially more sensitive to prompt phrasing than previously assumed — the same model on the same task can vary by 10–30 percentage points depending on superficial prompt variations that do not change the underlying task, indicating that reported benchmark scores implicitly reflect not only the model's capabilities but also the evaluator's prompt engineering skill.

**warning**: PromptBench's adversarial perturbations are designed to be meaning-preserving, but many perturbations at the character and word level change the distribution in ways that may genuinely require different capabilities from the model rather than testing the same capability under noise; what looks like robustness failure may sometimes be a model correctly identifying that a typo-laden prompt is a different input from the clean version.

## Faithfulness vs Factuality

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, rag-evaluation, information-verification]
- aliases: [faithfulness versus factuality, grounding vs accuracy, hallucination dimensions]
- broader: [llm-evaluation, hallucination-in-llms]
- narrower: []
- related: [llm-evaluation-benchmarks, model-graded-evaluation, self-evaluation-bias, retrieval-as-external-memory, hallucination-in-llms]
- prerequisites: [llm-evaluation, information-retrieval, knowledge-grounded-generation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Faithfulness versus factuality is a critical distinction in the evaluation of language model outputs that separates two different correctness dimensions: faithfulness (whether the output accurately reflects the provided source context — is it grounded in what was given?) and factuality (whether the output accurately reflects world knowledge — is it true?). A response can be faithful but not factual (accurately quoting an incorrect source), factual but not faithful (providing correct information not in the source document), both, or neither. This distinction is crucial for RAG system evaluation, where faithfulness to the retrieved context and factuality of the final answer are separate quality dimensions requiring separate evaluation methods.

**key_claim**: The faithfulness/factuality distinction resolves a systematic evaluation error in RAG systems — evaluating only factuality does not detect hallucinations that fabricate plausible-sounding information not in the retrieved context (faithfulness failures), while evaluating only faithfulness does not detect cases where the model correctly ignored an incorrect source and answered from world knowledge (factuality without faithfulness); both dimensions are necessary for complete quality assessment.

**warning**: The faithfulness/factuality distinction creates a practical dilemma for RAG system design when a retrieved source is factually incorrect — a faithfully grounded response will reproduce the source's error, while a factually correct response will contradict the source; current evaluation frameworks have no consensus on whether faithful-but-wrong or unfaithful-but-correct is preferable, and user expectations vary by domain (medical/legal: faithfulness to source; general knowledge: factual accuracy).

## Self-Evaluation Bias

- secondary_domains: [llm-evaluation, cognitive-biases-in-ai, model-graded-evaluation]
- aliases: [self-preference bias, self-assessment bias, LLM self-evaluation bias, narcissistic evaluation]
- broader: [model-graded-evaluation, llm-evaluation]
- narrower: []
- related: [model-graded-evaluation, g-eval-framework, sycophancy-mitigation, human-preference-evaluation]
- prerequisites: [model-graded-evaluation, llm-evaluation, cognitive-biases]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Self-evaluation bias in LLMs refers to the documented tendency of language models to rate or prefer their own outputs (or outputs from models with similar generation styles) more highly than outputs from different model families when acting as evaluation judges. Empirically, models acting as judges assign higher scores to responses that match their own characteristic style (sentence structure, verbosity level, vocabulary), reasoning patterns (e.g., chain-of-thought style), and response conventions (e.g., disclaimer style) — even when human raters do not prefer those stylistic choices. Self-evaluation bias corrupts model-graded evaluation when the judge model and the evaluated model share a generation distribution.

**key_claim**: Self-evaluation bias makes within-family model evaluation unreliable — using GPT-4 to evaluate GPT-4o versus Claude will systematically favour the GPT family output regardless of objective quality, and similarly for any model acting as judge when evaluating its own family versus competitors; using multiple diverse judge models and reporting ensemble evaluations is necessary to reduce judge-model bias in comparative assessments.

**warning**: Self-evaluation bias is particularly pernicious because it is difficult to detect from model-graded scores alone — inflated scores for similar models look like genuine high quality to anyone interpreting the results without access to the underlying judge preferences; independent human validation of a sample of judge decisions is necessary to quantify the magnitude of self-evaluation bias in any specific evaluation setup.
