# Batch: Language and Cognition

## Grice Maxims

- domain: language-and-cognition
- secondary_domains: [pragmatics, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [Gricean maxims, conversational maxims, cooperative principle]
- broader: [pragmatics]
- related: [pragmatic-inference, speech-acts, relevance-theory, cooperative-principle]
- prerequisites: [pragmatics]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Grice Maxims are the four conversational sub-principles — Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner — articulated by H. P. Grice as the operational specification of his Cooperative Principle, which governs how rational interlocutors are expected to contribute to a shared communicative exchange.

**key_claim**: The central explanatory move of the Grice Maxims is that conversational meaning routinely outruns sentence meaning: when a speaker visibly flouts a Grice Maxim while remaining cooperative, the listener is licensed to derive a "conversational implicature" that reconciles the apparent violation with the assumption of cooperation.

**warning**: The Grice Maxims have been criticized as culturally parochial and underspecified; Quantity and Manner in particular admit no precise metric, and cross-cultural pragmatics work has shown that what counts as cooperative contribution varies in ways the Grice Maxims framework treats as universal.

## Pragmatic Inference

- domain: language-and-cognition
- secondary_domains: [pragmatics, psycholinguistics]
- aliases: [conversational implicature, pragmatic enrichment]
- broader: [pragmatics]
- related: [grice-maxims, relevance-theory, theory-of-mind, scalar-implicature]
- prerequisites: [grice-maxims, theory-of-mind]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Pragmatic Inference is the cognitive process by which a comprehender derives intended meaning that goes beyond the linguistically encoded content of an utterance, exploiting context, shared knowledge, and assumptions about the speaker's communicative intentions.

**key_claim**: Pragmatic Inference is not a late, optional embellishment on semantic processing but appears to be incrementally interleaved with parsing: eye-tracking and ERP studies show that scalar implicatures and other Pragmatic Inferences are computed within hundreds of milliseconds, suggesting a tight coupling between literal decoding and pragmatic enrichment.

**warning**: Pragmatic Inference is sometimes treated as a uniform capacity, but its profile dissociates across populations and contexts; autistic individuals, for example, often pass Theory of Mind tasks while showing atypical patterns on specific Pragmatic Inferences, indicating that the construct masks several distinguishable mechanisms.

## Prototype Theory of Concepts

- domain: language-and-cognition
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, semantics]
- aliases: [Rosch prototype theory, prototype semantics]
- broader: [theories-of-concepts]
- related: [exemplar-theory, basic-level-categories, family-resemblance, classical-theory-of-concepts]
- prerequisites: [categorization]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Prototype Theory of Concepts, developed primarily by Eleanor Rosch, holds that natural categories are mentally represented around a central "prototype" — a most-typical exemplar or feature cluster — with category membership determined by graded similarity to that prototype rather than by satisfaction of necessary and sufficient conditions.

**key_claim**: The Prototype Theory of Concepts predicts and explains the robust empirical finding of typicality effects: subjects rate "robin" as a better example of "bird" than "penguin," verify category membership faster for typical than atypical instances, and produce typical members first in free listing — all anomalies under the classical definitional view of concepts.

**warning**: The Prototype Theory of Concepts struggles with compositionality; the prototype of "pet fish" is not derivable from the prototypes of "pet" and "fish," which is a serious problem because productive thought clearly composes concepts, and this gap motivated the development of exemplar and theory-theory accounts.

## Semantic Satiation

- domain: language-and-cognition
- secondary_domains: [psycholinguistics, attention]
- aliases: [word satiation, lexical satiation]
- broader: [word-recognition]
- related: [lexical-access, repetition-suppression, adaptation, neural-fatigue]
- prerequisites: [word-recognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Semantic Satiation is the phenomenon in which prolonged or rapid repetition of a word causes the word to temporarily lose its associated meaning for the perceiver, leaving the phonological or orthographic form intact while the semantic representation appears to become inaccessible.

**key_claim**: Semantic Satiation provides behavioral evidence that semantic activation is not a passive lookup but a fatigable neural process: repeated activation of the same lexical-semantic representation produces a measurable suppression effect that selectively impairs subsequent semantic-relatedness judgments while leaving formal lexical decisions relatively unaffected.

**warning**: Semantic Satiation is often described as "the word loses meaning," but this folk gloss overstates the effect; rigorous studies show the satiation is partial, transient, and confined to specific semantic-judgment tasks, and competing accounts dispute whether the locus is in semantic memory itself or in the comparison processes that operate over it.

## Speech Acts

- domain: language-and-cognition
- secondary_domains: [pragmatics, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [Austin speech act theory, illocutionary acts, Searle speech acts]
- broader: [pragmatics]
- related: [grice-maxims, illocutionary-force, performative-utterances, pragmatic-inference]
- prerequisites: [pragmatics]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Speech Acts, as analyzed by J. L. Austin and elaborated by John Searle, are utterances understood as actions — promising, ordering, warning, declaring — decomposed into the locutionary act (saying something), the illocutionary act (the action performed in saying it), and the perlocutionary act (the effect produced by saying it).

**key_claim**: Speech Acts theory's pivotal claim is that meaning is not exhausted by truth-conditions: many utterances such as "I promise" or "I name this ship" do not describe the world but constitute social actions whose felicity depends on conventional and institutional conditions, dissolving the descriptive-fallacy assumption that statements are paradigmatic of language use.

**warning**: Speech Acts taxonomy has been criticized as culturally and institutionally bound; Searle's five-fold classification presupposes Western legal and social conventions for declarations, commissives, and directives, and applying Speech Acts categories cross-culturally without adjustment risks importing those conventions as if they were features of language itself.
