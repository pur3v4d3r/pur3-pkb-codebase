# Batch: Developmental Psychology

## Eriksons Psychosocial Stages

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [personality-psychology, lifespan-development]
- aliases: [Erikson psychosocial development, eight stages of man]
- broader: [stage-theories-of-development]
- related: [identity-formation, generativity, psychoanalytic-theory, lifespan-development]
- prerequisites: [psychoanalytic-theory]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Eriksons Psychosocial Stages is the eight-stage lifespan model proposed by Erik Erikson in which personality development unfolds through a series of normative crises — from trust versus mistrust in infancy to integrity versus despair in old age — each defined by a tension whose resolution shapes ego strength.

**key_claim**: Eriksons Psychosocial Stages broke from orthodox Freudian theory by extending development beyond adolescence and reframing each stage around a social rather than purely sexual conflict, locating personality formation in the negotiation between the individual ego and culturally-mediated demands across the entire lifespan.

**warning**: Eriksons Psychosocial Stages has weak empirical support for its strict stage sequence and culturally-loaded content; the identity-versus-role-confusion stage in particular reflects mid-twentieth-century Western adolescent experience, and cross-cultural research finds that the timing and even the existence of Erikson's stages varies substantially across societies.

## Formal Operational Stage

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-development, education]
- aliases: [Piaget formal operations, abstract reasoning stage]
- broader: [piagetian-stages]
- related: [concrete-operational-stage, hypothetico-deductive-reasoning, abstract-thought, scientific-reasoning]
- prerequisites: [piagetian-stages, concrete-operational-stage]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: The Formal Operational Stage is the fourth and final stage in Jean Piaget's theory of cognitive development, beginning roughly at age eleven, characterized by the capacity for hypothetico-deductive reasoning, systematic manipulation of variables, and reasoning over propositions and possibilities rather than only over concrete objects.

**key_claim**: The Formal Operational Stage's distinguishing cognitive achievement is the inversion of the relation between the real and the possible: where the concrete-operational child reasons from what is actually present, the formal-operational reasoner treats the actual as one case within a space of logical possibilities, enabling combinatorial reasoning and the design of controlled experiments.

**warning**: The Formal Operational Stage is the most empirically contested of Piaget's stages; cross-cultural research finds that many adults from non-schooled populations fail standard formal-operational tasks, and even within Western samples performance is highly domain-specific, suggesting that the Formal Operational Stage is better understood as a learned skillset tied to formal education than as a universal developmental endpoint.

## Moral Development

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [moral-psychology, ethics]
- aliases: [Kohlberg stages, moral reasoning development]
- broader: [moral-psychology]
- related: [theory-of-mind, perspective-taking, ethics-of-care, social-domain-theory]
- prerequisites: [perspective-taking]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Moral Development refers to the age-graded changes in how people reason about and respond to ethical problems, most influentially modeled by Lawrence Kohlberg as a six-stage progression from preconventional self-interest, through conventional rule-following, to postconventional principled reasoning.

**key_claim**: Kohlberg's framework for Moral Development claims that the sequence of stages is invariant and culturally universal because higher stages are structurally more adequate at coordinating perspectives, not merely more socially approved — making the model a structural-developmental theory rather than a description of cultural moral content.

**warning**: Moral Development as Kohlberg operationalized it has been seriously challenged: Carol Gilligan argued the framework systematically devalues a "care" orientation more common in women, Jonathan Haidt's social-intuitionist work shows reasoning often rationalizes prior intuitions rather than driving judgment, and cross-cultural research finds the postconventional stage is rarely reached outside Western liberal samples.

## Object Permanence

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-development, infant-cognition]
- aliases: [Piaget object permanence, A-not-B error]
- broader: [piagetian-stages, sensorimotor-stage]
- related: [a-not-b-error, core-knowledge, violation-of-expectation, working-memory]
- prerequisites: [sensorimotor-stage]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Object Permanence is the understanding that objects continue to exist when they are no longer perceptible — out of sight, hearing, or touch — identified by Piaget as a major sensorimotor-stage achievement attained gradually across the first two years of life.

**key_claim**: Subsequent infant-cognition research using violation-of-expectation paradigms has dramatically revised the Object Permanence timeline: looking-time studies suggest infants as young as three to four months represent occluded objects, indicating that Piaget's reaching-based tasks underestimated competence by conflating Object Permanence with the motor and inhibitory demands of search.

**warning**: The mature Object Permanence literature is now divided over whether early looking-time successes reflect genuine conceptual representation of hidden objects or low-level perceptual mechanisms; treating Object Permanence as either fully present at three months or fully absent before eight months ignores the dissociation between recognition and action that the data actually show.

## Theory of Mind

- domain: developmental-psychology
- secondary_domains: [social-cognition, autism-research]
- aliases: [ToM, mentalizing, mindreading]
- broader: [social-cognition]
- related: [false-belief-task, perspective-taking, autism, pragmatic-inference]
- prerequisites: [perspective-taking]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Theory of Mind is the cognitive capacity to attribute unobservable mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge — to oneself and others, and to use those attributions to explain and predict behavior, classically operationalized via the false-belief task in which a child must predict an actor's behavior based on the actor's incorrect belief.

**key_claim**: Theory of Mind has been argued to undergo a discrete developmental transition around age four, when children begin to pass standard false-belief tasks; this finding shaped decades of research, but newer implicit measures using anticipatory looking suggest that an earlier non-verbal Theory of Mind may be present in infancy, raising the question of whether the four-year shift reflects representational change or executive-function maturation.

**warning**: Theory of Mind is frequently treated as a unitary trait that one either has or lacks, but the construct fractionates: cognitive perspective-taking, affective empathy, and spontaneous mentalizing dissociate empirically, and labeling autistic individuals as having "no Theory of Mind" misrepresents a heterogeneous profile in which some Theory of Mind components are intact while others differ from neurotypical patterns.
