# Batch: Learning Strategies

## Mnemonic Strategies

- secondary_domains: [memory, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [mnemonic devices, mnemonics]
- broader: [learning-strategies]
- related: [elaborative-encoding, retrieval-structure, dual-coding-theory]
- prerequisites: [encoding-depth]

**definition**: Mnemonic Strategies are deliberate encoding techniques — including the method of loci, peg systems, acronyms, keyword links, and chunking schemes — that impose pre-existing organized structure on otherwise arbitrary material so that the new content inherits the retrieval routes of the structure.

**key_claim**: Mnemonic Strategies work not because they make material easier but because they substitute organized retrieval cues for absent ones, which is why their effects scale with how much native structure the target material lacks rather than with how clever the mnemonic feels.

**warning**: Mnemonic Strategies excel at preserved-form recall (lists, sequences, vocabulary) but transfer poorly to conceptual flexibility; relying on mnemonics for material whose value depends on flexible reasoning produces high recall accuracy alongside low understanding.

## Pre-Testing Effect

- secondary_domains: [memory, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [prequestion effect, pretesting effect]
- broader: [testing-effect]
- related: [retrieval-practice, generation-effect, productive-failure]
- prerequisites: [testing-effect]

**definition**: The Pre-Testing Effect is the empirical finding that attempting to answer questions about material before studying it — even when the answers are unknown and the responses are wrong — improves later memory for the correct answers compared to studying the material directly.

**key_claim**: The Pre-Testing Effect implies that the act of unsuccessful retrieval primes the learner's encoding of the eventual correct answer, which is why "failed" pretests are not wasted effort but a form of preparatory encoding scaffolding.

**warning**: The Pre-Testing Effect is sometimes confused with the testing effect proper, but the two operate at different points in the study cycle; treating pre-testing as a substitute for post-study retrieval practice forfeits the larger effect of post-encoding retrieval.

## Productive Struggle

- secondary_domains: [instructional-design, mathematics-education]
- broader: [desirable-difficulties]
- related: [productive-failure, scaffolding, the-zone-of-proximal-development, cognitive-load-theory]
- prerequisites: [desirable-difficulties]

**definition**: Productive Struggle is the instructional design principle — anchored in mathematics-education research — that learners should work on problems that are within reach but require effortful, partly-unsuccessful problem-solving before instruction is given, because the struggle itself prepares the cognitive ground for the subsequent explanation.

**key_claim**: Productive Struggle reframes "the explanation" as a complement to prior effortful failure rather than as a substitute for it, which inverts the default lecture-then-practice sequence and predicts that pre-instruction struggle produces deeper schemata than post-instruction practice alone.

**warning**: Productive Struggle becomes counter-productive when the gap between learner capacity and task demand is too large; without scaffolding it collapses into unproductive frustration, and the principle's effectiveness depends on a careful read of the learner's current zone of proximal development.

## Observational Learning

- secondary_domains: [social-cognition, developmental-psychology]
- aliases: [modeling, vicarious learning]
- broader: [social-cognitive-theory]
- related: [vicarious-experience, cognitive-apprenticeship, communities-of-practice]
- prerequisites: [social-cognitive-theory]

**definition**: Observational Learning is Bandura's account of how new behaviors and cognitive patterns are acquired by attending to a model's actions and outcomes — without direct reinforcement of the observer — through the four sub-processes of attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation.

**key_claim**: Observational Learning is the mechanism by which culture-level skill is transmitted faster than individual trial-and-error could account for, and it shows that reinforcement is not required for acquisition — only for performance — which separates learning from doing more sharply than behaviorist accounts allow.

**warning**: Observational Learning is often misread as "watching is enough"; the retention and reproduction sub-processes require active rehearsal, and a learner who observes without rehearsing acquires a recognition-grade representation that fails when production is required.

## Worked-Example Variability

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-load-theory, instructional-design]
- aliases: [variability of worked examples]
- broader: [worked-examples]
- related: [the-worked-example-effect, far-transfer, schema-construction, cognitive-load-theory]
- prerequisites: [worked-examples]

**definition**: Worked-Example Variability is the cognitive-load-theory principle that worked examples drawn from diverse surface contexts but sharing the same underlying solution structure produce stronger and more transferable schemata than the same number of examples drawn from a narrow context, because the variation forces abstraction over surface features.

**key_claim**: Worked-Example Variability supplies the transfer mechanism that pure example-multiplication lacks: the schema constructed across varied examples is necessarily framed in terms of the deep structure they share, which is precisely the representation transfer requires.

**warning**: Worked-Example Variability incurs additional intrinsic load and is counter-productive for novices whose working memory cannot yet hold the to-be-abstracted invariant; the principle is reliably beneficial only once a baseline schema is in place to anchor the variation.

## Concept Mapping

- secondary_domains: [knowledge-representation, instructional-design]
- aliases: [Novakian concept mapping]
- broader: [knowledge-graph]
- related: [mind-mapping, schema-construction, meaningful-learning-theory]
- prerequisites: [meaningful-learning-theory]

**definition**: Concept Mapping is Joseph Novak's externalization technique — grounded in Ausubel's meaningful-learning theory — in which the learner draws concept nodes connected by labeled propositional edges, producing a hierarchical graph that makes the learner's current understanding visible and revisable.

**key_claim**: Concept Mapping derives its learning value not from the resulting diagram but from the production process: the requirement to assign explicit labels to every link forces the learner to commit to an interpretation of each relationship, exposing implicit confusions that prose hides.

**warning**: Concept Mapping is often degraded into mind-mapping-with-extra-steps when learners draw nodes and edges without labeling the relations; the unlabeled graph forfeits the propositional discipline that distinguishes concept maps from generic visual organizers.

## Mind Mapping

- secondary_domains: [creativity, notetaking]
- aliases: [mind map, idea mapping]
- broader: [externalized-cognitive-architecture]
- related: [concept-mapping, brainstorming, divergent-thinking]
- prerequisites: [externalized-cognitive-architecture]

**definition**: Mind Mapping is Tony Buzan's radial diagramming technique in which a central topic anchors successively branching associations, with each branch carrying a single keyword and visual emphasis, producing a fan-shaped externalization of associative thinking that prioritizes generation over organization.

**key_claim**: Mind Mapping is best understood as a divergent-thinking aid rather than a knowledge-representation format; its value lies in lowering the activation threshold for associations during ideation, not in producing a defensible structure of the topic.

**warning**: Mind Mapping is routinely sold as equivalent to concept mapping, but the absence of labeled relations and the imposed radial geometry produce diagrams that look organized while encoding only proximity — confusing the two methods leads to choosing the wrong tool for the cognitive task at hand.

## Summarization Strategy

- secondary_domains: [reading-comprehension, study-skills]
- aliases: [summarization, summary writing]
- broader: [learning-strategies]
- related: [self-explanation, elaborative-interrogation, progressive-summarization]
- prerequisites: [comprehension-monitoring]

**definition**: The Summarization Strategy is the study technique of writing a condensed restatement of a text in one's own words, capturing main ideas and their relationships, and is consistently rated as a moderately effective comprehension and retention strategy when summaries are written rather than copied.

**key_claim**: The Summarization Strategy works through forced selection and reformulation: the learner must decide what to keep and how to recompose it, which is a comprehension test masquerading as a study activity and exposes failures of understanding that re-reading would conceal.

**warning**: The Summarization Strategy collapses to ineffective highlighting when learners produce summaries by stitching together verbatim sentences from the source; without the reformulation step, the strategy degrades into a search-and-paste exercise that does not engage the comprehension processes that give it its effect.

## Question Generation Strategy

- secondary_domains: [reading-comprehension, study-skills]
- aliases: [self-questioning, question generation]
- broader: [learning-strategies]
- related: [elaborative-interrogation, self-explanation, comprehension-monitoring]
- prerequisites: [comprehension-monitoring]

**definition**: The Question Generation Strategy is the study technique in which the learner formulates their own questions about material as they read or study, ranging from factual recall to inferential and integrative questions, and then attempts to answer them — which engages both comprehension monitoring and retrieval practice in a single move.

**key_claim**: The Question Generation Strategy outperforms passive reading because question construction requires the learner to identify what is important and what is unclear, two judgments that re-reading systematically defers and that are precisely the levers of metacognitive accuracy.

**warning**: The Question Generation Strategy produces little benefit when the questions generated are uniformly factual; the strategy's transfer-relevant gains come from inferential and integrative questions, and learners must be explicitly coached toward higher-order question types or they default to surface ones.

## Faded Worked Examples

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-load-theory, instructional-design]
- aliases: [example-problem fading, completion problems]
- broader: [worked-examples]
- related: [worked-examples, the-worked-example-effect, scaffolded-fading, cognitive-load-theory]
- prerequisites: [worked-examples]

**definition**: Faded Worked Examples are an instructional sequence in which the learner is given full worked examples first, then partial worked examples with progressively more steps left blank for the learner to complete, until full problem-solving is required — a deliberate transfer of cognitive load from the example to the learner.

**key_claim**: Faded Worked Examples solve the expertise-reversal problem of static worked examples by making the example structure adaptive: as schema construction progresses, the support is withdrawn at the rate the learner can absorb, which is the bridge between worked-example efficacy for novices and problem-solving efficacy for intermediates.

**warning**: Faded Worked Examples lose their advantage when the fading schedule is fixed rather than responsive to learner performance; pre-set fading sequences re-create the expertise-reversal problem at a finer granularity, fading too fast for some learners and too slow for others.
