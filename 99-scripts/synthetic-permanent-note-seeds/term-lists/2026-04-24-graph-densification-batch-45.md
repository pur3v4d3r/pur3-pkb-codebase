# Batch: Graph Densification — 45 Concepts

Top ghost-link concepts that cleanly fill structural gaps across the cognitive-science, motivational-psychology, educational-psychology, and developer-tooling clusters.

## Motivational Psychology

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, social-cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [motivation science, psychology of motivation]
- broader: [psychology]
- narrower: [self-determination-theory, achievement-goal-theory, expectancy-value-theory, attribution-theory, self-efficacy-theory, goal-setting-theory]
- related: [intrinsic-motivation, extrinsic-motivation, autonomous-motivation, controlled-motivation, basic-psychological-needs]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Motivational Psychology is the sub-discipline of psychology that studies the *why* of behaviour — the directional, energising, and persistence-related determinants of action — by modelling how needs, goals, expectations, values, and causal attributions interact to produce engagement, choice, effort, and withdrawal across achievement, social, and self-regulatory contexts. Motivational Psychology integrates competing meta-theories — drive reduction, [[expectancy-value-theory]], [[attribution-theory]], [[self-determination-theory]], [[achievement-goal-theory]], [[self-efficacy-theory]], and [[goal-setting-theory]] — into a layered account in which proximal cognitions ([[perceived-self-efficacy]], [[task-value]], [[causal-attribution]]) mediate distal antecedents (needs, climate, history) into observable behaviour.

**key_claim**: Motivational Psychology has converged, since the cognitive turn of the 1970s, on a multi-process consensus: motivation is not a unitary energiser but a *layered system* in which need-based ([[basic-psychological-needs]]), goal-based ([[goal-setting-theory]], [[achievement-goal-theory]]), expectancy-based ([[expectancy-value-theory]], [[self-efficacy-theory]]), and attribution-based ([[attribution-theory]]) processes operate concurrently and interact, so any single-construct intervention will leave most variance unexplained.

**warning**: Motivational Psychology is regularly conflated with motivational *speech* — generic exhortation and affirmation packaged as evidence-based practice — which inverts the field's actual discipline; the empirically supported levers ([[autonomy-supportive-structure]], [[implementation-intentions]], [[mastery-climate]], [[attribution-retraining]]) are structural and procedural, not rhetorical, and Motivational Psychology's clinical track record depends on respecting that distinction.

## Schema Theory

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [schema-theoretic account]
- broader: [cognitive-psychology]
- narrower: [schema, schema-construction, schema-automation, hierarchical-chunk-structure]
- related: [cognitive-load-theory, working-memory, long-term-memory, chunk, retrieval-structure, the-coordination-thesis-for-schema-construction]
- prerequisites: [working-memory, long-term-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Schema Theory is the family of cognitive accounts — originating with Bartlett (1932) and formalised by Rumelhart, Anderson, and the Sweller tradition — that treats long-term knowledge as organised into *schemas*: hierarchical, slot-and-filler structures that compress recurrent regularities of the world into single retrievable units, and that mediate perception, comprehension, problem-solving, and memory by supplying the prior structure into which incoming information is assimilated. Within [[cognitive-load-theory]], Schema Theory grounds the central claim that expertise is the substitution of [[working-memory]]-bound element processing with [[long-term-memory]]-resident schema retrieval.

**key_claim**: Schema Theory predicts that the principal bottleneck of learning is not memorisation of items but the *construction of schemas* — the binding of previously unrelated elements into a single unit that can be retrieved and manipulated as one — which is why instructional designs that target [[schema-construction]] (worked examples, isolated-elements sequencing, scaffolded fading) outperform those that target item recall, and why expertise development follows [[the-power-law-of-practice]].

**warning**: Schema Theory is frequently weakened into the slogan "activate prior knowledge before teaching new content," collapsing a mechanistic account of [[long-term-working-memory]] into a generic instructional preamble; the empirically substantive Schema Theory commitments — that schemas are *constructed* through [[element-interactivity]]-respecting practice and that premature retrieval of an under-built schema produces interference, not consolidation — survive only when the design discipline survives.

## Pattern Recognition

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [perception, expertise-research]
- aliases: [perceptual pattern recognition]
- broader: [cognitive-psychology, expertise-research]
- narrower: [chunk, hierarchical-chunk-structure]
- related: [schema, schema-construction, automaticity, long-term-working-memory, retrieval-structure, type-1-processing, dual-process-theory]
- prerequisites: [long-term-memory, working-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Pattern Recognition is the cognitive process by which a perceptual or symbolic input is rapidly matched against stored representations — schemas, chunks, exemplars — to produce a categorisation, identification, or affordance reading without an exhaustive feature-by-feature comparison. In skilled domains, Pattern Recognition is the proximal mechanism through which expertise circumvents [[working-memory]] limits: the expert does not see "thirty-two pieces on a board" but a small set of meaningful patterns, each of which contains the relational structure necessary to drive action.

**key_claim**: Pattern Recognition is the dominant process by which experts outperform novices on domain tasks: chess-master perception studies, fire-ground command research, and medical-diagnosis literature converge on the finding that expert speed and accuracy derive primarily from [[schema]]-driven Pattern Recognition rather than from superior reasoning, deliberation, or working-memory capacity, making Pattern Recognition the operational signature of [[automaticity]].

**warning**: Pattern Recognition's reliability is bounded by the validity of the environment in which it was acquired ([[high-validity-environment]]); Pattern Recognition trained in low-validity domains — long-range geopolitical forecasting, stock-picking, novel medical syndromes — produces *fluency* indistinguishable from genuine expertise but predictively no better than chance, which is why Pattern Recognition must be diagnosed against the environment that produced it before being trusted as an expertise signal.

## Chunking

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, expertise-research]
- aliases: [perceptual chunking, knowledge chunking]
- broader: [schema-construction]
- narrower: [hierarchical-chunk-structure, chunk]
- related: [schema, automaticity, working-memory, long-term-working-memory, pattern-recognition, retrieval-structure, the-power-law-of-practice]
- prerequisites: [working-memory, long-term-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Chunking is the cognitive process by which several previously unrelated elements are bound into a single retrievable unit — a *chunk* — that thereafter occupies one slot of [[working-memory]] rather than several, allowing the freed capacity to be redeployed onto higher-order relations. First identified by Miller (1956) and operationalised by the Chase-and-Simon chess studies (1973), Chunking is the principal mechanism by which expertise expands the *effective* capacity of working memory without altering its raw slot count.

**key_claim**: Chunking is the proximal explanation for nearly every empirical signature of domain expertise: the expert's superior recall of structured but not random material, the expert's faster [[pattern-recognition]], and the expert's apparent ability to think strategically while novices struggle with surface features all reduce to the same cause — the expert's [[long-term-memory]] supplies pre-built chunks that bypass [[working-memory]] limits and compose into the [[hierarchical-chunk-structure]] that underwrites [[long-term-working-memory]].

**warning**: Chunking is often confused with mnemonic encoding — turning "1 4 9 2" into "1492" — but the substantive claim of Chunking research is structural, not mnemonic: a chunk is a *meaningful unit in long-term memory* whose retrieval restores the relations among its elements, and treating Chunking as a study trick rather than a [[schema-construction]] outcome produces cargo-cult learning protocols (acronyms without comprehension) that look like Chunking but deliver none of its expertise benefits.

## Autonomy Support

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, organizational-psychology]
- aliases: [autonomy-supportive teaching, autonomy-supportive parenting, autonomy-supportive style]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- narrower: [autonomy-supportive-structure, feedback-design-for-autonomy-and-mastery]
- related: [autonomy, autonomy-sdt, basic-psychological-needs, controlling-teaching-styles, internalization-continuum, identified-regulation, autonomy-structure-dialectic]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory, basic-psychological-needs]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Autonomy Support is, within [[self-determination-theory]], the interpersonal and structural practice of taking the other's perspective, acknowledging their feelings, providing rationale for requests, offering meaningful choice, and minimising controlling language and contingent reward — operationalised through a small set of teacher, manager, and clinician behaviours whose net effect is to satisfy the [[autonomy-sdt]] need and thereby support [[internalization]] rather than externally imposed compliance. Autonomy Support is contrasted with [[controlling-teaching-styles]] along the [[autonomy-structure-dialectic]] axis, not with structure or expectations.

**key_claim**: Autonomy Support reliably predicts more autonomous regulation, deeper engagement, higher conceptual learning, lower dropout, and better well-being across classroom, clinical, parenting, and workplace settings — a result robust enough that Autonomy Support has become one of the most replicable causal motivational interventions in field psychology, with effects mediated by [[basic-psychological-needs]] satisfaction rather than by reward contingencies.

**warning**: Autonomy Support is widely misread as *permissiveness* — the abandonment of structure, expectation, or evaluation — but the [[autonomy-structure-dialectic]] is explicit that Autonomy Support and structure are orthogonal, not opposed: the autonomy-supportive teacher provides clear expectations, informative feedback, and explicit consequences alongside choice and rationale, and removing structure under the banner of Autonomy Support produces the very chaos critics use to discredit [[self-determination-theory]].

## Constructivist Learning Theory

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [constructivism, constructivist epistemology of learning]
- broader: [learning-science]
- narrower: [the-coordination-thesis-for-schema-construction]
- related: [schema-theory, schema-construction, productive-failure, scaffolding, zone-of-proximal-development, andragogy, self-directed-learning]
- prerequisites: [schema-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Constructivist Learning Theory is the family of educational and cognitive accounts — drawing on Piaget, Vygotsky, Bruner, and Papert — that holds that knowledge is not transmitted intact from teacher to learner but *constructed* by the learner through the active assimilation and accommodation of new experience into existing [[schema]] structures. Constructivist Learning Theory ranges from radical (Piagetian, individual-cognitive) to social (Vygotskian, mediated by interaction within the [[zone-of-proximal-development]]) variants but converges on the design implication that instruction must engineer *opportunities for construction* rather than deliver compressed propositions.

**key_claim**: Constructivist Learning Theory makes the strong claim that the *quality of construction effort* — the [[germane-cognitive-load]] devoted to integrating new elements with existing schemas — is the principal determinant of durable learning, which is why instructional formats that reduce such effort to comfortable consumption (lecture-heavy, fluency-illusion-prone) reliably produce shallow performance no matter how engaging or efficient they appear.

**warning**: Constructivist Learning Theory is repeatedly invoked to justify *minimally guided instruction* — discovery learning, pure inquiry, unscaffolded problem-based learning — but the Kirschner-Sweller-Clark (2006) critique established that for novices, minimal guidance violates the very [[cognitive-load-theory]] constraints that Constructivist Learning Theory acknowledges; the defensible Constructivist Learning Theory commitment is to *guided* construction (worked examples that fade, scaffolding that withdraws), not to the absence of instruction.

## Heuristics and Biases

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [decision-research, behavioural-economics]
- aliases: [heuristics-and-biases program, Kahneman-Tversky program]
- broader: [decision-research, dual-process-theory]
- narrower: [attribute-substitution, fundamental-attribution-error-correspondence-bias, fluency-illusion]
- related: [bounded-rationality, type-1-processing, type-2-processing, dual-process-theory, cognitive-miserliness, mindware, default-interventionist-architecture]
- prerequisites: [dual-process-theory, working-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Heuristics and Biases is the research program — initiated by Tversky and Kahneman in the early 1970s and consolidated in Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* — that documents how human judgment under uncertainty systematically departs from the prescriptions of probability theory by relying on a small set of mental shortcuts (representativeness, availability, anchoring, [[attribute-substitution]]) whose ecological successes in routine tasks coexist with reproducible failures in formally structured ones. Heuristics and Biases is the empirical foundation on which [[dual-process-theory]] and [[bounded-rationality]] were elaborated.

**key_claim**: Heuristics and Biases established the descriptively decisive finding that bias is not a property of *uneducated* or *unmotivated* judges but a structural feature of [[type-1-processing]] that even trained statisticians, physicians, and judges fail to override without explicit [[mindware]] and [[cognitive-forcing-functions]] — which is why awareness of Heuristics and Biases without procedural countermeasures rarely changes behaviour.

**warning**: Heuristics and Biases is often misread as a wholesale indictment of intuition, but the program's mature position — Kahneman-Klein (2009), the [[high-validity-environment]] criterion — is more nuanced: Heuristics and Biases predicts systematic failure in low-validity domains and predicts reliable expert performance in high-validity ones, so the operational lesson is to diagnose the environment before trusting or distrusting an intuitive judgment, not to globally privilege [[type-2-processing]].

## Instructional Design

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [ID, learning design, instructional systems design]
- broader: [educational-psychology]
- narrower: [four-component-instructional-design-4c-id, scaffolding, scaffolded-fading, backward-design, curriculum-design]
- related: [cognitive-load-theory, worked-examples, the-worked-example-effect, expertise-reversal-effect, schema-construction, complex-learning, the-just-in-time-principle]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-load-theory, schema-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Instructional Design is the discipline that engineers learning environments — sequences of tasks, supports, feedback, and assessments — to maximise the probability that intended learning outcomes are achieved by the intended learners under the constraints of [[working-memory]], prior knowledge, and motivational ecology. Instructional Design draws on [[cognitive-load-theory]] for its load-management principles, on [[schema-theory]] for its acquisition model, and on [[scaffolding]] / [[scaffolded-fading]] for its support trajectory, and is operationalised in mature frameworks such as [[four-component-instructional-design-4c-id]].

**key_claim**: Instructional Design as a discipline is distinguished from generic teaching by its commitment to *empirical falsifiability*: a design is judged not by its surface plausibility or learner enjoyment but by measurable transfer to authentic post-instruction tasks, which is why Instructional Design has converged on principles ([[worked-examples]], [[the-just-in-time-principle]], [[isolated-elements-effect]]) that remain robust across decades of replication while pedagogical fashion churns.

**warning**: Instructional Design is regularly equated with the surface artifacts of e-learning — slide decks, quizzes, LMS modules — collapsing the discipline into a content-production craft; the substantive Instructional Design commitments are diagnostic (assess prior knowledge), structural (sequence by [[element-interactivity]]), and adaptive (fade scaffolds along the [[expertise-reversal-effect]] gradient), and a "well-designed course" that ignores them is Instructional Design in name only.

## Implicit Theories of Intelligence

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, social-cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [implicit theories, mindset theory, lay theories of ability]
- broader: [motivational-psychology, conception-of-ability]
- narrower: [growth-mindset, the-developmental-differentiation-of-effort-and-ability]
- related: [growth-mindset, achievement-goal-theory, attribution-theory, learned-helplessness, conception-of-ability, mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation]
- prerequisites: [conception-of-ability]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Implicit Theories of Intelligence are the lay belief systems — entity (fixed) vs. incremental ([[growth-mindset]]) — that learners and observers hold about the *nature* of cognitive ability: whether intelligence is a stable, largely inherited trait that is measured by performance, or a malleable capacity that is *developed* by effort, strategy, and instruction. Carol Dweck's research program established Implicit Theories of Intelligence as a *meaning system* whose orientation organises a downstream cluster of goals, attributions, and responses to setback.

**key_claim**: Implicit Theories of Intelligence function as motivational frames rather than as discrete beliefs: holding the incremental theory predicts mastery-approach goal selection ([[achievement-goal-theory]]), [[causal-attribution]] of failure to controllable factors, persistence under aversive feedback, and resilience against [[learned-helplessness]], whereas holding the entity theory predicts the inverse cluster — and the predictive power of Implicit Theories of Intelligence is greatest precisely under conditions of difficulty and challenge.

**warning**: Implicit Theories of Intelligence interventions have been widely diluted into "praise the process" slogans whose field-scale effects are far smaller than the original lab studies suggested; treating Implicit Theories of Intelligence as a one-shot motivational message — without restructuring the [[mastery-climate]], the assessment regime, and the feedback ecology that the entity theory exploits — typically produces null or trivial effects and risks training learners to *display* a growth mindset rather than internalise the incremental theory.

## Spaced Repetition

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, memory-research]
- aliases: [spaced practice, distributed practice]
- broader: [retrieval-practice, desirable-difficulties]
- narrower: []
- related: [spacing-effect, retrieval-practice, retrieval-practice-as-the-most-potent-single-strategy, desirable-difficulties, testing-effect, fluency-illusion]
- prerequisites: [retrieval-practice, long-term-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Spaced Repetition is the systematic scheduling of [[retrieval-practice]] across expanding time intervals — typically days to weeks rather than minutes — designed to exploit the [[spacing-effect]] by forcing each rehearsal to occur after a degree of forgetting has occurred, thereby producing more durable consolidation than the same number of rehearsals delivered in a massed (single-session) schedule. Modern Spaced Repetition is operationalised in algorithms (SM-2, FSRS) that adjust each item's next interval based on the learner's success at the prior retrieval.

**key_claim**: Spaced Repetition produces the largest empirically documented effect size of any single study-time-allocation policy, because it converts the [[fluency-illusion]] of massed practice into a measurable retrieval failure that triggers genuine [[schema-construction]]; the result is that Spaced Repetition consistently outperforms the same total study time delivered in a massed schedule by factors of 1.5–3× on long-delay retention measures.

**warning**: Spaced Repetition is regularly conflated with *passive review on a schedule* — re-reading flashcards at intervals rather than retrieving them — collapsing the construct into a pacing tactic and discarding the [[retrieval-practice]] mechanism that does the work; Spaced Repetition without retrieval is merely distributed re-reading, and distributed re-reading produces only a fraction of the retention benefit that distributed retrieval delivers.

## Formative Assessment

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [assessment for learning, AfL]
- broader: [assessment-for-autonomy]
- narrower: [self-assessment, retrieval-practice]
- related: [feedback-design-for-autonomy-and-mastery, scaffolding, calibration, metacognitive-monitoring, judgment-of-learning, mastery-climate, backward-design]
- prerequisites: [calibration, metacognitive-monitoring]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Formative Assessment is the use of assessment evidence — in-task, between-task, or end-of-unit — to *adjust ongoing teaching and learning* rather than to produce a terminal grade, operationalised through low-stakes retrieval, diagnostic questioning, peer review, and structured self-assessment whose purpose is to surface misconceptions and recalibrate effort while the learning is still in progress. Formative Assessment is the core mechanism by which [[scaffolding]] is calibrated and by which [[metacognitive-monitoring]] is externally informed.

**key_claim**: Formative Assessment is the single most evidence-supported instructional intervention in K-16 education (Black & Wiliam 1998 and successors), with effect sizes substantially exceeding those of curriculum reforms or technology adoptions, because Formative Assessment couples the [[retrieval-practice]] mechanism (consolidation) with the [[calibration]] mechanism (correcting [[fluency-illusion]]) within a single instructional move.

**warning**: Formative Assessment is regularly counterfeited by *frequent summative testing* — quizzes whose results feed grades but not feedback or instructional adjustment — which inverts the construct: when assessment evidence does not change what is taught next, the activity ceases to be Formative Assessment regardless of its frequency, and the high effect sizes attached to Formative Assessment do not transfer to such counterfeit implementations.

## Social Cognitive Theory

- domain: social-cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [motivational-psychology, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [Bandura social cognitive theory, SCT]
- broader: [psychology]
- narrower: [self-efficacy-theory, self-efficacy, perceived-self-efficacy, triadic-reciprocal-determinism, vicarious-experience]
- related: [self-efficacy, outcome-expectation, enactive-mastery-experience, vicarious-experience, verbal-persuasion, physiological-and-affective-states, the-active-organism-hypothesis]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Social Cognitive Theory is Albert Bandura's integrated account of human agency in which behaviour, cognition (including [[perceived-self-efficacy]] and [[outcome-expectation]]), and environmental influences interact in [[triadic-reciprocal-determinism]] — none of the three reducible to the others — and through which people exercise *forethought*, *self-reactiveness*, and *self-reflectiveness*. Social Cognitive Theory is the parent framework from which [[self-efficacy-theory]] is derived and which supplies the four canonical sources of efficacy information: [[enactive-mastery-experience]], [[vicarious-experience]], [[verbal-persuasion]], and [[physiological-and-affective-states]].

**key_claim**: Social Cognitive Theory established that human behaviour cannot be modelled as either pure operant conditioning (behaviourism) or pure cognitive computation (early information-processing) but only as the *bidirectional interaction* of cognition, behaviour, and environment — a position that anticipated and grounds the modern understanding of [[the-active-organism-hypothesis]] and the centrality of [[self-regulated-learning]].

**warning**: Social Cognitive Theory is often reduced to "the self-efficacy theory" (one of its sub-theories) or to "social learning theory" (its 1977 precursor), losing the distinctive [[triadic-reciprocal-determinism]] commitment that distinguishes mature Social Cognitive Theory from both; the truncated readings license interventions that target a single corner of the triad (e.g. modelling without efficacy assessment) and consequently underperform Social Cognitive Theory's predictions.

## Locus of Control

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [personality-psychology]
- aliases: [Rotter locus of control, perceived locus of control]
- broader: [attribution-theory]
- narrower: [the-locus-of-causality-dimension]
- related: [causal-attribution, locus-of-causality, perceived-locus-of-causality, self-efficacy, learned-helplessness, attribution-theory, controllability-dimension, stability-dimension]
- prerequisites: [causal-attribution]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Locus of Control is Julian Rotter's construct, predating Weiner's full [[attribution-theory]], that classifies people's generalised expectancy about *who or what* controls the outcomes of their actions along an internal-external continuum: *internal* Locus of Control attributes outcomes primarily to one's own effort and ability; *external* Locus of Control attributes them primarily to luck, fate, or powerful others. Locus of Control is conceptually the precursor to the [[locus-of-causality]] dimension Weiner integrated into a multi-dimensional [[attribution-theory]].

**key_claim**: Locus of Control predicts a coherent cluster of self-regulatory behaviours: internal Locus of Control reliably correlates with proactive coping, [[self-directed-learning]], adaptive [[causal-attribution]] of failure, and resilience against [[learned-helplessness]], whereas external Locus of Control correlates with passive coping and outcome-attribution patterns that license withdrawal — but the predictive power is *moderated* by domain specificity and by environmental controllability.

**warning**: Locus of Control is frequently misread as a static personality trait deserving of single-score classification, but Rotter himself emphasised that Locus of Control is *generalised expectancy*, not invariant disposition — and that in domains where external control is in fact dominant (acute illness, oppressive institutional environments) an *external* Locus of Control may be the more accurate and adaptive reading; treating internal Locus of Control as universally correct risks blaming the situated for circumstances they did not generate.

## Goal Orientation

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [achievement goal orientation, motivational orientation]
- broader: [achievement-goal-theory]
- narrower: [mastery-approach-goal, performance-avoidance-goal, mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation]
- related: [achievement-goal, achievement-goal-theory, mastery-climate, mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework, performance-goal-vs-learning-goal, growth-mindset]
- prerequisites: [achievement-goal-theory, conception-of-ability]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Goal Orientation is, within [[achievement-goal-theory]], the relatively stable purpose a learner brings to an achievement situation — the *kind of competence* they are pursuing and the *kind of feedback* against which they evaluate themselves — operationalised as a position within [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]] whose two axes are competence-definition (mastery vs. normative performance) and competence-valence (approach vs. avoidance). Goal Orientation is the trait-like analogue of the situation-specific *achievement goal*.

**key_claim**: Goal Orientation predicts a coherent and replicable downstream cluster of cognitive and behavioural outcomes: mastery-approach Goal Orientation correlates with deep processing, [[help-seeking]], persistence under failure, and adaptive [[causal-attribution]], whereas performance-avoidance Goal Orientation correlates with [[self-handicapping]], surface processing, and withdrawal — making Goal Orientation one of the most reliable individual-differences predictors of learning quality available to educational psychology.

**warning**: Goal Orientation is regularly flattened to "mastery good, performance bad," which both misrepresents the empirical record (performance-approach Goal Orientation reliably predicts attainment in competitive domains) and obscures the more important [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]] insight that *approach vs avoidance* valence carries most of the variance in adaptive vs maladaptive outcomes — meaning the substantive Goal Orientation lever is shifting learners from avoidance to approach, not from performance to mastery.

## Long-Term Memory

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [memory-research, neuroscience]
- aliases: [LTM, secondary memory]
- broader: [cognitive-architecture]
- narrower: [long-term-working-memory, schema, retrieval-structure]
- related: [working-memory, schema-construction, schema-theory, schema-automation, retrieval-practice, the-power-law-of-practice, encoding-depth, levels-of-processing]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-architecture]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Long-Term Memory is the cognitive store of effectively unlimited capacity and duration that holds the schemas, episodes, semantic knowledge, and procedural skills which constitute a person's accumulated learning, and which is organised so as to allow retrieval of selected contents into the much smaller [[working-memory]] for active manipulation. In [[cognitive-load-theory]] and [[schema-theory]], Long-Term Memory is not merely a storehouse but the *cognitive architecture* whose contents — schemas — circumvent working-memory limits and thereby make expertise possible.

**key_claim**: Long-Term Memory is the *substrate of expertise*: the central finding of skill-acquisition research is that experts do not have larger working memories than novices but possess Long-Term Memory contents (schemas, [[chunking]]-derived units, [[retrieval-structure]]s) that allow [[long-term-working-memory]] to bypass the [[working-memory]] bottleneck — a result that grounds the design imperative to invest instructional effort in Long-Term Memory construction rather than in working-memory training.

**warning**: Long-Term Memory is regularly described as a passive repository of "what we know," obscuring the *constructive* nature of retrieval that [[schema-theory]] and the testing-effect literature establish: every act of retrieval from Long-Term Memory partially reconstructs and modifies the retrieved content, which is why repeated [[retrieval-practice]] produces both consolidation and distortion, and why Long-Term Memory cannot be treated as an immutable record analogous to a hard disk.

## Cognitive Bias

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [decision-research, behavioural-economics]
- aliases: [systematic bias, judgment bias]
- broader: [heuristics-and-biases, dual-process-theory]
- narrower: [fundamental-attribution-error, fluency-illusion, attribute-substitution, illusion-of-explanatory-depth, expert-blind-spot]
- related: [heuristics-and-biases, type-1-processing, cognitive-miserliness, mindware, default-interventionist-architecture, bounded-rationality]
- prerequisites: [heuristics-and-biases, dual-process-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Bias is a systematic deviation of judgment, perception, or decision from a normative standard (probability theory, formal logic, base-rate respect, or instructed accuracy) that arises from the operation of [[type-1-processing]] heuristics under conditions where their ecological validity is low. Cognitive Bias is not random error but reproducible directional distortion: the same input reliably produces the same skewed output across populations and across repeated exposures, even after the bias is named and explained.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Bias is *resistant to mere awareness*: the empirical record from [[heuristics-and-biases]] research is that knowing about a Cognitive Bias does not reliably reduce its operation in oneself — debiasing requires explicit [[mindware]], procedural [[cognitive-forcing-functions]], or environmental redesign, not motivational exhortation, which is why so-called "bias training" programmes that teach bias names without procedural countermeasures rarely improve decisions.

**warning**: Cognitive Bias is regularly invoked to dismiss any judgment one disagrees with — *"that's just the X bias"* — converting a precise psychological construct into a rhetorical cudgel; the substantive Cognitive Bias claim is narrower than this usage suggests, requires evidence that [[type-1-processing]] in the relevant low-validity environment is the mechanism, and does not license using Cognitive Bias as a synonym for *error* or *wrong opinion*.

## Bounded Rationality

- domain: decision-research
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, behavioural-economics]
- aliases: [Simon bounded rationality]
- broader: [decision-research]
- narrower: [satisficing, cognitive-miserliness, attribute-substitution]
- related: [heuristics-and-biases, cognitive-bias, dual-process-theory, working-memory, type-1-processing, mindware, the-active-organism-hypothesis]
- prerequisites: [decision-research]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Bounded Rationality is Herbert Simon's account, originating in the 1950s, that human decision-making is constrained by *cognitive limits* (working-memory capacity, attentional bottlenecks, computational cost) and *informational limits* (incomplete knowledge of options, outcomes, probabilities), and that consequently real decision agents do not optimise but *satisfice* — searching until an acceptable option is found rather than until the optimal one is identified. Bounded Rationality is the conceptual ancestor of [[heuristics-and-biases]] and of all subsequent ecological-rationality and dual-process accounts.

**key_claim**: Bounded Rationality reframes the descriptive failure of normative rational-choice theory not as a deficiency of human reasoners but as a *correct adaptation to environmental and cognitive constraints*: heuristics that look biased against an idealised optimiser frequently outperform optimal procedures under realistic time, information, and computational budgets — a position elaborated by Gigerenzer's ecological-rationality programme as a defence and extension of Bounded Rationality rather than a refutation of [[heuristics-and-biases]].

**warning**: Bounded Rationality is often confused with *irrationality* or with the [[heuristics-and-biases]] critique of intuition, but Simon's original construct is neutral about the *quality* of the bounded decisions: Bounded Rationality says only that real reasoners satisfice within constraints, not that they err systematically — and the question of whether a given satisficing procedure is *adaptive* depends on the [[high-validity-environment]] criterion that the bare Bounded Rationality framework leaves open.

## Learned Helplessness

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [clinical-psychology, attribution-research]
- aliases: [Seligman learned helplessness, helplessness pattern]
- broader: [attribution-theory, motivational-psychology]
- narrower: []
- related: [attributional-vocabulary, explanatory-style-attributional-style, attribution-retraining, causal-attribution, controllability-dimension, the-stability-expectancy-principle, growth-mindset, self-efficacy]
- prerequisites: [attribution-theory, causal-attribution]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Learned Helplessness is the motivational, cognitive, and affective syndrome — first identified by Seligman and Maier (1967) in animal research and reformulated for humans by Abramson, Seligman, and Teasdale (1978) — in which prior exposure to *uncontrollable* aversive outcomes generalises into a stable expectancy that future outcomes are also uncontrollable, suppressing initiation of action even when control is now available. The reformulated Learned Helplessness account locates the syndrome's persistence in an internal-stable-global [[explanatory-style-attributional-style]] for failure.

**key_claim**: Learned Helplessness is the canonical demonstration that *the cognition of uncontrollability* — the [[causal-attribution]] of negative outcomes to stable, uncontrollable causes — produces the behavioural and affective deficits of helplessness *independently* of the actual contingency between behaviour and outcome, which is why [[attribution-retraining]] interventions that shift the explanatory style for failure can reverse Learned Helplessness even when the original aversive history remains.

**warning**: Learned Helplessness is widely misread as a *trait* of helpless individuals, when the central empirical claim of the reformulated theory is that Learned Helplessness is the joint product of an environmental history of uncontrollability *and* a particular [[explanatory-style-attributional-style]]; treating Learned Helplessness as a victim-blaming personality label inverts the construct, which originated in a critique of environments that systematically train uncontrollability into their inhabitants.

## Zone of Proximal Development

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [ZPD, zone of proximal development]
- broader: [scaffolding]
- narrower: []
- related: [scaffolding, scaffolded-fading, scaffolding-fading-progression, cognitive-scaffolding, metacognitive-scaffolding, the-just-in-time-principle, autonomy-supportive-structure, cognitive-load-theory, expertise-reversal-effect]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Zone of Proximal Development is Lev Vygotsky's construct for the band of tasks that a learner *cannot yet perform independently* but *can perform with assistance* from a more capable partner — distinguishing what the learner has consolidated (below the zone) from what is currently beyond reach (above the zone). The Zone of Proximal Development is the conceptual locus at which [[scaffolding]] does its work: supports calibrated to the upper edge of the zone today become unnecessary as that edge advances tomorrow.

**key_claim**: Zone of Proximal Development reframes assessment from a measure of *current independent performance* (which captures only the lower bound of the zone) to a measure of *learnability under support*, which is the predictive quantity instructional design actually needs — a reframing that grounds the case for dynamic assessment, formative testing, and the [[scaffolding-fading-progression]] over static end-of-unit measurement.

**warning**: Zone of Proximal Development is regularly used as a generic synonym for "challenging but doable," collapsing Vygotsky's specific construct into a vague pedagogical heuristic; the substantive Zone of Proximal Development commitments — that the zone is *defined by interaction with a more capable other*, that it *moves* as competence develops, and that assistance must be *withdrawn* in step with development — are precisely what survive translation into modern [[scaffolded-fading]] practice and what are lost when Zone of Proximal Development is treated as a mood word for "appropriate difficulty."

## Self-Regulation

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [motivational-psychology, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [self-regulatory capacity, behavioural self-regulation]
- broader: [self-regulated-learning]
- narrower: [metacognitive-control, motivational-regulation, the-four-areas-of-regulation]
- related: [self-regulated-learning, metacognition, metacognitive-monitoring, metacognitive-control, monitoring-control-loop, forethought-phase, control-phase, reaction-and-reflection-phase, executive-function]
- prerequisites: [metacognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Self-Regulation is the broad capacity to monitor and modulate one's own cognition, motivation, affect, and behaviour in pursuit of goals — operationalised across psychological sub-fields as the cyclical coupling of forethought (goal setting, planning), performance (monitoring, control), and self-reflection (evaluation, attribution). Self-Regulation is the parent construct from which [[self-regulated-learning]] is derived as the domain-specific application to academic and learning contexts, and from which [[metacognitive-control]] is derived as the cognition-specific layer.

**key_claim**: Self-Regulation is the most reliable single non-cognitive predictor of long-term life outcomes — academic attainment, occupational success, health behaviours, relationship stability — across decades of longitudinal research; and the empirical record is consistent that Self-Regulation is *trainable* through structured intervention ([[implementation-intentions]], [[mental-contrasting]], [[mcii]], cognitive-behavioural protocols) rather than being a fixed trait.

**warning**: Self-Regulation is regularly equated with *willpower* or *self-control* as a single depletable resource, but the modern empirical record on ego-depletion is mixed and the construct of Self-Regulation as conceptualised in [[self-regulated-learning]] research is a *cyclical control system*, not a fuel tank; treating Self-Regulation as willpower licenses moralised explanations of regulatory failure that ignore the cyclical-feedback architecture and the [[motivational-ecology]] that actually drive regulatory outcomes.

## Self-Explanation

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [self-explanation effect]
- broader: [elaborative-encoding]
- narrower: []
- related: [elaborative-interrogation, worked-examples, the-worked-example-effect, encoding-depth, levels-of-processing, schema-construction, germane-cognitive-load, comprehension-monitoring]
- prerequisites: [working-memory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Self-Explanation is the learning strategy in which a learner generates *their own explanations* of a presented worked example, text passage, or principle — articulating why a step was taken, how a new idea connects to prior knowledge, or what the underlying mechanism is — rather than passively reading the material. Self-Explanation was identified by Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann, and Glaser (1989) as the behaviour that distinguished successful from unsuccessful learners studying physics worked examples, and has since been replicated as one of the most reliably useful study strategies.

**key_claim**: Self-Explanation produces durable learning gains because it externalises the [[germane-cognitive-load]] that [[schema-construction]] requires: by forcing the learner to articulate the structural relations binding example elements, Self-Explanation surfaces gaps in the developing schema, triggers [[comprehension-monitoring]], and prevents the [[fluency-illusion]] of having understood a worked example merely because its surface was followed.

**warning**: Self-Explanation is often conflated with *re-stating* what the text already said — a form of verbal rehearsal that produces no [[encoding-depth]] gain — collapsing the construct into yet another fluency-illusion-prone study tactic; the substantive Self-Explanation commitment is to the *generation* of inferences, justifications, and mechanism descriptions that the source did not supply, and any re-statement of presented content is no more Self-Explanation than reading aloud is comprehension.

## Habit Formation

- domain: behavioural-psychology
- secondary_domains: [neuroscience, motivational-psychology]
- aliases: [habit acquisition, automaticity acquisition]
- broader: [strategic-automaticity]
- narrower: []
- related: [implementation-intention, implementation-intentions, the-habit-formation-imperative, automaticity, schema-automation, action-phase-model, goal-intention, intention-behavior-gap, mcii]
- prerequisites: [strategic-automaticity]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Habit Formation is the behavioural-cognitive process by which a deliberate action sequence — initiated under [[goal-intention]] and [[type-2-processing]] — becomes triggered automatically by stable contextual cues through repeated execution, transferring control of the action from conscious deliberation to cue-driven [[type-1-processing]]. Habit Formation is operationalised in the modern empirical literature (Lally et al., 2010) as a saturating-curve trajectory whose median time-to-asymptote is on the order of 66 days for moderate-complexity behaviours.

**key_claim**: Habit Formation is the principal mechanism by which [[implementation-intentions]] and [[mcii]] convert one-shot intentions into sustained behavioural change: by binding the desired action to a specific environmental cue, the [[implementation-intention]] structure delegates execution to the cue-driven Habit Formation pathway, bypassing the [[intention-behavior-gap]] that pure goal-intention work cannot close.

**warning**: Habit Formation is regularly counterfeited by *willpower-based repetition* in unstable contexts — performing the behaviour daily but in different settings, at different times, after different cues — which prevents the cue-binding that defines Habit Formation and produces only episodic compliance; without a stable cue, what looks like Habit Formation is sustained [[goal-intention]] effort whose persistence depends on motivational resources rather than on the cue-driven [[automaticity]] that Habit Formation actually delivers.

## Goal Setting

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [organizational-psychology, educational-psychology]

- broader: [goal-setting-theory]
- narrower: [goal-specificity, goal-difficulty, goal-commitment, implementation-intention]

- prerequisites: [goal-setting-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Goal Setting is the deliberate practice of formulating and committing to specific, difficult, and proximally testable performance targets that direct attention, mobilise effort, sustain persistence, and trigger task-relevant strategy search — operationalised in [[goal-setting-theory]] as the joint application of [[goal-specificity]] and [[goal-difficulty]] under sufficient [[goal-commitment]]. Goal Setting is the input layer of [[goal-setting-theory]]'s [[high-performance-cycle]].

**key_claim**: Goal Setting reliably outperforms "do your best" instructions across hundreds of laboratory and field studies because Goal Setting converts the abstract motivational intention into a concrete behavioural target whose attainment can be monitored — a result that gives [[goal-setting-theory]] the rare distinction of being one of the most replicated and effect-size-stable findings in industrial-organisational psychology.

## Performance-Approach Goal

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, achievement-motivation]
- aliases: [normative approach goal]
- broader: [achievement-goal-theory, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]
- narrower: []
- related: [mastery-approach-goal, performance-avoidance-goal, achievement-goal, achievement-goal-theory, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework, mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation, conception-of-ability]
- prerequisites: [achievement-goal-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Performance-Approach Goal is, within [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]], the achievement orientation in which competence is *defined normatively* — relative to others — and *valenced positively* (approaching the demonstration of superior competence rather than avoiding the demonstration of inferior competence). A learner under a Performance-Approach Goal pursues comparative success: outperforming peers, ranking high, demonstrating ability against external standards.

**key_claim**: Performance-Approach Goal is the most empirically nuanced cell of [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]]: in competitive, evaluation-rich, and high-stakes-attainment contexts, Performance-Approach Goal reliably predicts *attainment* gains comparable to or exceeding mastery-approach goals — a finding that complicates the simplified "mastery good, performance bad" reading of [[achievement-goal-theory]] and forces a contextual rather than universal evaluation of the Performance-Approach Goal cell.

**warning**: Performance-Approach Goal is often endorsed without its boundary conditions, but the empirical record is that Performance-Approach Goal degrades into [[performance-avoidance-goal]] under any threat to ability self-image — a single salient failure, a public ranking demotion, or a [[mastery-climate]] absent — which converts Performance-Approach Goal's attainment benefits into [[self-handicapping]] losses; treating Performance-Approach Goal as a stable adaptive orientation ignores its known instability under aversive feedback.

## Mastery-Avoidance Goal

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, achievement-motivation]
- aliases: [task-avoidance goal]
- broader: [achievement-goal-theory, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]
- narrower: []
- related: [mastery-approach-goal, performance-avoidance-goal, performance-approach-goal, achievement-goal, the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework, conception-of-ability]
- prerequisites: [achievement-goal-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Mastery-Avoidance Goal is, within [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]], the achievement orientation in which competence is *defined absolutely* — by reference to the task itself or to one's own prior performance — and *valenced negatively* (avoiding falling short of full task mastery, losing previously acquired skill, or making errors). A learner under a Mastery-Avoidance Goal is preoccupied with not regressing or not failing to master, rather than with positively pursuing mastery as such.

**key_claim**: Mastery-Avoidance Goal is the most theoretically distinctive contribution of Elliot's [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]] over the original mastery-vs-performance dichotomy, predicting a maladaptive cluster — anxiety, perfectionism, defensive disengagement — that resembles [[performance-avoidance-goal]] outcomes despite being driven by *task* rather than *normative* concerns; this distinguishes Mastery-Avoidance Goal as the orientation most relevant to expert decline, late-career stagnation, and skill-retention anxiety.

**warning**: Mastery-Avoidance Goal is often misread as a mild, benign form of caution, but the empirical record is that Mastery-Avoidance Goal carries the avoidance-cluster's full motivational cost — surface processing, reduced help-seeking, [[self-handicapping]] — without the attainment compensation that [[performance-approach-goal]] sometimes delivers; designing for Mastery-Avoidance Goal as an acceptable orientation tends to entrench the very stagnation it claims to prevent.

## Need Satisfaction

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, organizational-psychology]
- aliases: [psychological need satisfaction, BPN satisfaction]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- narrower: []
- related: [basic-psychological-needs, basic-psychological-need, the-three-basic-psychological-needs, need-frustration, autonomy-sdt, competence-sdt, relatedness-sdt, autonomous-motivation, internalization]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory, basic-psychological-needs]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Need Satisfaction is, within [[self-determination-theory]], the experiential state in which the [[the-three-basic-psychological-needs]] — [[autonomy-sdt]], [[competence-sdt]], and [[relatedness-sdt]] — are concurrently met by the prevailing motivational environment, producing the affective and cognitive substrate from which [[autonomous-motivation]] and successful [[internalization]] develop. Need Satisfaction is conceptually and empirically distinct from [[need-frustration]], which is an active pathogenic state rather than the mere absence of Need Satisfaction.

**key_claim**: Need Satisfaction is the *proximal mediator* through which [[self-determination-theory]] converts environmental supports — autonomy-supportive teaching, optimally challenging tasks, warm relational climates — into downstream motivational, cognitive, and well-being outcomes, which is why interventions whose only causal pathway is increased contingent reward reliably fail to deliver Need Satisfaction even when they raise short-term performance.

**warning**: Need Satisfaction is regularly conflated with *desire satisfaction* (getting what one wants) or *outcome satisfaction* (achieving one's goals), but [[self-determination-theory]] is explicit that Need Satisfaction refers specifically to the three universal psychological needs, *not* to derived preferences; satisfying derived desires (status, wealth, image) without Need Satisfaction reliably fails to produce well-being and may actively erode it through the [[need-frustration]] mechanism that [[intrinsic-vs-extrinsic-goal-contents]] research documents.

## Cognitive Evaluation Theory

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [CET]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- narrower: []
- related: [intrinsic-motivation, extrinsic-motivation, autonomy-sdt, competence-sdt, the-replication-challenge-to-the-undermining-effect, perceived-locus-of-causality, organismic-integration-theory, self-determination-theory]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory, intrinsic-motivation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Evaluation Theory is the first of the six sub-theories of [[self-determination-theory]] (Deci & Ryan), addressing how external events — rewards, deadlines, surveillance, evaluation, choice, positive feedback — affect [[intrinsic-motivation]] by shifting the [[perceived-locus-of-causality]] of the activity from internal to external and by altering perceived [[competence-sdt]]. Cognitive Evaluation Theory generated the original "undermining effect" research and the operational distinction between *informational* and *controlling* events.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Evaluation Theory established that the *functional significance* of an external event to the recipient — whether the event is experienced as informational or as controlling — determines its motivational consequence, not the event's surface form: the same reward is undermining when delivered as a control device and supportive when delivered as informational feedback, which is why Cognitive Evaluation Theory's recommendations are about delivery context rather than reward avoidance.

**warning**: Cognitive Evaluation Theory is routinely caricatured as the claim that "rewards always undermine intrinsic motivation," but the mature Cognitive Evaluation Theory position — refined through [[the-replication-challenge-to-the-undermining-effect]] and the meta-analyses of Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999, 2017) — is far narrower: tangible *expected contingent* rewards undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks that are *already intrinsically interesting*, while informational verbal rewards and unexpected tangible rewards do not.

## Basic Psychological Needs Theory

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, well-being-research]
- aliases: [BPNT, needs theory]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- narrower: [autonomy-sdt, competence-sdt, relatedness-sdt, need-satisfaction, need-frustration]
- related: [the-three-basic-psychological-needs, basic-psychological-needs, basic-psychological-need, autonomous-motivation, internalization-continuum, organismic-integration-theory, the-active-organism-hypothesis]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Basic Psychological Needs Theory is the third of the six sub-theories of [[self-determination-theory]], asserting that human well-being depends on the satisfaction of three innate, universal, and non-substitutable psychological needs — [[autonomy-sdt]], [[competence-sdt]], and [[relatedness-sdt]] — across the lifespan and across cultures. Basic Psychological Needs Theory is the conceptual core that distinguishes [[self-determination-theory]] from desire-fulfilment and preference-satisfaction accounts of motivation and well-being.

**key_claim**: Basic Psychological Needs Theory's central empirical claim is the *universality and additivity* of the three needs: across cultures, ages, life domains, and economic conditions, satisfaction of all three needs predicts well-being and adaptive functioning, frustration of any predicts ill-being, and the effects are *additive* rather than substitutable — high relatedness does not compensate for low autonomy, and material reward does not substitute for [[need-satisfaction]].

**warning**: Basic Psychological Needs Theory is widely confused with *Maslow's hierarchy of needs*, but the constructs differ fundamentally: Basic Psychological Needs Theory's three needs are *non-hierarchical, concurrent, and psychological* (not biological), and the empirical evidence rejects any universal ordering or prepotency among them; treating Basic Psychological Needs Theory as a Maslow successor obscures both the distinct need set and the [[self-determination-theory]] commitment to non-substitutability.

## Causal Attribution

- domain: social-cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [motivational-psychology, attribution-research]
- aliases: [attribution]
- broader: [attribution-theory]
- narrower: [attribution, attributional-vocabulary, explanatory-style-attributional-style, the-locus-of-causality-dimension, the-stability-dimension, the-controllability-dimension]
- related: [attribution-theory, locus-of-causality, controllability-dimension, stability-dimension, attribution-dependent-emotion, attribution-retraining, the-attributional-bridge, the-functional-priority-of-unexpected-and-negative-outcomes]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Causal Attribution is the cognitive process by which a person constructs an explanation for an event — particularly an outcome of their own or another's action — by assigning the outcome to one or more causes whose locations along the [[locus-of-causality]], [[stability-dimension]], and [[controllability-dimension]] axes determine the affective, expectancy, and motivational consequences of the explanation. Causal Attribution is the cognitive primitive on which [[attribution-theory]] is built and from which [[explanatory-style-attributional-style]] is derived as a stable disposition.

**key_claim**: Causal Attribution is *cognitively constructed, not perceptually given*: the same outcome admits multiple defensible attributions, and the attribution selected — rather than the outcome itself — drives the downstream affective and motivational cascade, which is why [[attribution-retraining]] interventions can reverse [[learned-helplessness]] without changing the original aversive history simply by changing the Causal Attribution of failure from internal-stable-uncontrollable to internal-unstable-controllable.

**warning**: Causal Attribution is often described as if a single "correct" attribution exists for each outcome, ignoring the under-determination of cause by event that the [[attribution-theory]] tradition centred; treating Causal Attribution as a fact-finding rather than a *construction* licenses attributional moralism — blaming victims for the attributions they made — and obscures the [[functional-priority-of-unexpected-and-negative-outcomes]] that explains why some outcomes trigger Causal Attribution effort while others are simply absorbed.

## Active Learning

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [active learning approach, active engagement learning]
- broader: [educational-psychology]
- narrower: [retrieval-practice, productive-failure, self-explanation, elaborative-interrogation]
- related: [retrieval-practice, retrieval-practice-as-the-most-potent-single-strategy, productive-failure, schema-construction, germane-cognitive-load, fluency-illusion, desirable-difficulties, the-coordination-thesis-for-schema-construction]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-load-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Active Learning is the pedagogical category that contrasts with passive reception (lecture, assigned reading) by requiring the learner to *generate* something — answers, explanations, predictions, problem solutions, retrieval attempts — that externalises the cognitive work of [[schema-construction]] and converts the latent [[germane-cognitive-load]] of comprehension into observable learner behaviour. Active Learning encompasses [[retrieval-practice]], [[self-explanation]], [[elaborative-interrogation]], [[productive-failure]], and instructed problem-solving, among others.

**key_claim**: Active Learning produces durable learning gains because it eliminates the [[fluency-illusion]] that passive review cultivates: the act of generating a response surfaces gaps in the developing schema in a way that the act of reading does not, and the resulting [[desirable-difficulties]] trigger the consolidation that [[retrieval-practice]] research has documented as the single most reliable study-strategy effect.

**warning**: Active Learning is regularly counterfeited by *active behaviours that do not produce active cognition* — colourful group activities, kinesthetic demonstrations, peer chat — collapsing Active Learning into a category of *visible motion* rather than *generative cognition*; the substantive Active Learning commitment is to learner generation of cognitively load-bearing output, and any classroom activity that engages the body without engaging [[germane-cognitive-load]] is Active Learning in surface only.

## Transfer of Learning

- domain: cognitive-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [transfer, learning transfer]
- broader: [educational-psychology, cognitive-psychology]
- narrower: [the-integration-transfer-advantage]
- related: [schema-construction, schema, four-component-instructional-design-4c-id, complex-learning, whole-task-approach, the-integration-transfer-advantage, expertise-reversal-effect, retrieval-practice, productive-failure]
- prerequisites: [schema-theory, schema-construction]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Transfer of Learning is the application of knowledge, skill, or strategy acquired in one context to performance in a different context — distinguished into *near transfer* (closely matching contexts) and *far transfer* (structurally similar but surface-different contexts). Transfer of Learning is the criterion by which instructional designs are ultimately judged: a design that produces high in-course performance but no Transfer of Learning has produced [[pseudoexpertise]], not durable learning.

**key_claim**: Transfer of Learning depends on the *abstraction level* at which the original learning was encoded: surface-bound learning transfers narrowly or not at all, while learning encoded around [[schema]] structures and explicit principles transfers far more reliably — which is why [[four-component-instructional-design-4c-id]] and the [[whole-task-approach]] privilege variability of practice and integration of partial skills as the principal Transfer of Learning levers.

**warning**: Transfer of Learning, especially *far* transfer, has been historically over-promised by educational interventions (brain-training games, generic-thinking-skills curricula) whose evidence base is consistently weak; the substantive Transfer of Learning literature is humbler — far transfer is rare without explicit instruction in *the abstraction itself* and without varied practice across the target transfer surface — and treating Transfer of Learning as an automatic by-product of "good teaching" reliably overestimates what any single instructional unit will deliver.

**definition**: Externalized Metacognition is the practice of offloading metacognitive functions — monitoring, planning, evaluation, knowledge-of-cognition — into external artifacts (notes, dashboards, review queues, planning systems) so that the cognitive work of regulating one's own learning is partially performed by the structure of the artifact rather than entirely by online cognition. **definition**: Cognitive Strategy Use is the deployment, by a learner, of specific cognitive procedures — rehearsal, elaboration, organisation, retrieval, monitoring, repair — selected to advance a particular learning goal under a particular task condition, and adjusted in response to monitoring feedback. Cognitive Strategy Use is one of [[the-four-areas-of-regulation]] in [[pintrich-s-framework-of-self-regulated-learning]] and is the layer at which [[metacognitive-control]] does its visible work.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Strategy Use is *the* mediating variable between metacognitive knowledge and learning outcome: a learner who possesses accurate [[metacognitive-knowledge]] but does not deploy the corresponding strategy gains nothing, whereas a learner who deploys the strategy without explicit knowledge often gains the benefit anyway — which is why Cognitive Strategy Use, not metacognitive knowledge per se, is the most reliable cross-sectional predictor of learning outcomes in the [[self-regulated-learning]] literature.

**warning**: Cognitive Strategy Use is regularly conflated with *strategy instruction* (teaching learners to name strategies they could use), but the substantive Cognitive Strategy Use literature consistently finds that *naming-without-deploying* is among the least productive interventions in the field; effective Cognitive Strategy Use development requires conditional knowledge (when to deploy which strategy), self-regulatory triggering, and deliberate practice in deployment — none of which is delivered by strategy-list curricula.

## Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning

- domain: educational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [self-regulated-learning, motivational-psychology]
- aliases: [Zimmerman cyclical model, three-phase cyclical model]
- broader: [self-regulated-learning]
- narrower: [forethought-phase, control-phase, reaction-and-reflection-phase]
- related: [self-regulated-learning, pintrich-s-framework-of-self-regulated-learning, monitoring-control-loop, the-cyclical-feedback-architecture-as-learning-engine, forethought-as-regulatory-front-loading, reaction-and-reflection-as-cyclic-coupling, monitoring-phase, metacognitive-monitoring, metacognitive-control]
- prerequisites: [self-regulated-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning is Barry Zimmerman's three-phase account of [[self-regulated-learning]] in which forethought ([[forethought-phase]]: goal-setting, strategic planning, self-efficacy assessment), performance ([[control-phase]]: self-control and self-observation during action), and self-reflection ([[reaction-and-reflection-phase]]: self-judgment and self-reaction) are coupled into a *closed loop* whose output feeds back into the next forethought episode. The Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning is the canonical operationalisation of [[the-cyclical-feedback-architecture-as-learning-engine]].

**key_claim**: The Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning's empirical contribution is the demonstration that *failure to close the loop* — strong forethought without monitoring, strong monitoring without reflective integration, strong reflection without revised forethought — is the modal pattern of self-regulatory failure, and that effective intervention targets the broken edge of the cycle rather than any single phase in isolation.

**warning**: The Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning is regularly flattened to a *checklist* of three phases ("plan, do, review") whose mere completion is treated as evidence of self-regulation, but the substantive Cyclical Model of Self-Regulated Learning commitment is to the *causal coupling* between phases — the next forethought episode must be *informed by* the prior reflection — and ritualised three-phase compliance without coupling is decorative self-regulation that delivers none of the model's predicted benefits.

## Controlling Teaching Styles

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [controlling style, controlling teaching, controlling parenting]
- broader: [self-determination-theory]
- narrower: [conditional-regard, external-regulation, introjected-regulation]
- related: [autonomy-support, autonomy-supportive-structure, autonomy-structure-dialectic, conditional-regard, controlled-motivation, need-frustration, internalization-continuum, organismic-integration-theory]
- prerequisites: [self-determination-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Controlling Teaching Styles are, within [[self-determination-theory]], the interpersonal patterns — pressuring language ("you must," "you should"), contingent reward, surveillance, evaluative comparison, withdrawal of warmth contingent on performance — that thwart the [[autonomy-sdt]] need by establishing an external [[perceived-locus-of-causality]] for the student's learning behaviours. Controlling Teaching Styles are the structural opposite of [[autonomy-support]], not its absence, and operate through a distinct causal pathway with its own [[need-frustration]] consequences.

**key_claim**: Controlling Teaching Styles reliably degrade [[autonomous-motivation]], engagement quality, conceptual learning, and well-being, with effects mediated by [[need-frustration]] rather than by mere absence of [[need-satisfaction]] — a finding that establishes Controlling Teaching Styles as an *active* pathogenic factor rather than a neutral baseline, and that grounds the [[self-determination-theory]] claim that controlling and autonomy-supportive practices have distinguishable, non-redundant motivational consequences.

**warning**: Controlling Teaching Styles are routinely defended as *necessary structure* or *high expectations*, but the [[autonomy-structure-dialectic]] is explicit that structure and control are orthogonal: a teacher can sustain demanding standards, clear expectations, and consequential evaluation without Controlling Teaching Styles, and conflating control with structure both licenses pressuring practice and discredits the genuine structure that [[self-determination-theory]] endorses.

## Causal Attribution in Motivation

- domain: motivational-psychology
- secondary_domains: [attribution-research, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [motivational attribution]
- broader: [attribution-theory]
- narrower: []
- related: [causal-attribution, attribution-theory, attribution-dependent-emotion, the-attributional-bridge, attributional-vocabulary, explanatory-style-attributional-style, attribution-retraining, the-functional-priority-of-unexpected-and-negative-outcomes, learned-helplessness, growth-mindset]
- prerequisites: [attribution-theory, causal-attribution]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Causal Attribution in Motivation is the application of [[attribution-theory]] to the prediction of motivated behaviour — specifically, the chain by which the explanation a person constructs for a prior outcome ([[causal-attribution]] along the locus, stability, and controllability dimensions) generates an [[attribution-dependent-emotion]] (pride, shame, guilt, anger, hopelessness), an updated expectancy for similar future outcomes, and a corresponding behavioural choice (re-engagement, withdrawal, strategy revision). Causal Attribution in Motivation is the operational form of [[the-attributional-bridge]].

**key_claim**: Causal Attribution in Motivation establishes that the motivational signal carried by a success or failure is *encoded by the attribution*, not by the outcome itself: identical outcomes attributed to internal-stable-uncontrollable causes (lack of ability) produce withdrawal, while attributed to internal-unstable-controllable causes (insufficient strategic effort) produce re-engagement — making Causal Attribution in Motivation the principal lever on which [[attribution-retraining]] interventions operate.

**warning**: Causal Attribution in Motivation is regularly diluted into the slogan "attribute failure to effort" without the [[the-stability-expectancy-principle]] qualification that effort attributions are protective only when effort was in fact deployed; attributing strategy-deficient failure to insufficient effort exemplifies [[the-fundamental-attribution-error]] turned inward, sustains repetition of the same failed approach, and is precisely the maladaptive pattern Causal Attribution in Motivation research warns against.

## Debugging

- domain: software-engineering
- secondary_domains: [developer-tooling, computer-science]
- aliases: [debug, troubleshooting code]
- broader: [software-engineering]
- narrower: [breakpoint, traceback, exception]
- related: [breakpoint, traceback, exception, integrated-development-environment, github-copilot, language-server-protocol, the-replication-challenge-to-the-undermining-effect]
- prerequisites: [integrated-development-environment]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Debugging is the systematic activity of locating, diagnosing, and removing defects in software by formulating hypotheses about a discrepancy between expected and observed behaviour, then constructing controlled observations — instrumented logging, [[breakpoint]] inspection of runtime state, minimal-reproduction isolation, [[traceback]] interpretation — that confirm or falsify each hypothesis. Debugging is conceptually closer to scientific inquiry than to typing: the bottleneck is hypothesis quality, not edit speed.

**key_claim**: Debugging skill is the proximal differentiator between novice and expert programmers far more than syntactic fluency or library knowledge: novices treat Debugging as random trial-and-error code modification, while experts treat Debugging as disciplined hypothesis testing — instrument first, hypothesise explicitly, change one variable at a time, falsify before fixing — which is why effective Debugging instruction targets the inquiry process rather than tool features.

**warning**: Debugging is regularly conflated with *making the symptom go away*, but a change that suppresses the visible failure without identifying the underlying defect leaves the defect in place to re-emerge — often more obscurely — under slightly different conditions; the substantive Debugging discipline ends not when the test passes but when the *causal mechanism* of the original failure is understood, and Debugging that stops short of mechanism risks the cargo-cult code-modification that [[cargo-cult-coding]] research documents.

## Version Control

- domain: software-engineering
- secondary_domains: [developer-tooling, computer-science]
- aliases: [VCS, source control, version control system]
- broader: [software-engineering]
- narrower: []
- related: [debugging, dependency-management, github-copilot, integrated-development-environment, modular-design, virtual-environment]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Version Control is the systematic recording of changes to a set of files over time — most often source code — into a queryable history that allows any past state to be reconstructed, any change to be attributed and inspected, and parallel lines of work (branches) to be developed and later merged. Modern Version Control (Git, Mercurial, Fossil) implements a content-addressable distributed graph rather than the centralised linear logs of earlier systems.

**key_claim**: Version Control is the substrate that makes modern software collaboration possible at all: branching enables parallel development without coordination overhead, merging resolves divergent work without overwriting, and the history-as-graph structure converts what would otherwise be an unmanageable coordination problem into a tractable one — which is why Version Control adoption (specifically Git adoption) is the strongest single discriminator between the productivity of pre-2010 and post-2010 software engineering practice.

**warning**: Version Control is regularly reduced to the surface ritual of `commit` and `push`, ignoring the substantive disciplines that make it valuable — meaningful commit granularity (one logical change per commit), informative commit messages, branch hygiene, and conflict resolution by mechanism rather than by file overwriting; without these disciplines, Version Control's history degenerates into a useless record of "WIP" commits that destroys the very inspectability that justifies the tool.

## Dependency Management

- domain: software-engineering
- secondary_domains: [developer-tooling, computer-science]
- aliases: [package management, dependency resolution]
- broader: [software-engineering]
- narrower: [requirements-txt, virtual-environment, python-package]
- related: [requirements-txt, virtual-environment, python-package, integrated-development-environment, version-control, modular-design, github-copilot]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Dependency Management is the systematic specification, resolution, installation, and pinning of the external libraries and tools on which a project depends, performed through declarative manifests ([[requirements-txt]], `pyproject.toml`, `package.json`, `Cargo.toml`) and resolved by package managers (pip, uv, npm, cargo) into reproducible installation graphs. Dependency Management is what allows a project to be re-built deterministically across machines, time, and contributors.

**key_claim**: Dependency Management is one of the central reproducibility levers in software engineering: a project whose dependencies are specified by declarative manifest with pinned versions and an explicit resolver lock file (`requirements.txt` + `pip freeze`, `package-lock.json`, `Cargo.lock`) can be re-built years later by anyone with access to the package registry, while a project whose dependencies are implicit ("install whatever pip gives you") cannot be re-built reliably even by its original author six months on.

**warning**: Dependency Management is regularly weakened by the practice of installing packages without recording them in the manifest — relying on the developer's local environment to "just have" the dependency — which produces the *works on my machine* failure mode that destroys the very reproducibility Dependency Management exists to deliver; effective Dependency Management requires that the manifest be the *single source of truth* for what the project depends on.

## Modular Design

- domain: software-engineering
- secondary_domains: [computer-science, systems-design]
- aliases: [modularity, modular architecture]
- broader: [software-engineering]
- narrower: []
- related: [dependency-management, version-control, debugging, integrated-development-environment, github-copilot, the-isolation-principle-as-engineering-discipline, the-compartmentalization-thesis, complex-learning, the-architectural-imperative]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Modular Design is the organisation of a software system into discrete, well-bounded components — modules, packages, services — each of which exposes a *narrow interface* and conceals an *implementation* whose internals can be changed without disturbing other modules. Modular Design rests on the principles of *information hiding* (Parnas, 1972), *separation of concerns* (Dijkstra), and *low coupling, high cohesion*, and is the structural prerequisite for tractable maintenance, parallel development, and unit testing.

**key_claim**: Modular Design is the principal lever on long-term maintenance cost: a system whose modules have narrow interfaces and hidden implementations can absorb requirement changes locally, while a system whose modules leak implementation details across interfaces propagates each change through the codebase — which is why Modular Design discipline accounts for most of the cross-team variance in defect rates and feature-velocity once a codebase exceeds the size that a single developer can hold in mind.

**warning**: Modular Design is regularly counterfeited by *file-level partition* — splitting a monolithic file into many small files without redrawing the interfaces between them — which preserves the original coupling structure under a more decorated surface; the substantive Modular Design commitment is to interface narrowness and implementation hiding, and any decomposition that does not reduce the *information* that crosses module boundaries is Modular Design in surface only.

## Code Editor

- domain: developer-tooling

- broader: [developer-tooling]
- narrower: []
- related: [integrated-development-environment, integrated-development-environment-ide-vs-code-editor, language-server-protocol, github-copilot, debugging, the-extension-ecosystem-as-capability-assembly]
- prerequisites: []

**definition**: A Code Editor is a text-editing application optimised for source code through syntax highlighting, smart indentation, bracket matching, regex search, and (modernly) integration with [[language-server-protocol]] for completion and diagnostics — but lacking the integrated build, debug, profile, and project-management tooling that distinguishes an [[integrated-development-environment]]. **key_claim**: A Code Editor's defining characteristic is *minimal default scope with extensible capability*: the editor itself does little beyond editing, and its richness derives from the extension ecosystem the user assembles — a pattern that mirrors [[the-extension-ecosystem-as-capability-assembly]] thesis and that gives the user fine-grained control over which capabilities are present at the cost of requiring the user to assemble the working environment.

**warning**: A Code Editor is regularly described as "the same as an IDE but lighter," obscuring the substantive distinction that [[integrated-development-environment-ide-vs-code-editor]] documents: a Code Editor leaves build, debug, project graph, and tool integration to the user (or to extensions), while an IDE bundles them into a coherent default; treating the two as interchangeable misleads new developers about what the tool will and will not do without configuration.

## Environment Variables

- domain: developer-tooling
- secondary_domains: [operating-systems, software-engineering]
- aliases: [env vars, shell environment variables, env]
- broader: [developer-tooling]
- narrower: [path-environment-variable]
- related: [path-environment-variable, virtual-environment, dependency-management, debugging, integrated-development-environment]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Environment Variables are named values held in the shell or process environment that are inherited by child processes and read at runtime to influence program behaviour without requiring code changes — used for configuration ([[path-environment-variable]] for executable lookup, `VIRTUAL_ENV` for active venv, `PYTHONPATH` for module search), credentials (API keys, database URLs), and feature flags. Environment Variables are the conventional channel by which deployment-specific configuration is supplied to otherwise identical code.

**key_claim**: Environment Variables are the modern conventional substrate for *configuration without recompilation*: by reading deployment-specific values from the environment rather than from hardcoded constants, the same code artifact runs across dev, staging, and production environments — a discipline codified in the Twelve-Factor App methodology and underpinning every modern deployment pipeline that separates code from configuration.

**warning**: Environment Variables are regularly misused as a credentials store committed to source control via `.env` files that end up in version-control history — defeating the very purpose of Environment Variables as a configuration channel by leaking secrets; the substantive discipline is that secret Environment Variables must be supplied by the deployment platform (or a secrets manager), never by a file checked into [[version-control]], and the `.env.example` convention exists precisely to document required Environment Variables without exposing their values.

## Client-Server Architecture

- domain: computer-science
- secondary_domains: [software-engineering, systems-design]
- aliases: [client-server model, client/server pattern]
- broader: [computer-science]
- narrower: []
- related: [language-server-protocol, github-copilot, integrated-development-environment, modular-design, the-extension-ecosystem-as-capability-assembly]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Client-Server Architecture is the distributed-system pattern in which a *client* process initiates requests to a *server* process that holds shared resources and arbitrates access, with the two communicating over a defined protocol — typically over a network but sometimes intra-process or via local IPC. Client-Server Architecture is the foundational pattern for the modern web, for [[language-server-protocol]] (the editor is the client, the language server is the server), and for most multi-user applications.

**key_claim**: Client-Server Architecture's design contribution is *the separation of presentation from authoritative state*: by concentrating shared state on the server and treating clients as views into that state, Client-Server Architecture allows independent evolution of clients (web, mobile, CLI, editor extension) against a single source of truth — which is why the pattern remains dominant despite peer-to-peer alternatives that periodically claim to supersede it.

**warning**: Client-Server Architecture is regularly conflated with *web architecture* specifically, but the pattern is broader: any process that initiates requests to another process holding shared resources implements Client-Server Architecture regardless of transport (HTTP, gRPC, stdio, named pipes); recognising the pattern in non-web contexts — [[language-server-protocol]] is the canonical example — is what allows the same architectural reasoning to transfer between domains.
