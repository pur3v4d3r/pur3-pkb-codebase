# Batch: Social Psychology Anchors

## Social Psychology

- secondary_domains: [psychology]
- aliases: [social psychological science]
- broader: [psychology]
- narrower: [social-influence, social-identity-theory, self-categorization-theory, attribution-theory]
- related: [cultural-psychology, cross-cultural-psychology, social-cognition, persuasion-theory, conformity, obedience-to-authority, in-group-out-group-bias]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Social Psychology is the scientific study of how the actual, imagined, or implied presence of others shapes individual cognition, affect, and behavior — the discipline that takes the dyad, the group, and the culturally situated actor as its primary unit of analysis rather than the isolated organism studied by general experimental psychology.

**key_claim**: Social Psychology has repeatedly demonstrated that situational forces — role, audience, norm, framing — exert effects on behavior that are systematically larger than naive observers (and the actors themselves) predict, an asymmetry codified as the fundamental attribution error and reproduced across the discipline's classic experimental paradigms.

**warning**: Social Psychology is in the middle of a long-running replication audit; several canonical findings (ego depletion, social priming, power posing) have failed to replicate at original effect sizes, so any citation of a single iconic study should be checked against the current meta-analytic estimate before being used as a load-bearing premise.

## Social Influence

- secondary_domains: [persuasion-research, group-dynamics]
- aliases: [interpersonal influence]
- broader: [social-psychology]
- narrower: [conformity, obedience-to-authority, social-proof, persuasion-theory]
- related: [normative-influence, informational-influence, group-polarization, minority-influence, deindividuation, bystander-effect]
- prerequisites: [social-cognition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Social Influence is the family of processes by which one person's or group's presence, communication, or expected reaction changes another person's beliefs, attitudes, or behavior — encompassing normative influence (acting to be liked or accepted) and informational influence (treating others' responses as evidence about reality).

**key_claim**: Social Influence operates on private acceptance and public compliance through partly independent pathways, which is why behavioral conformity and genuine attitude change can dissociate — a person can publicly comply with a group while privately disagreeing, and the relative strength of normative versus informational pressure determines which channel dominates.

**warning**: Social Influence research is often invoked to explain individual decisions retrospectively, but in any specific case, multiple influence mechanisms run in parallel with self-selection and reverse causality; treating a single classic paradigm (Asch, Milgram, Sherif) as a complete model of "why people conform" exports lab-specific assumptions into field settings where they do not hold.

## Prejudice

- secondary_domains: [intergroup-relations, moral-psychology]
- aliases: [intergroup prejudice]
- broader: [social-cognition]
- related: [stereotype-threat, implicit-bias, in-group-out-group-bias, social-identity-theory, self-categorization-theory, attribution-theory]
- prerequisites: [in-group-out-group-bias]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Prejudice is a negatively-valenced attitude toward a person or group held on the basis of perceived category membership, integrating affective (felt antipathy), cognitive (stereotyped beliefs), and behavioral (discrimination tendency) components — distinguished from stereotype (the cognitive content) and discrimination (the behavioral expression) by being the evaluative attitude that links them.

**key_claim**: Prejudice can operate explicitly through endorsed beliefs accessible to introspection and implicitly through automatic associations that diverge from those endorsed beliefs, and contemporary measurement programs treat the two as related but separable constructs whose joint patterning predicts behavior better than either alone.

**warning**: Prejudice reduction interventions are often evaluated on attitude-scale change while ignoring behavioral and structural outcomes, but a measured drop in self-reported Prejudice can coexist with unchanged behavior and unchanged outcome distributions — so the construct is most useful when paired with downstream metrics rather than treated as the terminal target of intervention.

## Pluralistic Ignorance

- secondary_domains: [group-dynamics, social-cognition]
- aliases: [collective misperception]
- broader: [social-influence]
- related: [conformity, bystander-effect, diffusion-of-responsibility, false-consensus-effect, social-proof, group-polarization]
- prerequisites: [conformity]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Pluralistic Ignorance is the situation in which most members of a group privately reject a norm but mistakenly believe most other members accept it, with the result that the unsupported norm persists because no one acts on their actual private view — the misperception is mutual and self-stabilizing.

**key_claim**: Pluralistic Ignorance is one of the few well-replicated mechanisms by which a norm can survive the disappearance of its support base: as long as each member uses others' silence as evidence of others' endorsement, no individual update is triggered, and a costly norm can persist for years across an organization or community without anyone privately favoring it.

**warning**: Pluralistic Ignorance is sometimes invoked as a universal solvent for awkward group behavior, but the construct requires a specific evidentiary signature — private rejection paired with public conformity paired with second-order misperception — and casual labeling without that triad inflates the explanatory weight the mechanism actually carries.

## Self-Categorization Theory

- secondary_domains: [social-identity-research, intergroup-relations]
- aliases: [SCT, Turner self-categorization]
- broader: [social-identity-theory]
- related: [social-identity-theory, in-group-out-group-bias, prejudice, group-polarization, conformity, social-influence]
- prerequisites: [social-identity-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Self-Categorization Theory is John Turner's extension of Social Identity Theory that specifies how a person comes to perceive themselves at different levels of self-categorization — personal, social-group, or superordinate human — and shows that the active level of self-categorization governs perception, comparison, and conformity in any given context.

**key_claim**: Self-Categorization Theory establishes that group behavior is not the breakdown of individual rationality but the predictable output of a self-system that has shifted to a higher-order categorization: when "we" rather than "I" is salient, in-group prototypes guide judgment and the same person's decisions become coherent at the group level rather than the individual level.

**warning**: Self-Categorization Theory is sometimes read as licensing strong-form group-mind claims, but Turner's formulation is explicit that the group level is one of several active self-categorizations, not a replacement of the individual; treating Self-Categorization Theory as eliminative of individual agency misreads its core claim about contextual salience.

## Social Learning Theory

- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, behavior-change]
- aliases: [Bandura social learning theory]
- broader: [social-cognitive-theory]
- narrower: [observational-learning, vicarious-experience]
- related: [social-cognitive-theory, observational-learning, self-efficacy, mirror-neurons, modeling]
- prerequisites: [observational-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Social Learning Theory is Albert Bandura's framework — later extended into Social Cognitive Theory — proposing that human learning occurs in large part by observing others' behavior and its consequences, mediated by the four sub-processes of attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation, which together explain learning that occurs in the absence of direct reinforcement.

**key_claim**: Social Learning Theory broke with strict behaviorism by demonstrating that no overt reinforcement of the learner is required for behavior acquisition: vicarious reinforcement of the model and symbolic representation of the modeled behavior together suffice, which forced learning theory to incorporate cognitive mediation as a non-optional component.

**warning**: Social Learning Theory is often cited to argue that media exposure causes imitation in a one-to-one fashion, but Bandura's framework conditions imitation on the four sub-processes plus model-similarity, perceived self-efficacy, and outcome expectations; ignoring those moderators reduces the theory to an unrecognizable caricature whose predictions consistently fail.

## Self-Construal

- secondary_domains: [cultural-psychology, cross-cultural-psychology]
- aliases: [self-construal style, independent vs interdependent self]
- broader: [self-concept]
- related: [cultural-psychology, cross-cultural-psychology, self-concept, social-identity-theory, autonomy, relatedness-need]
- prerequisites: [self-concept]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Self-Construal is the way a person represents the self in relation to others, classically partitioned by Markus and Kitayama into an independent Self-Construal (the self as a bounded, autonomous unit defined by internal attributes) and an interdependent Self-Construal (the self as defined by and embedded in social relationships and roles).

**key_claim**: Self-Construal moderates a wide range of cognitive and affective processes — attribution patterns, motivation contingencies, emotional appraisals, persuasion responsiveness — and these moderating effects appear both as stable cultural-mean differences and as situationally primable shifts within individuals, which is why the construct is best treated as a state-trait hybrid.

**warning**: Self-Construal is often used as shorthand for "East Asian versus Western" cognition, but the original formulation is psychological and within-population variability is large; using national stereotypes as proxies for Self-Construal both inflates effect sizes and obscures the within-culture and intra-individual variation the construct was designed to capture.

## Secure Attachment

- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, clinical-psychology]
- aliases: [secure attachment style]
- broader: [attachment-theory]
- related: [attachment-theory, basic-psychological-needs-theory, relatedness-need, psychological-resilience, emotional-regulation, self-compassion]
- prerequisites: [attachment-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Secure Attachment is the attachment pattern, identified in the Strange Situation paradigm and the Adult Attachment Interview, in which a person uses an attachment figure as both a safe haven for distress regulation and a secure base for exploration — the pattern most consistently associated with adaptive emotion regulation, exploratory behavior, and relational outcomes across the lifespan.

**key_claim**: Secure Attachment is not equivalent to the absence of distress; secure individuals show distress on separation and seek proximity on reunion just like insecure individuals do — the diagnostic feature is the post-distress trajectory, in which contact with the attachment figure rapidly down-regulates arousal and exploratory behavior resumes.

**warning**: Secure Attachment is often presented as a fixed individual trait determined in infancy, but longitudinal studies show meaningful change across life — particularly through corrective relational experiences — so treating Secure Attachment as a destiny licenses both unwarranted pessimism in the insecurely attached and unwarranted complacency in the securely attached.

## Organizational Psychology

- secondary_domains: [industrial-organizational-psychology, management-science]
- aliases: [I-O psychology, work psychology]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [self-determination-theory, goal-setting-theory, high-performance-cycle, motivational-design, communities-of-practice, transactive-memory-systems]
- prerequisites: [social-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Organizational Psychology is the scientific study of human behavior in workplace settings — covering selection, training, motivation, leadership, team dynamics, organizational culture, and the design of work itself — and serves as the applied bridge between general psychological science and the engineering of workplaces, often paired with industrial psychology under the I-O label.

**key_claim**: Organizational Psychology has shown that workplace outcomes (performance, retention, well-being) are shaped at least as strongly by structural and interpersonal context as by individual selection, and the construct of person-environment fit captures the empirical finding that the same individual will produce systematically different outcomes across different organizational designs.

**warning**: Organizational Psychology findings are often imported into management practice as universal prescriptions, but most validated effects are moderated by sector, occupation, national context, and organizational scale; transplanting an intervention that worked in one organizational ecology into another without re-validation is the modal source of disappointing implementation outcomes.

## Teacher Development

- secondary_domains: [education, professional-development]
- aliases: [teacher professional development, educator development]
- broader: [educational-development]
- related: [pedagogy, communities-of-practice, deliberate-practice, cognitive-apprenticeship, expertise-development, reflective-thinking]
- prerequisites: [educational-development]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Teacher Development is the longitudinal process by which a teacher's pedagogical knowledge, instructional skill, professional identity, and adaptive expertise mature across a career — encompassing initial preparation, induction, in-service learning, mentoring, communities of practice, and the development of pedagogical content knowledge specific to subject and grade level.

**key_claim**: Teacher Development is consistently better predicted by sustained, content-specific, practice-embedded learning experiences than by one-off workshops or seat-time accumulation, and the meta-analytic evidence indicates that durations under approximately fifty hours per topic produce effect sizes indistinguishable from zero on student-learning outcomes.

**warning**: Teacher Development is widely treated as synonymous with the credentialing or compliance hours teachers complete, but compliance hours and developmental change are loosely correlated at best; conflating the two leads to systems that maximize the proxy while leaving Teacher Development itself stagnant.
