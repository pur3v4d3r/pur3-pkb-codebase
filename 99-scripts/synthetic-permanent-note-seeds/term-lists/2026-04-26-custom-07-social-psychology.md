# Batch: Social Psychology

## Conformity

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [group-dynamics, influence]
- aliases: [social conformity, majority influence]
- broader: [social-influence]
- related: [obedience-to-authority, groupthink, normative-influence, informational-influence]
- prerequisites: [social-influence]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Conformity is the adjustment of an individual's beliefs, judgments, or behaviors to align with those expressed by a real or imagined group, encompassing both surface-level public compliance and deeper private acceptance of the group's position.

**key_claim**: Conformity is driven by two analytically distinct motives — informational influence, in which the group is treated as evidence about an uncertain reality, and normative influence, in which divergence is avoided to preserve social standing — and Asch-style line-judgment paradigms isolate normative Conformity by holding informational ambiguity near zero.

**warning**: Conformity effect sizes vary dramatically with cultural context, group composition, and task ambiguity, and the iconic Asch findings are routinely overgeneralized; replication work shows that even a single dissenting confederate collapses Conformity rates, so the "75% conformed at least once" headline conceals a much more fragile phenomenon.

## Deindividuation

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [group-dynamics, self-regulation]
- aliases: [loss of self-awareness, crowd anonymity]
- broader: [group-processes]
- related: [groupthink, conformity, social-identity-theory, mob-behavior]
- prerequisites: [self-awareness]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Deindividuation is the psychological state, classically associated with anonymity and immersion in a group, in which individuals experience reduced self-awareness and weakened internal regulation of behavior, often resulting in actions they would not perform when individuated.

**key_claim**: Contemporary work — particularly the SIDE (Social Identity model of Deindividuation Effects) account — argues that Deindividuation does not produce a generic loss of standards but a shift from personal to social identity, so anonymous group members enact the salient group's norms more strongly rather than abandoning norms altogether.

**warning**: Popular treatments of Deindividuation as "anonymity causes antisocial behavior" misread the evidence: anonymous online groups can become more prosocial when the salient group identity is prosocial, and invoking Deindividuation to explain crowd violence often substitutes a label for an explanation of the specific norms in play.

## Groupthink

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [decision-science, organizational-behavior]
- aliases: [Janis groupthink]
- broader: [group-decision-making]
- related: [conformity, confirmation-bias, polarization, dissent-suppression]
- prerequisites: [group-decision-making]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Groupthink, theorized by Irving Janis, is a pattern of defective group decision-making in which a cohesive in-group's striving for unanimity overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives, producing premature consensus, suppression of dissent, and an illusion of moral and analytic superiority.

**key_claim**: Groupthink's central diagnostic claim is that high group cohesion under threat is not protective but pathogenic for decision quality, because cohesion converts the social cost of dissent into a strong incentive to self-censor, and the resulting unanimity is then misread by the group as evidence of the decision's correctness.

**warning**: Empirical tests of Groupthink as a coherent syndrome have been mixed; while specific antecedents (insulated leader, directive leadership, time pressure) reliably degrade decisions, the full Janis model bundling cohesion, structural faults, and symptoms has not held together cleanly, so invoking Groupthink as a unified explanation often outruns the evidence.

## Halo Effect

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [person-perception, judgment]
- aliases: [halo bias, evaluative halo]
- broader: [cognitive-bias]
- related: [implicit-bias, attractiveness-stereotype, fundamental-attribution-error, anchoring]
- prerequisites: [person-perception]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Halo Effect is the cognitive bias in which an evaluator's overall positive (or negative) impression of a person, brand, or object causes ratings of that target's specific, logically independent attributes to be inflated (or deflated) toward the global impression.

**key_claim**: The Halo Effect is best understood not as a single perceptual error but as a consequence of evaluative coherence — the mind reduces ambiguous attribute information to its global affective summary — which is why the Halo Effect grows as raters become less informed about the specific attributes they are scoring.

**warning**: The Halo Effect is routinely confused with mere correlation among traits; some attributes (warmth and trustworthiness) genuinely covary in people, and labeling that covariation as a Halo Effect treats real structure in the world as bias, which both inflates the perceived prevalence of the effect and obscures the cases where it actually distorts judgment.

## Implicit Bias

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [intergroup-relations, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [implicit attitudes, automatic bias]
- broader: [intergroup-bias]
- related: [stereotype-threat, halo-effect, iat, prejudice]
- prerequisites: [attitudes]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Implicit Bias refers to evaluations and stereotypes that are activated automatically — without conscious intent and often in conflict with explicitly endorsed beliefs — as measured by reaction-time tasks such as the Implicit Association Test, and theorized to influence judgment and behavior outside of awareness.

**key_claim**: Implicit Bias is theoretically important because it predicts that intergroup discrimination can persist even among people with sincere egalitarian beliefs, but its empirical foundations are weaker than the public discourse implies: meta-analyses of the IAT report low test-retest reliability and small, inconsistent correlations with actual behavior.

**warning**: Implicit Bias trainings built on the assumption that reducing IAT scores reduces discriminatory behavior have largely failed to produce durable behavioral change; treating Implicit Bias as the primary explanatory mechanism for structural disparities can crowd out attention to institutional policies and incentives that operate independently of any individual's automatic associations.

## Obedience to Authority

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [influence, ethics-of-research]
- aliases: [Milgram obedience, authority compliance]
- broader: [social-influence]
- related: [conformity, deindividuation, situationism, agentic-state]
- prerequisites: [social-influence]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Obedience to Authority is the tendency to comply with the directives of a person perceived as legitimately empowered to issue them, even when the directed behavior conflicts with the actor's personal moral standards — the phenomenon famously operationalized by Stanley Milgram's shock-administration experiments.

**key_claim**: Obedience to Authority is not adequately explained by sadistic dispositions or coercion; Milgram's data and subsequent reanalyses suggest that participants comply when an authority absorbs perceived responsibility (the "agentic shift") and when the situation supplies a stepwise escalation that never presents a clear refusal point, making the determinants situational rather than dispositional.

**warning**: Reanalyses of Milgram's archives show substantial variation across his many experimental conditions and serious questions about how scripted prods, debriefing, and participant suspicion affected results; citing Obedience to Authority as a fixed "65% will torture" finding flattens a much messier evidence base and overstates how lawful the original effect actually was.

## Social Loafing

- domain: social-psychology
- secondary_domains: [group-performance, motivation]
- aliases: [Ringelmann effect, free riding]
- broader: [group-performance]
- related: [diffusion-of-responsibility, social-facilitation, accountability, group-size-effect]
- prerequisites: [group-dynamics]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Social Loafing is the empirical finding that individuals exert less effort when their contribution to a collective output is pooled and unidentifiable than when their individual performance is evaluated, generalizing the original Ringelmann rope-pulling observation to cognitive and creative tasks.

**key_claim**: Social Loafing is moderated by identifiability and task meaningfulness rather than by group size alone; when individual contributions can be measured or when the task is personally engaging and the group is valued, Social Loafing shrinks substantially or reverses into social compensation, suggesting the effect is fundamentally about evaluation potential, not about crowds dampening effort.

**warning**: Social Loafing is often invoked as a generic argument against group work, but in tasks requiring genuine integration of expertise the comparison "individual effort vs. group effort per person" is ill-posed; loss of individual effort can be more than offset by gains from coordination and division of labor, so Social Loafing critiques apply mainly to additive tasks where pooling buys no synergy.
