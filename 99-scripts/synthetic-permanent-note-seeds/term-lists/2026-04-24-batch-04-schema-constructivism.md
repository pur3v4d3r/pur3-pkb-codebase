# Batch: Schema, Knowledge, Constructivism

## Declarative Schemas

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, knowledge-representation]
- aliases: [declarative knowledge schemas]
- broader: [schema-theory, knowledge-schemas]
- related: [procedural-schemas, declarative-memory, semantic-memory, [[concept-formation]]]
- prerequisites: [schema-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Declarative Schemas are organised structures of factual and conceptual knowledge — categories, hierarchies, propositional networks, conceptual frames — that represent *what* a learner knows about a domain and support recognition, classification, and verbal description, in contrast to procedural schemas that represent *how to do* things.

**key_claim**: Declarative Schemas are the first knowledge structures novices typically build, often through explicit instruction, and provide the conceptual anchors onto which procedural schemas later attach; pedagogy that demands procedural performance before adequate Declarative Schemas exist produces brittle skill that fails when the learner needs to reason about edge cases.

**warning**: Declarative Schemas can be elaborate and verbalisable yet *inert* — present in semantic memory but not retrieved when relevant — which is the well-documented phenomenon of inert knowledge; making Declarative Schemas usable requires conditionalising them on retrieval cues from authentic problem contexts, not merely strengthening the declarative trace itself.

## Procedural Schemas

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, skill-acquisition]
- aliases: [procedural knowledge schemas, production rules]
- broader: [schema-theory, knowledge-schemas]
- related: [declarative-schemas, procedural-memory, [[automaticity]], [[act-r]]]
- prerequisites: [schema-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Procedural Schemas are organised structures encoding *how* to perform tasks — condition-action rules, action sequences, problem-solving operators — that mediate skilled performance and become increasingly automatic and tacit with practice, in contrast to the declarative knowledge structures that describe domains.

**key_claim**: Procedural Schemas in cognitive architectures like ACT-R are typically formalised as condition-action production rules whose firing patterns produce skilled behaviour; this formalism captures the empirical pattern that procedural skill becomes faster, less error-prone, and less attention-demanding with practice while remaining hard to verbalise.

**warning**: Procedural Schemas acquired through practice on narrow problem types do not automatically generalise to structurally similar but superficially different problems; the [[encoding-specificity-principle]] applies — Procedural Schemas are bound to the cue conditions present during their acquisition, which is why varied practice is required to produce procedurally robust performance.

## Knowledge Schemas

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, knowledge-representation]
- aliases: [cognitive schemas, knowledge structures]
- broader: [schema-theory]
- narrower: [declarative-schemas, procedural-schemas]
- related: [prior-knowledge, schema-theory-and-learning, [[scripts]], [[frames]]]
- prerequisites: [schema-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Knowledge Schemas is the umbrella term for the organised cognitive structures — declarative concepts, procedural rules, scripts for events, frames for situations — that constitute the long-term knowledge a learner brings to bear on new situations and through which all new experience is interpreted.

**key_claim**: Knowledge Schemas determine what learners notice, encode, infer, and remember from any given experience; the same lecture, text, or demonstration produces qualitatively different learning depending on the Knowledge Schemas the learner activates, which is why pre-instructional schema activation is among the most cost-effective pedagogical moves available.

**warning**: Knowledge Schemas can be wrong as well as right, and incorrect schemas actively distort new learning toward themselves rather than passively waiting to be replaced; confronting and reorganising faulty Knowledge Schemas (the [[conceptual-change]] task) is qualitatively harder than building schemas where none existed, and pedagogy that merely adds correct content on top of unrevised faulty schemas often leaves the faulty schemas dominant.

## Prior Knowledge

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science]
- aliases: [background knowledge, prior learning]
- broader: [knowledge-types]
- related: [knowledge-schemas, prior-knowledge-activation, meaningful-learning-theory, [[matthew-effect]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Prior Knowledge is the totality of relevant declarative, procedural, and conceptual knowledge a learner brings to a new learning situation — including correct concepts, partial understandings, misconceptions, and skill — and is the single strongest predictor of how much will be learned from any given instructional episode.

**key_claim**: Prior Knowledge produces a Matthew effect in cognition: learners with richer Prior Knowledge encode more from the same exposure, integrate it with more existing structure, and retrieve it from more cues, which compounds initial differences over time and explains why early literacy and early-domain exposure have such lasting consequences.

**warning**: Prior Knowledge is heterogeneous and often incorrect: a learner who has heard "atoms are little solar systems" enters an instruction unit on atomic structure with active misconceptions that can defeat well-designed instruction; pedagogy must elicit and probe Prior Knowledge rather than merely "build on" an assumed clean baseline.

## Prior Knowledge Activation

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [activating prior knowledge, prior-knowledge prompting]
- broader: [prior-knowledge]
- related: [advance-organiser, meaningful-learning-theory, [[anticipation-guide]], [[KWL-chart]]]
- prerequisites: [prior-knowledge]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Prior Knowledge Activation refers to deliberate instructional moves — pre-questions, advance organisers, predictions, KWL prompts, conceptual diagnostic items — that bring relevant existing knowledge into working memory before new content is introduced, so that the new content has anchoring concepts available during encoding.

**key_claim**: Prior Knowledge Activation reliably improves comprehension and retention of subsequently presented material when the activated knowledge is *relevant and correct*; the effect is strongest for texts that are otherwise hard for the reader and weakest for texts that are already well within the reader's domain.

**warning**: Prior Knowledge Activation can backfire when it activates *incorrect* prior knowledge: an unprompted brainstorm at the start of a science unit can entrench misconceptions by giving them a public airing without subsequent confrontation, which is why effective Prior Knowledge Activation pairs elicitation with explicit subsequent revision rather than treating the activation step as sufficient on its own.

## Assimilation

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [Piagetian assimilation]
- broader: [genetic-epistemology, learning-processes]
- related: [accommodation, cognitive-disequilibrium, schema-theory, [[piaget]]]
- prerequisites: [genetic-epistemology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Assimilation, in Piaget's framework, is the cognitive process by which the learner incorporates new information into existing schemas without modifying those schemas — a child who calls a zebra "horse" is assimilating the new instance to a pre-existing animal schema — and is the conservative pole of cognitive adaptation.

**key_claim**: Assimilation is the default response to novelty: the system attempts to interpret new input through existing structure before considering structural revision, which makes Assimilation simultaneously efficient (most input does fit) and risky (when input genuinely doesn't fit, Assimilation can produce systematic distortion that the learner doesn't notice).

**warning**: Assimilation should not be moralised as a learning failure — it is a normal and necessary cognitive process — but its dominance in any given learning episode should be treated as a diagnostic signal: if learners are predominantly assimilating, they are not undergoing [[conceptual-change]], and more cognitive challenge or contradiction is needed to provoke [[accommodation]].

## Accommodation

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [Piagetian accommodation]
- broader: [genetic-epistemology, learning-processes]
- related: [assimilation, cognitive-disequilibrium, conceptual-change, schema-theory]
- prerequisites: [genetic-epistemology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Accommodation, in Piaget's framework, is the cognitive process by which existing schemas are restructured to fit input that resists [[assimilation]] — a child confronted with the structural difference between zebras and horses revises the animal schema to accommodate the new category — and is the productive but effortful pole of cognitive adaptation.

**key_claim**: Accommodation is triggered by [[cognitive-disequilibrium]]: when the gap between schema and input becomes too large to ignore through assimilation, the system either restructures (Accommodation) or rejects the input; pedagogy that wants to produce structural learning must engineer the disequilibrium that motivates Accommodation rather than allowing learners to assimilate the input into unchanged structure.

**warning**: Accommodation is cognitively expensive and is selectively avoided by motivated reasoning when the existing schema is identity-relevant or socially important; understanding why people resist updating their views about politically charged scientific findings (climate, vaccines) requires recognising that Accommodation is not a purely cold cognitive computation but is gated by motivational defences against schema change.

## Cognitive Disequilibrium

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [disequilibration]
- broader: [genetic-epistemology, learning-processes]
- related: [assimilation, accommodation, [[productive-failure]], conceptual-change]
- prerequisites: [genetic-epistemology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Disequilibrium, in Piagetian terms, is the state in which a learner encounters input that cannot be assimilated by existing schemas — producing surprise, confusion, contradiction, or conflict — and which motivates the schema restructuring (accommodation) that constitutes structural learning.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Disequilibrium is the engine of conceptual change: pedagogies that successfully produce schema restructuring (Socratic questioning, productive failure, counter-example sequences, anomalous data) all share the strategy of inducing Cognitive Disequilibrium and then supporting the learner through resolution rather than relieving the disequilibrium prematurely.

**warning**: Cognitive Disequilibrium is productive only when the learner has the resources to resolve it; when disequilibrium exceeds the learner's capacity, it produces frustration, withdrawal, or the entrenchment of the original schema as a defensive response, which is why "challenging" instruction without adequate scaffolding so reliably backfires for novices.

## Genetic Epistemology

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, philosophy]
- aliases: [Piagetian epistemology]
- broader: [developmental-psychology, epistemology]
- related: [assimilation, accommodation, cognitive-disequilibrium, [[piaget]], constructivism]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Genetic Epistemology is Piaget's research programme — published in the 1950s and elaborated until his death — that studied the *origins and development of knowledge* by tracing how cognitive structures emerge through the interaction of biological maturation and environmental engagement across childhood, treating epistemology as a developmental science.

**key_claim**: Genetic Epistemology established constructivism as a developmental claim: knowledge is not absorbed from environment nor unfolded from genes but is *constructed* through the child's active engagement with the world, with the operative mechanisms being assimilation, accommodation, and equilibration acting on increasingly sophisticated cognitive structures.

**warning**: Genetic Epistemology's specific stage sequence (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operations, formal operations) has been substantially revised by post-Piagetian research showing earlier competencies than Piaget's tasks revealed and more domain-specific patterns than the universal stages predicted; teaching the strict stage model as current science misrepresents the field.

## Constructivism

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, education]
- aliases: [constructivist learning theory]
- broader: [learning-theories]
- narrower: [[radical-constructivism], [[social-constructivism], [[cognitive-constructivism]]]
- related: [genetic-epistemology, situated-learning-theory, [[vygotsky]], discovery-learning]
- prerequisites: [learning-theories]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Constructivism is the family of learning theories holding that learners actively construct knowledge through engagement with experience rather than receiving it from an external source — encompassing cognitive constructivism (Piaget), social constructivism (Vygotsky), and radical constructivism (von Glasersfeld) — and standing in deliberate contrast to transmissionist accounts of teaching.

**key_claim**: Constructivism's central pedagogical implication is that the learner's existing structure mediates all learning: the same input produces different acquisition depending on what the learner brings, so instruction must engage with learner construction rather than treating learners as passive recipients — a commitment that informs everything from formative assessment to inquiry pedagogy.

**warning**: Constructivism is widely conflated with "discovery learning" or "minimal guidance," but the underlying epistemological commitment (learners construct) does not entail any particular instructional method; high-guidance, explicit instruction is fully compatible with Constructivism, and the Kirschner-Sweller-Clark critique of "constructivist teaching" is best read as a critique of unguided discovery, not of Constructivism per se.

## Communities of Practice

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [anthropology, organisational-learning]
- aliases: [CoP]
- broader: [situated-learning-theory]
- related: [[lave-and-wenger], [[legitimate-peripheral-participation], distributed-cognition, [[apprenticeship], [[social-learning]]]
- prerequisites: [situated-learning-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Communities of Practice, theorised by Lave and Wenger, are groups of people bound together by shared engagement in a domain of practice — developing shared repertoires of tools, discourse, and identity — through which newcomers gradually become old-timers via [[legitimate-peripheral-participation]], and which constitute the social substrate of much real-world learning.

**key_claim**: Communities of Practice reframe learning as identity transformation rather than information acquisition: becoming a midwife, a software engineer, or a scholar is not primarily a matter of internalising propositions but of becoming a recognised participant whose contributions and judgements the community accepts, which has direct implications for what counts as a learning outcome.

**warning**: Communities of Practice can be exclusionary, slow to admit newcomers, resistant to needed change, and replicators of existing inequities; the romantic framing of CoPs as inherently nurturing learning environments obscures that they are also political structures, and pedagogical proposals to "build communities of practice" must address access, voice, and equity rather than assuming the form delivers the substance.

## Elaboration

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, study-strategies]
- aliases: [elaborative encoding]
- broader: [generative-learning-theory, learning-strategies]
- related: [elaboration-interrogation, elaborative-rehearsal, deep-processing, [[self-explanation]]]
- prerequisites: [generative-learning-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Elaboration is the encoding strategy in which a learner enriches incoming material by generating connections to prior knowledge — examples, analogies, causal explanations, comparisons, contrasts, applications — increasing the number and quality of associative routes by which the trace can later be retrieved.

**key_claim**: Elaboration produces durable retention gains across content types and ages, and is the mechanism behind the effectiveness of self-explanation, elaborative interrogation, generative summarisation, and worked-example study — making it one of the most generally applicable [[learning-strategies]] in the evidence-based toolkit.

**warning**: Elaboration carries a quality requirement that is easy to violate: spurious or incorrect Elaboration (relating new material to a misconception, generating misleading examples) embeds the trace inside an inaccurate network and makes subsequent correction harder; without feedback or accuracy checks, sustained Elaboration can entrench errors as efficiently as it consolidates correct understanding.

## Elaboration Interrogation

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, study-strategies]
- aliases: [elaborative interrogation]
- broader: [elaboration, learning-strategies]
- related: [self-explanation, [[why-questions]], deep-processing, generative-learning-theory]
- prerequisites: [elaboration]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Elaboration Interrogation is the specific elaboration strategy in which a learner asks themselves *why* a stated fact is true and attempts to generate or retrieve an explanatory answer, transforming declarative content into a causally connected schema and producing measurably better retention than re-reading.

**key_claim**: Elaboration Interrogation is rated by Dunlosky et al. (2013) as a moderate-utility strategy with consistent benefits across age ranges and content types, particularly for factual material that has explicable underlying causes — biology, history, physics — where asking "why" recruits relevant prior knowledge that anchors the fact.

**warning**: Elaboration Interrogation works best when learners possess the prior knowledge needed to answer their own "why" questions; for genuine novices, repeated *why* prompts produce shrugs or made-up answers that can be worse than no elaboration at all, which is why the strategy presupposes some baseline domain knowledge before being deployed.

## Interleaving Effect

- domain: learning-science
- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, study-strategies]
- aliases: [interleaved practice, mixed practice]
- broader: [principles-of-learning, learning-strategies]
- related: [[blocked-practice], [[discrimination-learning], [[desirable-difficulties], [[spacing-effect]]]
- prerequisites: [principles-of-learning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Interleaving Effect is the empirical finding that practising multiple categories of problems in mixed order — ABCABC — produces better long-term discrimination and transfer than blocked practice of the same items — AAABBBCCC — despite typically lower performance during the practice itself, making it a paradigmatic [[desirable-difficulty]].

**key_claim**: The Interleaving Effect is robust for category discrimination tasks where learners must recognise *which* procedure or concept applies — algebra problem types, painting styles, bird species — because interleaving forces the learner to perform the discriminative judgement during practice that they will need to perform on novel problems.

**warning**: The Interleaving Effect is often misapplied as a universal study principle, but its benefits are tied specifically to discrimination tasks; for skill acquisition where the same procedure must be smoothed and automated, blocked practice often outperforms interleaving, and the productive rule is "interleave when discrimination matters; block when execution does."

## Fixed Mindset

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [psychology, education]
- aliases: [entity mindset]
- broader: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence]
- related: [entity-theory-of-intelligence, [[growth-mindset], [[carol-dweck]], [[helpless-pattern]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: medium

**definition**: A Fixed Mindset, in Carol Dweck's framework, is the implicit belief that intelligence and ability are stable traits one has in fixed quantities, leading to performance goals (proving competence), avoidance of challenges that risk exposing low ability, and helpless responses to setbacks interpreted as evidence of trait deficiency.

**key_claim**: A Fixed Mindset predicts maladaptive responses to academic challenge — challenge avoidance, effort minimisation, attribution of failure to stable ability — that compound over time as the learner systematically opts out of the high-effort engagement through which competence develops, which is the mechanism Dweck's research programme identified.

**warning**: A Fixed Mindset and its growth-mindset counterpart have been the subject of major replication concerns: large-scale interventions (Yeager et al. 2019) find effects much smaller than the popular literature suggests, often confined to specific subgroups; treating Fixed Mindset reduction as a high-leverage universal intervention overstates the evidence and underweights the structural factors (curriculum, teaching, opportunity) that are larger drivers of outcomes.

## Entity Theory of Intelligence

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [psychology, education]
- aliases: [entity theory]
- broader: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence]
- related: [fixed-mindset, [[incremental-theory-of-intelligence], [[implicit-theories], [[carol-dweck]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: medium

**definition**: The Entity Theory of Intelligence is the implicit belief — central to Dweck's research programme — that intelligence is a fixed entity ("you have a certain amount and that's it"), in contrast to the [[incremental-theory-of-intelligence]] that holds intelligence is malleable through effort and learning.

**key_claim**: The Entity Theory of Intelligence is the cognitive substrate of the [[fixed-mindset]] behavioural pattern: holding the belief that ability is fixed leads to performance goals, ego-involvement in tasks, and helpless responses to failure, because effort and struggle become diagnostic of low ability rather than of growth in progress.

**warning**: The Entity Theory of Intelligence is correlationally linked to maladaptive patterns, but the causal direction and intervention leverage are more contested than the popular literature suggests; large field replications find weaker and more conditional effects than early laboratory studies, and the Entity Theory framing should be treated as one productive lens, not a foundational cause.

## Incremental Theory of Intelligence

- domain: motivation-research
- secondary_domains: [psychology, education]
- aliases: [incremental theory, malleable view of intelligence]
- broader: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence]
- related: [[growth-mindset], entity-theory-of-intelligence, [[implicit-theories], [[carol-dweck]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: medium

**definition**: The Incremental Theory of Intelligence is the implicit belief that intelligence is a malleable quality that grows through effort, strategy, and instruction, in contrast to the [[entity-theory-of-intelligence]] that holds it fixed; it underwrites the behavioural pattern Dweck calls a [[growth-mindset]].

**key_claim**: The Incremental Theory of Intelligence predicts adaptive responses to academic challenge: learners holding it set learning goals, treat effort as the path to competence, and interpret failure as informative rather than diagnostic of stable trait deficits, producing more persistent engagement and steeper learning trajectories under difficulty.

**warning**: The Incremental Theory of Intelligence is widely promoted in education without adequate attention to its limits: the implicit theory must be paired with effective strategies (effort without strategy doesn't reliably produce growth) and with structural opportunity (effort within a broken system still doesn't produce growth), so promoting Incremental Theory beliefs in isolation risks blaming learners whose context constrains the gains the theory promises.
