# Batch: Philosophy of Mind & Epistemology

## Functionalism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, cognitive-science]
- aliases: [machine-state functionalism, computational functionalism]
- broader: [theories-of-mind]
- related: [multiple-realizability, computational-theory-of-mind, putnam-hilary, fodor-jerry, qualia, eliminative-materialism]
- prerequisites: [theories-of-mind]

**definition**: Functionalism is the philosophical thesis, developed by Hilary Putnam and elaborated by Jerry Fodor and others, that mental states are individuated not by their physical substrate but by their *functional role* — their causal relations to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states — so that anything (biological, silicon, or otherwise) implementing the right functional organization realizes the same mental state.

**key_claim**: Functionalism's central virtue is its compatibility with multiple realizability: the empirical fact that pain in humans, octopuses, and (plausibly) future artificial systems involves radically different physical substrates is well accommodated by Functionalism, which forced behaviorism and identity-theory to give ground and remains the working ontology of most cognitive science.

**warning**: Functionalism faces serious challenges from qualia-based arguments (the absent-qualia and inverted-qualia objections, Block's Chinese-Nation thought experiment, Searle's Chinese Room) which contend that functional organization alone cannot fix phenomenal character; treating Functionalism as a settled view ignores that these objections remain unresolved within the philosophical literature.

## Phenomenology

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, continental-philosophy]
- aliases: [phenomenological method]
- broader: [philosophy-of-mind]
- related: [husserl-edmund, heidegger-martin, merleau-ponty-maurice, intentionality, embodied-cognition, qualia]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-mind]

**definition**: Phenomenology is the philosophical tradition founded by Edmund Husserl and developed by Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, and others, whose program is the rigorous description of conscious experience *as experienced* — bracketing (epoché) questions about the existence and nature of an external world to attend to the structures of intentional consciousness, embodied perception, and lived temporality.

**key_claim**: Phenomenology established that first-person experiential structure is a legitimate object of systematic inquiry, distinct from both introspective psychology and third-person cognitive science, and its descriptions of intentionality, the lifeworld, and embodied perception have substantively informed contemporary research programs in embodied cognition, enactivism, and the science of consciousness.

**warning**: Phenomenology in its rigorous Husserlian form is methodologically demanding (the practice of phenomenological reduction is non-trivial and contested) and is frequently invoked in popular and applied settings as little more than a label for "first-person reflection," a usage that the philosophical tradition would not endorse.

## Qualia

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, consciousness-studies]
- aliases: [phenomenal properties, raw feels]
- broader: [consciousness-theories]
- related: [hard-problem-of-consciousness, functionalism, inverted-qualia, knowledge-argument, nagel-thomas, chalmers-david]
- prerequisites: [consciousness-theories]

**definition**: Qualia are the subjective, qualitative, intrinsic properties of conscious experience — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the taste of coffee — that constitute *what it is like* to undergo a mental state, and whose status in physicalist ontology is the central battleground between reductive and non-reductive accounts of consciousness.

**key_claim**: Qualia ground the Hard Problem of Consciousness (Chalmers): even a complete functional and neural account of cognition appears to leave unanswered why those processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all, an explanatory gap that Frank Jackson's Knowledge Argument (Mary the color scientist) and Thomas Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" attempt to formalize.

**warning**: Qualia talk is contested at the foundational level: Daniel Dennett and other eliminativists deny that qualia (in the strong sense of intrinsic, ineffable, private properties) exist at all, treating them as a confused theoretical posit; presenting qualia as an uncontroversial datum of philosophical analysis sidesteps a substantive metaphysical disagreement.

## Intentionality

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [aboutness]
- broader: [philosophy-of-mind]
- related: [phenomenology, mental-content, brentano-franz, searle-john, computational-theory-of-mind, representationalism]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-mind]

**definition**: Intentionality, in the philosophical sense established by Franz Brentano and refined throughout twentieth-century philosophy of mind, is the property of mental states by which they are *about* or *directed toward* objects, properties, or states of affairs — beliefs are about propositions, desires about possible states, perceptions about scenes — a feature Brentano took to demarcate the mental from the physical.

**key_claim**: Intentionality poses a foundational challenge to physicalist theories of mind: physical states do not appear, on their face, to exhibit aboutness, so any naturalistic account of mind owes an explanation of how aboutness could arise from non-intentional physical substrate — the "naturalization of intentionality" project pursued by Fodor, Dretske, Millikan, and others, with no consensus solution.

**warning**: Intentionality is often informally used as a synonym for "intent" or "purpose"; the philosophical sense is broader (any directedness toward content, including beliefs and perceptions, not just goals) and the conflation produces miscommunication between philosophers and applied audiences who hear "intentionality" through the everyday meaning.

## Mental Causation

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, metaphysics]
- aliases: [mind-body causation problem]
- broader: [mind-body-problem]
- related: [mind-body-problem, supervenience, kim-jaegwon, epiphenomenalism, multiple-realizability, exclusion-problem]
- prerequisites: [mind-body-problem]

**definition**: Mental Causation is the philosophical problem of how mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions — can be genuine causes of physical events (notably bodily action) given the apparent causal closure of the physical domain, the supervenience of the mental on the physical, and the threat that any physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause.

**key_claim**: Mental Causation is sharpened by Jaegwon Kim's *exclusion problem*: if every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, and mental causes are not identical to physical causes, then mental causes appear to be either redundant (overdetermination) or excluded (epiphenomenal) — a trilemma that drives much of contemporary non-reductive physicalism and remains a live constraint on theories of mind.

**warning**: Mental Causation discussions sometimes presuppose causal closure of the physical as an unargued empirical premise; the principle is well supported but is itself a substantive metaphysical commitment, and presentations that treat causal closure as obviously true mask the dialectical role it plays in motivating exclusion arguments.

## Consciousness Theories

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, cognitive-neuroscience]
- broader: [theories-of-mind]
- related: [global-workspace-theory, integrated-information-theory, higher-order-theories, qualia, hard-problem-of-consciousness, dehaene-stanislas]
- prerequisites: [theories-of-mind]

**definition**: Consciousness Theories are the family of competing scientific and philosophical accounts of what consciousness is and how it arises, including Global Workspace Theory (Baars, Dehaene: consciousness is information broadcast across a global workspace), Integrated Information Theory (Tononi: consciousness is identical to integrated information Φ), Higher-Order theories (a state is conscious iff it is the object of a higher-order representation), and various predictive-processing accounts.

**key_claim**: Consciousness Theories now constitute a tractable scientific subfield rather than purely philosophical speculation, with active empirical programs operationalizing each leading theory in ways that yield differentiable predictions (e.g., the recent adversarial-collaboration tests pitting GWT against IIT) — a development that has shifted "what is consciousness?" from a stalemated philosophy question to a contested but progressing research program.

**warning**: Consciousness Theories often address different explananda — *access* consciousness vs. *phenomenal* consciousness, the Easy Problems vs. the Hard Problem — and theories are sometimes compared as if they competed on the same playing field when they target different phenomena; assessing competing consciousness theories without first clarifying which sense of consciousness they explain produces misleading verdicts.

## Eliminative Materialism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, philosophy-of-science]
- aliases: [eliminativism]
- broader: [physicalism]
- related: [folk-psychology, churchland-paul, churchland-patricia, intentionality, qualia, theory-theory]
- prerequisites: [physicalism]

**definition**: Eliminative Materialism, defended most prominently by Paul and Patricia Churchland, is the position that *folk-psychological* categories (beliefs, desires, propositional attitudes) are posits of a defective theory and will be eliminated in favor of more accurate neuroscientific concepts — analogous to the way "phlogiston," "vital force," and "ether" were eliminated rather than reduced.

**key_claim**: Eliminative Materialism takes seriously the possibility that the mind sciences will, on the scale of generations, *replace* rather than vindicate everyday mental vocabulary, which raises a sharp meta-scientific question that other physicalist positions soften: physicalism alone does not entail that folk psychology will survive in any form, and Eliminative Materialism is the consistent extreme of taking that contingency seriously.

**warning**: Eliminative Materialism is often dismissed as self-refuting (the eliminativist must believe there are no beliefs); the Churchlands' careful response is that the appearance of self-refutation depends on assuming the very folk-psychological framework whose adequacy is in question, and dismissals that do not engage this response misrepresent the dialectic.

## Mind Body Problem

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, metaphysics]
- aliases: [mind-body problem, mind-brain problem]
- broader: [philosophy-of-mind]
- related: [dualism, physicalism, mental-causation, qualia, descartes-rene, supervenience]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-mind]

**definition**: The Mind Body Problem is the perennial philosophical problem of characterizing the relationship between mental phenomena and physical phenomena — whether they are distinct substances (substance dualism), distinct properties of one substance (property dualism), identical (identity theory), functionally related (functionalism), or one reducible to the other (eliminative or reductive physicalism) — together with the question of how, on any answer, mental and physical can causally interact.

**key_claim**: The Mind Body Problem is not a single problem but a family — the metaphysical question (what mind is), the explanatory question (how to explain experience), the causal question (mental causation), and the epistemological question (how mind is known) — and progress on one need not entail progress on the others, which is why the problem persists despite genuine advances in cognitive neuroscience.

**warning**: The Mind Body Problem is sometimes declared dissolved by appeal to neuroscientific success; the dissolution is at best partial — neuroscience addresses the *easy* problems of cognitive function while the hard problem of phenomenal consciousness remains contested, and triumphalist dissolutions overstate what current empirical advances actually settle.

## Justified True Belief

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology]
- aliases: [JTB, classical analysis of knowledge]
- broader: [theories-of-knowledge]
- related: [gettier-problem, epistemic-justification, foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, plato-theaetetus]
- prerequisites: [theories-of-knowledge]

**definition**: Justified True Belief is the classical analysis of knowledge — traceable to Plato's Theaetetus and dominant through most of the twentieth century — that holds a subject S knows that p iff (1) p is true, (2) S believes p, and (3) S is justified in believing p, with the three conditions taken to be jointly sufficient as well as severally necessary for knowledge.

**key_claim**: Justified True Belief was decisively challenged in 1963 by Edmund Gettier's three-page paper presenting cases (now called Gettier cases) where the three JTB conditions are satisfied but the subject intuitively does not know — driving a half-century research program in epistemology to either find the missing fourth condition, replace justification with a more demanding notion, or argue that knowledge resists analysis altogether.

**warning**: Justified True Belief is often taught as if it were a straightforwardly defective account refuted by Gettier; the modern dialectic is more complex — some epistemologists (Williamson) argue knowledge is unanalyzable, others defend modified JTB, and Gettier's intuitions themselves have been contested by experimental philosophers — so the simple "JTB is dead" narrative misrepresents an active debate.

## Epistemic Justification

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology]
- aliases: [theory of justification]
- broader: [epistemology]
- related: [justified-true-belief, foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, internalism-vs-externalism, evidentialism]
- prerequisites: [epistemology]

**definition**: Epistemic Justification is the property a belief possesses when the believer has appropriate epistemic grounds for it — evidence, reliable cognitive processes, coherence with other justified beliefs, or some combination — and is the central evaluative notion in epistemology, distinct from truth (a justified belief can still be false) and from mere belief (an unjustified belief is still a belief).

**key_claim**: Epistemic Justification splits the field along the internalism-vs-externalism axis: internalists hold that what justifies a belief must be cognitively accessible to the believer (evidentialism, classical foundationalism), while externalists hold that justification depends on objective features of the belief-forming process (reliabilism, proper function) regardless of accessibility — a divide that organizes most contemporary work in the theory of justification.

**warning**: Epistemic Justification is sometimes equated with rationality or with truth-tracking; the construct is more constrained — it is specifically the property whose presence is needed (alongside truth and belief) for knowledge — and conflating justification with broader normative notions in epistemology systematically blurs important distinctions among competing theories.

## Foundationalism

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology]
- aliases: [foundationalist epistemology]
- broader: [structure-of-justification]
- related: [coherentism, basic-beliefs, regress-argument, descartes-rene, classical-foundationalism, modest-foundationalism]
- prerequisites: [structure-of-justification]

**definition**: Foundationalism is the structural thesis about epistemic justification that holds the body of justified beliefs has a *foundational* layer of *basic beliefs* — beliefs justified non-inferentially (by experience, self-evidence, or some non-doxastic ground) — upon which all other (non-basic, inferentially justified) beliefs rest, terminating the regress of justification at the foundational layer.

**key_claim**: Foundationalism's core motivation is the regress argument: if every justified belief required justification from a further belief, justification would either continue infinitely, circle back (coherentism's option), or terminate in unjustified beliefs (skepticism's outcome) — Foundationalism takes the regress to terminate in non-inferentially justified basic beliefs as the only remaining alternative.

**warning**: Foundationalism in its classical (Cartesian) form, requiring infallible basic beliefs, has been widely abandoned as too demanding; modest foundationalism (basic beliefs are merely *prima facie* justified, defeasibly) is much more defensible but loses some of the original motivation for adopting Foundationalism in the first place — a concession that critics argue weakens the foundationalist position more than its defenders acknowledge.

## Coherentism

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology]
- aliases: [coherentist theory of justification]
- broader: [structure-of-justification]
- related: [foundationalism, holistic-justification, bonjour-laurence, lehrer-keith, regress-argument, isolation-objection]
- prerequisites: [structure-of-justification]

**definition**: Coherentism is the structural thesis about epistemic justification that holds a belief is justified by virtue of its membership in a coherent system of beliefs — where coherence is more than mere consistency, requiring positive mutual support, explanatory connections, and relational structure — and that there are no privileged basic beliefs whose justification is non-inferential.

**key_claim**: Coherentism dissolves the regress problem by replacing the linear inferential picture (each belief justified by another) with a holistic one (each belief justified by its place in the network), and offers a unified treatment of justification that avoids what coherentists view as Foundationalism's arbitrary stopping point at allegedly self-justifying basic beliefs.

**warning**: Coherentism faces the *isolation objection*: a maximally coherent belief system may be entirely disconnected from reality (a coherent fictional world), so coherence alone does not appear to be a truth-conducive property, and coherentists' responses (constraints on the belief base, observational input requirements) tend to reintroduce something like foundationalist commitments through the back door.

## Reliabilism

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology, externalism]
- aliases: [process reliabilism]
- broader: [externalist-epistemology]
- related: [epistemic-justification, internalism-vs-externalism, goldman-alvin, generality-problem, virtue-epistemology, proper-function]
- prerequisites: [externalist-epistemology]

**definition**: Reliabilism, developed by Alvin Goldman, is the externalist theory of justification that holds a belief is justified iff it is produced by a *reliable* belief-forming process — one that produces a sufficiently high ratio of true to false beliefs across relevant counterfactual circumstances — making justification a function of the actual reliability of cognitive mechanisms rather than the believer's reflective access to evidence.

**key_claim**: Reliabilism aligns the theory of justification with the kinds of properties that empirical cognitive science actually measures (accuracy, calibration, base-rate sensitivity), giving epistemology a closer connection to psychology than internalist theories permit, and naturally accommodating the justified beliefs of young children and non-human animals who lack reflective access to their cognitive mechanisms.

**warning**: Reliabilism faces the *generality problem*: any token belief is produced by indefinitely many process types of varying reliability ("perception in normal lighting" vs. "vision" vs. "perception of red things"), and Reliabilism does not specify which process-type's reliability matters for justification, leaving the theory underdeterminate at the application level despite its theoretical economy.

## Social Epistemology

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology, social-philosophy]
- aliases: [collective epistemology]
- broader: [epistemology]
- related: [testimony, epistemic-injustice, group-belief, peer-disagreement, fricker-miranda, goldman-alvin]
- prerequisites: [epistemology]

**definition**: Social Epistemology is the branch of epistemology concerned with the social dimensions of knowledge — testimony as a knowledge source, epistemic dependence on experts, peer disagreement, group belief, the design of knowledge-producing institutions (science, journalism, courts), and the social distribution of epistemic credibility — distinct from individualistic epistemology in taking the social as constitutive rather than incidental to knowledge.

**key_claim**: Social Epistemology has shifted from a fringe to a central area of analytic epistemology over the past three decades, in part because most actual human knowledge is acquired via testimony and institutional intermediaries rather than first-personal observation, so an epistemology that does not theorize the social transmission of knowledge omits the dominant pathway by which most beliefs are acquired and credentialed.

**warning**: Social Epistemology covers very heterogeneous projects — formal modeling of group belief, normative theorizing about expert deference, critical analysis of credibility hierarchies — and conflating these subprograms (especially the descriptive and the normative) produces miscommunication about what "social epistemology" claims; specifying which strand is at issue is necessary for productive engagement.

## Epistemic Injustice

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [social-epistemology, political-philosophy]
- aliases: [Fricker epistemic injustice]
- broader: [social-epistemology]
- related: [testimonial-injustice, hermeneutical-injustice, fricker-miranda, credibility-economy, standpoint-theory, identity-prejudice]
- prerequisites: [social-epistemology]

**definition**: Epistemic Injustice, developed by Miranda Fricker, is the family of distinctively *epistemic* wrongs done to a person in their capacity as a knower — most prominently *testimonial injustice* (a hearer accords reduced credibility to a speaker because of identity prejudice) and *hermeneutical injustice* (the resources for a marginalized group to interpret and articulate their own experience are absent from the collective conceptual repertoire).

**key_claim**: Epistemic Injustice extends moral and political philosophy into the epistemic domain by identifying wrongs that are not reducible to material harm or general moral wrong but are *constitutively epistemic* — being wronged as a knower — and gives a framework that has been extensively applied across feminist epistemology, critical race theory, disability studies, and clinical communication research.

**warning**: Epistemic Injustice as a concept has been criticized for over-extension in applied uses, where any disagreement involving a marginalized speaker is sometimes labeled testimonial injustice without verification that *identity prejudice* (rather than ordinary disagreement) is the operative mechanism; the original framework requires that prejudice cause the credibility deficit, and casual applications that skip this requirement weaken the construct.

## Knowledge By Acquaintance Vs Description

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [Russell knowledge by acquaintance]
- broader: [theories-of-knowledge]
- related: [russell-bertrand, sense-data, definite-descriptions, propositional-knowledge, demonstrative-thought]
- prerequisites: [theories-of-knowledge]

**definition**: Knowledge By Acquaintance Vs Description is Bertrand Russell's distinction between two ways of knowing an object: *knowledge by acquaintance* is direct cognitive contact with an object (Russell's candidates were sense-data, universals, and possibly oneself), unmediated by descriptive content, while *knowledge by description* is knowledge of an object as the unique satisfier of some descriptive condition ("the F").

**key_claim**: Knowledge By Acquaintance Vs Description grounds Russell's broader epistemological program: every proposition we can grasp must, ultimately, be analyzable into constituents with which we are directly acquainted, so the apparent reference to objects we have never met (Julius Caesar, distant stars) must be analyzed away in favor of descriptions whose constituents trace back to acquaintance — a constraint that drives Russell's theory of definite descriptions.

**warning**: Knowledge By Acquaintance Vs Description has been substantially superseded in mainstream philosophy of language by direct-reference theories (Kripke, Kaplan) that allow genuine reference to objects we have not met without reductive paraphrase; presenting Russell's distinction as a settled framework rather than a historically influential but now-contested position misrepresents the current state of the field.
