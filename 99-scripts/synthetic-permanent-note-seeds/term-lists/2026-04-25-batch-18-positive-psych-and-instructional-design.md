# Batch: Positive Psychology, Intelligence, Instructional Design & Argumentation

## Broaden And Build Theory

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, emotion-regulation]
- aliases: [Fredrickson broaden-and-build]
- broader: [positive-emotion-theory]
- related: [positive-emotions, fredrickson-barbara, undoing-effect, upward-spirals, psychological-resilience, character-strengths]
- prerequisites: [positive-emotion-theory]

**definition**: Broaden And Build Theory, developed by Barbara Fredrickson, is the positive-psychology account of the function of positive emotions, holding that whereas negative emotions narrow attention and behavioral repertoires for immediate threat response, positive emotions *broaden* momentary thought-action repertoires and over time *build* enduring personal resources — physical, intellectual, social, and psychological — that remain available long after the triggering emotion has passed.

**key_claim**: Broaden And Build Theory reframes positive emotions as functional rather than ornamental: they are not simply pleasant by-products of well-being but evolved mechanisms whose adaptive role is the long-term construction of resource reserves, which gives positive psychology a principled functional account complementary to the well-established functional accounts of negative emotions in evolutionary affective science.

**warning**: Broaden And Build Theory's experimental support is real but uneven across its specific claims; the broadening of attention has the strongest empirical foundation, while the long-term resource-building claims rely on longitudinal evidence with the inferential limitations that domain inherits, so practitioner applications that promise specific resource gains from positive-emotion induction overstate what the underlying evidence actually establishes.

## Learned Optimism

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, cognitive-therapy]
- aliases: [Seligman learned optimism, optimistic explanatory style]
- broader: [explanatory-style]
- related: [learned-helplessness, explanatory-style, seligman-martin, cognitive-restructuring, attributional-style, depression-prevention]
- prerequisites: [explanatory-style]

**definition**: Learned Optimism, developed by Martin Seligman, is the cognitive-behavioral framework that characterizes optimism and pessimism as *learned explanatory styles* — habitual ways of explaining good and bad events along three dimensions (permanence, pervasiveness, personalization) — and demonstrates that pessimistic explanatory style can be modified through cognitive techniques to confer measurable benefits in mood, performance, and resilience.

**key_claim**: Learned Optimism gives optimism a non-mystical operationalization (a measurable explanatory-style profile) and a teachable mechanism (cognitive disputation of pessimistic explanations), placing it on the same methodological footing as cognitive therapy for depression — to which it is in fact closely related, as Seligman's work on learned helplessness directly informed Beck's cognitive theory of depression.

**warning**: Learned Optimism is sometimes oversold in popular self-help adaptations as a guarantee of life outcomes; the empirical effects, though real, are moderate, are most reliable for affective and motivational outcomes rather than objective achievement, and can be counterproductive when optimism is detached from accurate reality assessment — defensive pessimism, for instance, is the better strategy for some personality profiles.

## Psychological Resilience

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, clinical-psychology]
- aliases: [resilience]
- broader: [positive-psychology]
- related: [post-traumatic-growth, broaden-and-build-theory, coping-strategies, hardiness, masten-ann, ego-resiliency]
- prerequisites: [positive-psychology]

**definition**: Psychological Resilience is the capacity to maintain or recover psychological functioning in the face of adversity, trauma, threat, or significant stress — operationalized in the empirical literature as the absence of expected adverse outcomes (chronic PTSD, prolonged depression) following exposure to events that typically produce them — distinct from both passive lack of distress and active coping in being characterized by *adaptive functional continuity*.

**key_claim**: Psychological Resilience is, contrary to popular framing, the *modal* adult outcome following most adverse events: empirical work by Bonanno and others shows that the majority of adults exposed to bereavement, disaster, or trauma exhibit resilience trajectories rather than chronic disturbance, recasting resilience as the typical case rather than the exceptional achievement and shifting research attention toward understanding the minority who do not exhibit it.

**warning**: Psychological Resilience as a construct is contested at the boundaries: definitions vary across studies (absence of pathology vs. presence of growth vs. functional continuity), measurement is heterogeneous, and the construct can shade into victim-blaming when individual resilience is invoked to explain away the role of structural conditions in producing distress; using "resilience" without specifying the operationalization invites these confusions.

## Post Traumatic Growth

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, trauma-studies]
- aliases: [PTG, Tedeschi-Calhoun PTG]
- broader: [positive-psychology]
- related: [psychological-resilience, trauma, meaning-making, tedeschi-richard, calhoun-lawrence, benefit-finding]
- prerequisites: [psychological-resilience]

**definition**: Post Traumatic Growth, formalized by Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, is the phenomenon in which a person reports positive psychological changes — in self-perception, relationships, life philosophy, sense of new possibilities, or spiritual development — *as a consequence of* the cognitive struggle following a major life crisis, and is characterized as growth that goes beyond mere recovery to a higher level of functioning than pre-trauma baseline.

**key_claim**: Post Traumatic Growth is empirically distinguishable from resilience: where resilience is functional continuity through adversity, PTG is *transformative change emerging from* adversity, with both occurring at meaningful rates in trauma-exposed populations and predicted by partly different individual and contextual variables — establishing positive trauma sequelae as a legitimate research target alongside the long-dominant focus on PTSD.

**warning**: Post Traumatic Growth measurement relies on self-report and is vulnerable to *perceived* growth that does not correspond to *actual* behavioral or functional improvement; longitudinal and informant-rated studies show meaningful but smaller effects than the cross-sectional self-report literature suggests, so claims about PTG should specify the measurement approach to avoid inheriting the inflation built into the most commonly cited self-report-only estimates.

## Character Strengths

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, virtue-ethics]
- aliases: [VIA character strengths, signature strengths]
- broader: [positive-psychology]
- related: [virtues, peterson-christopher, seligman-martin, via-classification, signature-strengths, psychological-flourishing]
- prerequisites: [positive-psychology]

**definition**: Character Strengths, in the VIA classification developed by Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman, are 24 positive personality traits organized under six broad virtues (wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence) and identified through cross-cultural and historical analysis as morally-valued capacities universally recognized across major moral traditions, providing positive psychology with a non-pathology-based parallel to the DSM's classification of disorders.

**key_claim**: Character Strengths gives positive psychology a structured, measurable taxonomy of positive traits — an explicit corrective to the field's prior focus on what goes wrong — and the related "signature strengths" intervention (identifying one's top strengths and deliberately deploying them in new ways) has accumulated experimental support as a relatively reliable producer of well-being gains, with effect sizes comparable to many established positive interventions.

**warning**: Character Strengths' cross-cultural universality claim is sometimes overstated; the VIA classification was derived from a curated set of philosophical and religious traditions, and the universality argument is closer to "broadly recognized across these traditions" than to "empirically universal across all human cultures," so framing the 24 strengths as scientifically established cultural universals overshoots what the construction methodology actually warranted.

## Psychological Flourishing

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, well-being-research]
- aliases: [flourishing, eudaimonic well-being, PERMA]
- broader: [well-being-theories]
- related: [eudaimonia, hedonic-vs-eudaimonic-well-being, perma-model, seligman-martin, ryff-carol, character-strengths]
- prerequisites: [well-being-theories]

**definition**: Psychological Flourishing is the conception of well-being as not merely the presence of positive feelings (hedonic well-being) but the realization of *eudaimonic* goods — meaning, engagement, positive relationships, accomplishment, autonomy, personal growth — typically operationalized through frameworks such as Seligman's PERMA model (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment) or Ryff's six-factor model of psychological well-being.

**key_claim**: Psychological Flourishing as a construct dissociates well-being from happiness in the popular sense: empirically, eudaimonic indicators predict different outcomes than hedonic indicators (longer-term mental health, physiological markers, longevity correlations) and are independently modifiable, which justifies treating flourishing as a richer target for psychological intervention than mood-focused happiness research alone.

**warning**: Psychological Flourishing is operationalized differently across the major frameworks (PERMA, Ryff's six factors, Diener's flourishing scale, Keyes's mental-health continuum), and these operationalizations capture overlapping but distinct constructs; cross-study comparisons that treat "flourishing" as a single construct without specifying which operationalization is in use produce systematically misleading comparisons.

## Benefit Finding

- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, health-psychology]
- aliases: [silver lining effect, finding meaning in adversity]
- broader: [meaning-making]
- related: [post-traumatic-growth, meaning-making, sense-making, illness-narratives, helgeson-vicki, coping-strategies]
- prerequisites: [meaning-making]

**definition**: Benefit Finding is the cognitive process and outcome by which people identify positive consequences, lessons, or meaning in adverse experiences (illness, loss, trauma) — a construct closely related to but narrower than Post Traumatic Growth, focusing specifically on the *identification of benefits* rather than the broader transformative changes PTG encompasses, and operationalized through scales asking respondents to enumerate benefits derived from a specific adverse event.

**key_claim**: Benefit Finding has been studied extensively in health-psychology contexts (cancer, cardiac events, chronic illness) where its presence is associated with better adjustment outcomes, suggesting that the cognitive activity of finding benefits in adversity has psychological-protective value distinct from objective improvement in the underlying situation — a finding with practical implications for therapeutic and patient-support contexts.

**warning**: Benefit Finding's relationship to actual well-being is more complex than early enthusiasm suggested: meta-analyses show heterogeneous effects, some studies find benefit-finding associated with worse adjustment in certain populations or time-frames, and the construct may sometimes reflect denial or socially desirable responding rather than authentic adaptive cognition; presenting Benefit Finding as uniformly beneficial overstates the empirical record.

## Multiple Intelligences Theory

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [intelligence-research, education]
- aliases: [Gardner MI theory]
- broader: [intelligence-theories]
- related: [gardner-howard, general-factor-g, triarchic-theory-of-intelligence, intelligence-testing, modularity-of-mind, learning-styles]
- prerequisites: [intelligence-theories]

**definition**: Multiple Intelligences Theory, proposed by Howard Gardner, is the framework that posits human intelligence is not a single general capacity but a collection of relatively independent intelligences — originally seven, later expanded — including linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalist intelligences, each with its own developmental trajectory, neural substrate, and end-states of expertise.

**key_claim**: Multiple Intelligences Theory had substantial influence on educational practice and on the cultural framing of "different kinds of smart," and its historical contribution is the legitimation of non-academic forms of capacity within institutions previously dominated by linguistic and logical-mathematical assessment — an effect on educational discourse that persists even as the theory's empirical foundations have been heavily contested.

**warning**: Multiple Intelligences Theory is, by mainstream intelligence-research consensus, *not empirically supported* as a theory of intelligence: the proposed intelligences fail standard tests of independence (they correlate with each other and with g), the theory was constructed on conceptual rather than psychometric grounds, and Gardner himself has acknowledged the limited empirical support; presenting MI Theory as established intelligence science misrepresents both the evidence and the disciplinary verdict.

## Triarchic Theory Of Intelligence

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [intelligence-research]
- aliases: [Sternberg triarchic theory]
- broader: [intelligence-theories]
- related: [sternberg-robert, analytical-intelligence, creative-intelligence, practical-intelligence, general-factor-g, multiple-intelligences-theory]
- prerequisites: [intelligence-theories]

**definition**: The Triarchic Theory Of Intelligence, developed by Robert Sternberg, is the framework partitioning intelligence into three components — *analytical* intelligence (the capacities measured by standard IQ tests), *creative* intelligence (the capacity to deal with novelty and produce original work), and *practical* intelligence (the capacity to apply knowledge to real-world contexts, sometimes glossed as "tacit knowledge" or "street smarts") — and arguing that conventional intelligence assessment overweights analytical at the expense of the other two.

**key_claim**: The Triarchic Theory Of Intelligence retains more empirical respectability than Multiple Intelligences Theory: the analytical-creative-practical distinction has accumulated some incremental-validity evidence (practical intelligence measures predict job performance beyond what cognitive ability tests predict, in some studies), giving the framework a more defensible empirical posture even if it remains contested whether the three components are genuinely separable from g.

**warning**: The Triarchic Theory Of Intelligence's empirical support, while better than MI Theory's, is itself contested — the incremental-validity findings for practical intelligence have not consistently replicated, the construct of "tacit knowledge" overlaps substantially with conscientiousness and domain expertise, and Sternberg's measures have faced methodological critiques; advocates and critics disagree, and presenting the triarchic theory as either established or refuted overstates the resolution of an active debate.

## General Factor G

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [intelligence-research, psychometrics]
- aliases: [g, general intelligence, Spearman g]
- broader: [intelligence-theories]
- related: [spearman-charles, factor-analysis, fluid-intelligence, crystallized-intelligence, multiple-intelligences-theory, intelligence-testing]
- prerequisites: [intelligence-theories]

**definition**: General Factor G is the statistical factor extracted from the consistent positive intercorrelations among diverse cognitive ability tests — first identified by Charles Spearman in 1904 — interpreted as a general cognitive capacity that contributes to performance across all cognitive tasks, alongside task-specific abilities, and constituting the most-replicated finding in differential psychology over more than a century of research.

**key_claim**: General Factor G is exceptionally robust as an empirical regularity: the *positive manifold* (positive correlations among all standard cognitive tests) replicates across decades, cultures, ages, and test types, and g extracted from any sufficiently broad cognitive battery correlates substantially with g extracted from any other — making g one of the best-established constructs in psychology, regardless of theoretical disputes about what underlying mechanism it reflects.

**warning**: General Factor G's robustness as a *statistical* regularity does not settle the *mechanistic* interpretation: g is consistent with multiple underlying causal pictures (a single biological capacity, mutualism among independent cognitive abilities, or sampling effects from heterogeneous abilities), and the popular framing of g as "general intelligence" embeds a specific causal interpretation that the statistical evidence alone does not establish.

## Practical Intelligence

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [intelligence-research, organizational-psychology]
- aliases: [tacit knowledge, street smarts]
- broader: [intelligence-theories]
- related: [triarchic-theory-of-intelligence, sternberg-robert, tacit-knowledge, expertise, common-sense, social-intelligence]
- prerequisites: [triarchic-theory-of-intelligence]

**definition**: Practical Intelligence, in Sternberg's program, is the cognitive capacity to apply knowledge effectively to real-world contexts — characterized by the acquisition and use of *tacit knowledge* (knowledge of how to do things that is rarely explicitly taught and is often acquired implicitly from experience) — and contrasted with the analytical intelligence measured by conventional IQ tests, which assesses well-defined problems with known correct answers.

**key_claim**: Practical Intelligence as a construct attempts to operationalize the long-standing observation that conventional IQ tests fail to capture some cognitive capacities relevant to everyday and professional success; the strongest version of the empirical case is that tacit-knowledge measures predict job performance with some incremental validity over IQ in certain occupations, justifying the construct as worth measuring rather than dismissing.

**warning**: Practical Intelligence's empirical record is mixed: the incremental-validity claims have been challenged on methodological grounds (Gottfredson and others have argued the apparent incremental validity disappears under more rigorous controls, and the construct overlaps substantially with conscientiousness and job-specific expertise), so adopting Practical Intelligence as an established complement to IQ understates the contested status of the supporting evidence.

## Wisdom

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [positive-psychology, philosophy]
- aliases: [psychological wisdom, Berlin wisdom paradigm]
- broader: [character-strengths]
- related: [practical-intelligence, baltes-paul, sternberg-robert, character-strengths, expertise-fundamental-pragmatics-of-life, judgment]
- prerequisites: [character-strengths]

**definition**: Wisdom, in the empirical psychological tradition (notably the Berlin Wisdom Paradigm of Paul Baltes and colleagues), is operationalized as expert-level knowledge about the *fundamental pragmatics of life* — uncertain and conflict-laden problems of human existence — and is assessed through structured think-aloud responses to dilemmas, scored against criteria including factual knowledge, procedural knowledge, lifespan contextualism, value relativism, and recognition and management of uncertainty.

**key_claim**: Wisdom in the Berlin paradigm and related programs has been moved from a purely philosophical or popular notion into a measurable psychological construct with documented developmental, individual-difference, and intervention findings — including the empirical regularity that wisdom-related performance does not increase monotonically with age (contradicting the folk assumption) but is associated with specific combinations of personality, professional experience, and cognitive style.

**warning**: Wisdom is operationalized differently across competing programs (Berlin, Sternberg's balance theory, Ardelt's three-dimensional model, Grossmann's wise-reasoning paradigm), and these operationalizations measure overlapping but distinct phenomena; treating "wisdom" as a single construct in cross-program syntheses produces conclusions that depend on unacknowledged choices about which operationalization is privileged.

## Gagnes Conditions Of Learning

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [Gagne nine events of instruction, Conditions of Learning]
- broader: [instructional-design-theories]
- related: [gagne-robert, nine-events-of-instruction, learning-hierarchies, instructional-design-models, addie-model, merrill-s-first-principles]
- prerequisites: [instructional-design-theories]

**definition**: Gagnes Conditions Of Learning is the instructional-design framework developed by Robert Gagné that identifies five categories of learning outcomes (intellectual skills, cognitive strategies, verbal information, motor skills, attitudes) and specifies the *internal* and *external* conditions required for each, operationalized for instructional practice through Gagné's *Nine Events of Instruction* — a sequenced template (gain attention, inform learners of objectives, stimulate recall of prior learning, present content, provide guidance, elicit performance, provide feedback, assess performance, enhance retention and transfer) that organizes the conditions into a teaching workflow.

**key_claim**: Gagnes Conditions Of Learning was foundational to the emergence of instructional design as a discipline distinct from educational psychology and pedagogy: by tying specific instructional events to specific learning processes, Gagné gave instructional designers a principled basis for designing instruction rather than relying on craft intuition, an influence visible in the structure of nearly all subsequent ID models including ADDIE and Merrill's First Principles.

**warning**: Gagnes Conditions Of Learning is sometimes mechanically applied as a script rather than as a principled framework: the Nine Events were intended as conditions to be satisfied (potentially in various orders, using various techniques) rather than as a fixed nine-step sequence to be marched through, and lock-step applications of the events miss Gagné's underlying point about matching instructional events to the conditions actually needed for the targeted learning outcome.

## Merrill S First Principles

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology]
- aliases: [First Principles of Instruction, Merrill principles]
- broader: [instructional-design-theories]
- related: [merrill-david, problem-centered-instruction, activation-principle, demonstration-principle, application-principle, integration-principle]
- prerequisites: [instructional-design-theories]

**definition**: Merrill S First Principles, formulated by M. David Merrill through synthesis of major instructional-design theories, are five principles that he argued are common to effective instruction across theoretical traditions: instruction is most effective when learners (1) are engaged in solving real-world *problems*, (2) *activate* prior knowledge as a foundation, (3) observe a *demonstration* of new knowledge, (4) *apply* new knowledge, and (5) *integrate* new knowledge into their everyday life.

**key_claim**: Merrill S First Principles offers a relatively rare cross-theoretical convergence in instructional design: the five principles emerged as common ground from analysis of disparate ID frameworks (Gagné, Reigeluth, van Merriënboer, others), giving practitioners a defensible minimal set of design commitments that does not require choosing among competing ID schools and that has accumulated empirical evaluation in subsequent intervention studies.

**warning**: Merrill S First Principles is sometimes treated as a sufficient ID framework on its own; in practice the principles are deliberately abstract and require complementary mid-level design decisions (sequencing, assessment, scaffolding) that Merrill's framework does not specify, so adopting the First Principles as the entire ID toolkit produces designs that satisfy the principles formally but fall short on the implementation choices the framework intentionally leaves underdetermined.

## Addie Model

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, training-design]
- aliases: [ADDIE]
- broader: [instructional-design-models]
- related: [analyze-design-develop-implement-evaluate, instructional-systems-design, gagnes-conditions-of-learning, merrill-s-first-principles, agile-instructional-design, sam-model]
- prerequisites: [instructional-design-models]

**definition**: The ADDIE Model is the most widely-used instructional-systems-design process model, organizing instructional development into five phases — *Analysis* (audience, context, needs), *Design* (objectives, sequence, assessments), *Development* (production of instructional materials), *Implementation* (delivery), and *Evaluation* (formative throughout, summative after) — with ADDIE serving as the canonical reference for ID process work despite no single original author and various competing rapid and agile alternatives.

**key_claim**: The ADDIE Model's longevity reflects its function as a *language of organization* for ID work rather than as a prescriptive method: ADDIE phases give cross-organizational ID teams shared vocabulary and shared expectations about what work each phase produces, which is why ADDIE persists as the discipline's lingua franca even where practitioners follow more iterative or agile execution patterns within each phase.

**warning**: The ADDIE Model is frequently caricatured as inherently waterfall and contrasted with "agile" alternatives; in practice ADDIE phases are explicitly meant to be iterative with formative evaluation looping back to earlier phases, and the waterfall reading is a misreading of the model — though the misreading has been widespread enough that practitioners working in agile contexts often need to either rebrand their work as non-ADDIE or actively defend ADDIE against the strawman version of itself.

## Learning Objectives Taxonomy

- domain: instructional-design
- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, assessment]
- aliases: [Bloom's taxonomy, revised Bloom's taxonomy]
- broader: [educational-objectives]
- related: [bloom-benjamin, anderson-krathwohl, cognitive-domain, affective-domain, psychomotor-domain, learning-objectives, marzano-taxonomy]
- prerequisites: [educational-objectives]

**definition**: Learning Objectives Taxonomy refers to the family of hierarchical classifications of educational objectives — most prominently Bloom's Taxonomy (1956) for the cognitive domain, organized as Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation, with the Anderson-Krathwohl revision (2001) reframing the levels as verbs (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) and elevating Create to the top — used to specify and assess the cognitive complexity targeted by instruction.

**key_claim**: Learning Objectives Taxonomy gave education a shared vocabulary for *cognitive complexity* in objectives and assessment, allowing curricula to be examined for whether they over-target the lower levels (recall) at the expense of higher-order thinking (analysis, evaluation, creation) — an analytical capability that has structured curriculum reform, assessment design, and instructional planning across decades and educational systems.

**warning**: Learning Objectives Taxonomy levels are not as cleanly hierarchical as the original visual presentation suggests: tasks routinely engage multiple levels simultaneously, "higher" levels can sometimes be achieved without mastery of "lower" levels in the traditional sequence, and the empirical evidence for the strict hierarchy has always been modest; using the taxonomy as a rigid scaffold rather than a heuristic vocabulary embeds assumptions the underlying theory does not warrant.

## Toulmin Argument Model

- domain: argumentation
- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, critical-thinking, philosophy-of-argument]
- aliases: [Toulmin model, Toulmin scheme]
- broader: [argumentation-models]
- related: [toulmin-stephen, claim-data-warrant, argumentation-theory, rhetorical-analysis, informal-logic, rebuttal]
- prerequisites: [argumentation-models]

**definition**: The Toulmin Argument Model, developed by Stephen Toulmin in *The Uses of Argument* (1958), is a structural model of practical argument that decomposes arguments into six components — *claim* (the conclusion advanced), *data* (the grounds supporting it), *warrant* (the inferential principle licensing the move from data to claim), *backing* (support for the warrant), *qualifier* (the strength of the claim, e.g., "presumably"), and *rebuttal* (conditions under which the claim would not hold) — providing a tool for analyzing real-world reasoning that formal logic was not designed to model.

**key_claim**: The Toulmin Argument Model became foundational to contemporary informal-logic and argumentation studies because it captured features of real arguments — defeasibility, field-dependence of warrants, qualified conclusions — that classical logic abstracts away, giving rhetoric, composition studies, and applied epistemology a structural vocabulary suited to the kinds of arguments people actually make outside formalized contexts.

**warning**: The Toulmin Argument Model is frequently taught as a rigid template for *constructing* arguments, often producing wooden writing in which every essay marches through claim-data-warrant in fixed order; Toulmin's purpose was *analysis* of existing arguments rather than a writing template, and the prescriptive use distorts both the model and the writing it shapes.

## Explanatory Coherence

- domain: argumentation
- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, cognitive-science]
- aliases: [Thagard's explanatory coherence, ECHO]
- broader: [theory-evaluation]
- related: [thagard-paul, inference-to-the-best-explanation, theoretical-virtues, explanation, coherentism, scientific-reasoning]
- prerequisites: [theory-evaluation]

**definition**: Explanatory Coherence, developed by Paul Thagard, is a theory of theory-evaluation that holds the acceptability of a hypothesis is determined by its *coherence* with other accepted propositions, where coherence is operationalized through seven principles (symmetry, explanation, analogy, data priority, contradiction, competition, acceptance) and computed via Thagard's ECHO connectionist model that takes propositions as nodes and coherence/incoherence relations as weighted links.

**key_claim**: Explanatory Coherence operationalizes the otherwise hand-waved notion of "inference to the best explanation" by giving precise meaning to *better explanation* — a hypothesis is better when it explains more, is explained by other accepted propositions, displays analogies to accepted explanations, and is not contradicted by data — making theory-choice a tractable computation rather than a purely intuitive comparison.

**warning**: Explanatory Coherence's ECHO model, while a major formal contribution to philosophy of science, depends on prior choices about which propositions to include and what coherence/incoherence relations to assert, and these choices smuggle in much of the work that the model is supposed to perform; treating Explanatory Coherence as an objective metric of theory acceptability obscures the role of upstream judgment in setting up the inputs the algorithm then operates on.

## Rhetorical Modes

- domain: argumentation
- secondary_domains: [rhetoric, composition-studies, persuasion]
- aliases: [modes of discourse, modes of writing, ethos-pathos-logos]
- broader: [rhetoric]
- related: [aristotle, ethos, pathos, logos, narration, exposition, description, argumentation, persuasion]
- prerequisites: [rhetoric]

**definition**: Rhetorical Modes is a term used in two related senses: (1) the classical Aristotelian *appeals* — *ethos* (credibility of the speaker), *pathos* (emotional engagement of the audience), and *logos* (logical structure of the argument) — that constitute the persuasive resources available to any speaker, and (2) the composition-studies *modes of discourse* — narration, description, exposition, and argumentation — that classify the dominant purposes of pieces of writing.

**key_claim**: Rhetorical Modes in the Aristotelian sense remains a productive analytical framework for any persuasive communication: identifying which appeals a piece of communication is making, and which it is neglecting or overusing, is a robust diagnostic that survives the wide variations across media (oral, written, visual, digital) and across centuries of rhetorical practice precisely because it tracks irreducible features of communicative situations rather than features of any particular medium.

**warning**: Rhetorical Modes in the composition-studies sense (narration, description, exposition, argumentation) is a pedagogical taxonomy whose neat separation does not survive examination of actual texts, which routinely blend modes; treating the modes as discrete writing-types to be practiced in isolation produces artificial student writing and ignores how mode-blending is itself a core craft skill of mature prose.
