# Batch: B03-02 Output Quality and Coherence

## Discourse Coherence in LLM Outputs

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, computational-linguistics, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [textual coherence in LLMs, discourse structure in AI-generated text, inter-sentence coherence]
- broader: [natural-language-generation, computational-linguistics, large-language-models]
- narrower: [narrative-consistency-prompting, stance-consistency-across-output, non-sequitur-detection-in-outputs]
- related: [narrative-consistency-prompting, contradiction-detection-in-outputs, stance-consistency-across-output, register-and-tone-control]
- prerequisites: [discourse-analysis, computational-linguistics, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Discourse Coherence in LLM Outputs refers to the structural and semantic property of LLM-generated text whereby sentences, paragraphs, and sections are logically and topically connected — each unit relates to the preceding and following units through recognisable discourse relations such as elaboration, cause-effect, contrast, exemplification, or temporal sequence — producing text that reads as a unified, purposeful whole rather than as a sequence of isolated statements. Discourse coherence subsumes local coherence (sentence-to-sentence connectivity via coreference, ellipsis resolution, and discourse markers) and global coherence (consistency of topic, stance, and argument structure across the entire output). LLMs frequently generate locally coherent but globally incoherent text — producing paragraphs that are individually fluent but collectively contradictory, topic-drifting, or structurally incoherent when the full output is examined.

**key_claim**: Discourse Coherence in LLM Outputs is the primary distinguishing quality dimension between fluent-but-shallow and genuinely useful long-form LLM text — short outputs of one to three sentences almost never exhibit discourse incoherence because global structure is trivially maintained at that scale, but outputs of five-hundred or more words frequently exhibit coherence failures (contradictory claims across sections, unexplained topic transitions, unresolved coreference, and structural organisation that shifts mid-document) that fluency metrics based on local perplexity completely miss; assessing coherence at the discourse level rather than the sentence level reveals substantially higher rates of LLM output quality failure than standard fluency evaluation suggests.

**warning**: Discourse Coherence in LLM Outputs degrades non-linearly with output length — coherence is reasonably maintained for outputs up to approximately 500 words but deteriorates substantially for outputs exceeding 1,000–2,000 words, particularly for complex argumentative or narrative structures; production deployments requiring coherent long-form outputs should implement explicit structural scaffolding via outline-first prompting, section-by-section generation with coherence checkpoints, or retrieval-augmented context maintenance rather than relying on end-to-end generation to maintain discourse coherence over extended texts.

## Narrative Consistency Prompting

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, creative-writing, prompt-engineering, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [story consistency in LLMs, character and plot coherence prompting, narrative continuity control]
- broader: [discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs, prompt-engineering, creative-writing-generation]
- related: [stance-consistency-across-output, contradiction-detection-in-outputs, context-window-management, multi-turn-dialogue-management]
- prerequisites: [discourse-coherence, prompt-engineering, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Narrative Consistency Prompting refers to the set of prompting strategies and architectural techniques designed to maintain coherent character behaviour, plot logic, causal continuity, and world-state consistency across long-form narrative outputs generated by large language models. Narrative consistency encompasses factual consistency (a character's age, name, and attributes remain stable across the narrative), behavioural consistency (characters act in ways that are coherent with their established motivations and personality), causal consistency (events follow from established causes in the narrative world), and temporal consistency (the ordering and timing of events is internally coherent). Without explicit consistency management, LLMs generating long narratives frequently contradict previously established facts, change character attributes without narrative motivation, and introduce causal impossibilities.

**key_claim**: Narrative Consistency Prompting requires explicit external consistency management at scale — LLMs cannot maintain narrative consistency through in-context attention alone for narratives exceeding their effective context window, because even within the context window the primacy-recency effect causes early-established narrative facts to be underweighted relative to recently generated content; effective production narrative systems maintain an explicit consistency database (character sheets, world-state dictionaries, event timelines) that is refreshed into the context at regular generation checkpoints, treating narrative consistency as an external state management problem rather than a model capability problem.

**warning**: Narrative Consistency Prompting that relies solely on explicit consistency instructions in the prompt (e.g., "maintain consistent character behaviour as described above") provides only weak consistency enforcement — models will acknowledge such instructions and then generate inconsistencies anyway when the relevant context is distant in the window or when the inconsistency produces locally more fluent text; robust narrative consistency requires active detection of consistency violations through a separate verification pass followed by rewriting inconsistent segments, not prevention through instruction alone.

## Logical Entailment Verification

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, formal-logic, natural-language-inference, prompt-engineering]
- aliases: [entailment checking in LLM outputs, logical consistency verification, NLI-based output validation]
- broader: [natural-language-inference, formal-verification, output-quality-in-llms]
- related: [contradiction-detection-in-outputs, claim-strength-calibration, discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs, chain-of-thought-prompting]
- prerequisites: [natural-language-inference, formal-logic, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Logical Entailment Verification refers to the process and prompting strategies used to assess whether the claims made in LLM-generated outputs logically follow from the premises, evidence, and prior claims established in the same output or in the input context — verifying that the generation does not assert conclusions that contradict, are non-sequiturs from, or are unwarranted by the established logical premises. Logical entailment verification can be performed by a separate NLI (natural language inference) model, by a second LLM prompted as a logical auditor, or by structured chain-of-thought prompting that forces the generating LLM to explicitly justify each claim in terms of prior established facts before proceeding. It is a critical quality control step for applications where logical validity of outputs is a hard requirement, including legal drafting, scientific reporting, and formal argument generation.

**key_claim**: Logical Entailment Verification using a second LLM as a logical auditor detects substantially more logical errors in generated text than human reviewers performing a single linear reading — experiments demonstrate that asking a separate LLM to verify each claim against established premises in a generated document catches logical violations that human reviewers miss at rates of 30–60%, particularly for claims separated by many intervening sentences from their relevant premises; implementing a two-stage generate-then-verify pipeline with automatic entailment checking provides quality gates that are both more reliable and substantially faster than expert human logical review for routine document types.

**warning**: Logical Entailment Verification by LLM auditors is only reliable for first-order logical relationships and simple multi-hop inferences — LLM-based entailment checkers fail systematically on modal logic, counterfactual reasoning, negation in complex sentences, and implicit presuppositions, producing a false sense of logical rigor for argument structures that are beyond the LLM auditor's reliable capability; deployers of entailment verification pipelines should characterise the specific logical form types their auditor reliably handles versus misses before trusting verification outcomes for high-stakes applications.

## Non-Sequitur Detection in Outputs

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, computational-linguistics, output-quality, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [relevance failure detection, topical drift detection in LLMs, incoherent transition detection]
- broader: [discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs, output-quality-in-llms, natural-language-generation]
- related: [logical-entailment-verification, contradiction-detection-in-outputs, discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs, narrative-consistency-prompting]
- prerequisites: [discourse-analysis, coherence-theory, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Non-Sequitur Detection in Outputs refers to the identification of sentences, paragraphs, or sections in LLM-generated text that do not logically or topically follow from the preceding content — insertions that lack coherent discourse-level connection to the surrounding material, either because they introduce an unrelated topic, make a claim that does not follow from the established argument, or provide information that is locally fluent but globally disconnected from the response's purpose. Non-sequiturs in LLM outputs arise from model attention failures that cause the generation to latch onto a semantically proximal but thematically tangential continuation, from knowledge injection patterns where the model inserts related but contextually inappropriate facts, and from coherence drift in long-form generation where accumulated context dilutes the original topical constraint.

**key_claim**: Non-Sequitur Detection in Outputs reveals a systematic weakness in standard LLM fluency evaluations — perplexity-based and BLEU-based automatic metrics, as well as human coherence ratings obtained from brief review, fail to flag a substantial proportion of non-sequitur insertions because non-sequiturs are often locally fluent and contextually plausible as isolated sentences; only discourse-level coherence evaluation that assesses each sentence's coherence relation to the preceding and following content reliably detects non-sequitur insertions, indicating that standard automatic quality metrics systematically overestimate the discourse-level quality of LLM outputs.

**warning**: Non-Sequitur Detection in Outputs implemented via LLM self-evaluation is unreliable for the model's own outputs — models exhibit systematic confirmation bias when evaluating their own coherence, rating non-sequiturs they generated as contextually appropriate at substantially higher rates than independent evaluators; effective non-sequitur detection requires either a separate model for evaluation or structured prompts that force explicit step-by-step coherence relationship labelling rather than holistic coherence ratings.

## Hedging Calibration

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, calibration, natural-language-generation, epistemic-uncertainty]
- aliases: [uncertainty expression calibration, epistemic hedge calibration in LLMs, verbal confidence calibration]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, calibration-in-llms, epistemic-uncertainty-in-llms]
- related: [claim-strength-calibration, verbalized-uncertainty, overconfidence-in-llm-outputs, verbosity-control-in-prompts]
- prerequisites: [calibration-in-machine-learning, hedging-in-language, epistemic-uncertainty]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Hedging Calibration refers to the alignment between the degree of epistemic uncertainty expressed in LLM-generated text through linguistic hedges (probably, possibly, it appears, research suggests, I believe, there is evidence that) and the actual probability that the corresponding claims are true. Well-calibrated hedging means that claims expressed with high confidence hedges (it is well established that, research clearly shows, it is certain that) are correct substantially more often than claims expressed with low confidence hedges (it is possible that, some researchers suggest, there is debate about), and that the linguistic hedging spectrum maps monotonically to claim reliability. Poorly calibrated hedging — the dominant LLM failure mode — manifests as confident expression of false claims and uncertain expression of well-supported facts, providing users with systematically misleading reliability signals.

**key_claim**: Hedging Calibration in LLMs is substantially worse than in well-trained human expert communicators — studies measuring the correspondence between linguistic hedge strength and empirical claim accuracy show that LLMs produce confident-hedged claims (it is well established that, clearly) that are false at rates of 15–30%, while producing tentative-hedged claims (possibly, there is some evidence) that are actually well-supported at similar rates, indicating that linguistic hedging in LLM outputs provides less than half the reliability signal it appears to provide and should not be used as a proxy for claim confidence without independent verification.

**warning**: Hedging Calibration cannot be improved by simply instructing the model to hedge more or less — blanket hedging instructions produce either globally under-confident outputs where well-supported claims are unnecessarily hedged or globally over-confident outputs where genuine uncertainties are stated assertively, but do not improve the correspondence between hedge level and claim reliability; improving hedging calibration requires claim-level uncertainty estimation (e.g., through sampling-based consistency measures) that provides an independent reliability signal to drive hedge level, not a uniform hedging policy applied to all output.

## Specificity vs Generality Tradeoff

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, prompt-engineering, natural-language-generation, information-theory]
- aliases: [abstraction-specificity balance in LLMs, level-of-detail control, granularity calibration in outputs]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, abstraction-level-control, prompt-engineering]
- related: [abstraction-level-control, verbosity-control-in-prompts, information-density-optimization, register-and-tone-control]
- prerequisites: [information-theory, prompt-engineering, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Specificity vs Generality Tradeoff in LLM outputs refers to the tension between producing responses that are concrete, detailed, and instance-specific (high specificity) versus responses that are abstract, principled, and broadly applicable (high generality), and the challenge of calibrating the specificity-generality balance to match the informational needs of the user and task. Overly specific outputs enumerate details that overwhelm the user's ability to extract general principles; overly general outputs provide abstractions that fail to translate into actionable guidance. LLMs exhibit a default tendency toward moderate generality that satisfies most users but systematically under-serves users who need either high-specificity technical detail or high-generality architectural guidance, and the appropriate balance is highly context-dependent.

**key_claim**: The Specificity vs Generality Tradeoff in LLM outputs is not well-managed by LLMs without explicit prompting guidance — LLMs default to an intermediate specificity level calibrated to the median user for the topic area, which systematically under-serves both expert users requiring high specificity and novice users requiring high generality; explicit prompting that specifies the desired specificity level (e.g., "give me a specific concrete example" versus "give me the general principle") or the user's expertise level (e.g., "explain for a second-year engineering student" versus "explain for a domain expert") produces substantially better-calibrated specificity alignment than expecting the model to infer the appropriate level from request content alone.

**warning**: The Specificity vs Generality Tradeoff in LLM outputs is confounded with factual accuracy — high-specificity outputs are both more useful when correct and more dangerous when incorrect because errors are specific and thus more difficult to detect through plausibility reasoning alone; practitioners should be more rigorous about verification of high-specificity claims (specific numbers, specific code, specific citations) than high-generality claims (general principles, widely applicable frameworks), as LLM hallucination is harder to recognise in specific-fact form than in general-claim form.

## Abstraction Level Control

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, prompt-engineering, cognitive-load-theory, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [abstraction gradient control, conceptual altitude control, level of abstraction prompting]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, specificity-vs-generality-tradeoff, prompt-engineering]
- related: [specificity-vs-generality-tradeoff, verbosity-control-in-prompts, register-and-tone-control, audience-calibration]
- prerequisites: [abstraction-in-cognition, cognitive-load-theory, prompt-engineering]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Abstraction Level Control refers to the prompting strategies and output design principles used to manage the conceptual altitude at which LLM outputs are pitched — governing whether explanations operate at the level of concrete instances, operational procedures, functional principles, or abstract theoretical frameworks — and ensuring that the abstraction level is consistently appropriate for the user's background knowledge and the task's cognitive requirements. Effective abstraction level control requires matching the output's conceptual altitude to the user's zone of proximal development: too concrete and expert users are buried in instances without extractable principles; too abstract and novice users lack the concrete grounding needed to understand and apply abstract descriptions. LLMs exhibit tendency toward multiple simultaneous abstraction levels within a single output, mixing concrete examples with abstract principles in ways that create cognitive load without adding informational value.

**key_claim**: Abstraction Level Control is the primary differentiator between LLM explanations that produce genuine understanding and those that produce the illusion of understanding — outputs that mix abstraction levels without explicit bridging between them allow users to follow each individual statement while failing to build a coherent mental model, because the transitions between abstraction levels require inference that novice users cannot reliably make; effective prompting that specifies a single target abstraction level with explicit bridging instructions between levels produces substantially higher comprehension rates than the default multi-level explanations LLMs generate without guidance.

**warning**: Abstraction Level Control through prompting is less stable for domain-crossing topics — when a concept spans multiple domains at different abstraction levels (e.g., a biological mechanism described at molecular, cellular, and systems levels), models reliably drift across abstraction levels even when instructed to maintain a single level, because the training data for multi-level topics inherently contains text pitched at multiple conceptual altitudes; reliable abstraction level control for multi-domain topics requires iterative prompting that identifies and explicitly removes abstraction-level drift from generated outputs rather than prevention alone.

## Verbosity Control in Prompts

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, prompt-engineering, information-density-optimization]
- aliases: [length control in LLMs, output size management, response length calibration]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, prompt-engineering, information-density-optimization]
- related: [information-density-optimization, redundancy-reduction-in-outputs, token-efficient-prompting, prompt-token-budgeting]
- prerequisites: [prompt-engineering, large-language-models, information-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Verbosity Control in Prompts refers to the prompting strategies and system design patterns used to regulate the length and information density of LLM outputs, preventing both over-verbose outputs that bury key information in padding and under-verbose outputs that omit necessary explanation. LLMs have strong default verbosity tendencies that are biased toward moderate-to-high verbosity because training data rewards and RLHF preferences systematically favour longer, more complete-seeming responses — raters perceive length as thoroughness even when additional content adds no informational value. Verbosity control requires explicit length guidance in prompts (word counts, sentence counts, or structural constraints), format specifications that impose length constraints implicitly (bullet points, numbered lists, tables), or post-generation filtering that extracts key content from verbose outputs.

**key_claim**: Verbosity Control in Prompts is a persistent challenge because LLM verbosity tendencies are reinforced by RLHF alignment — human raters systematically reward longer, more comprehensive responses over shorter but equivalently informative responses, creating an alignment pressure that makes models verbose even when the task clearly calls for brevity; this means that explicitly instructing models to be brief ("answer in one sentence", "give a concise response") produces shorter outputs but also frequently produces lower-quality outputs where brevity is achieved by omitting relevant content rather than by achieving genuine information density, indicating that verbosity and information density are orthogonal qualities that must be explicitly jointly optimised.

**warning**: Verbosity Control in Prompts interacts with instruction following in ways that produce counterintuitive failures — instructions specifying exact word counts (e.g., "answer in exactly 100 words") produce outputs that approximate but do not precisely meet the target, and the quality of the approximation varies widely across models and task types; robust verbosity control requires output-level validation and re-prompting rather than relying on in-prompt word count specifications as reliable constraints.

## Information Density Optimization

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, information-theory, prompt-engineering, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [content density in LLMs, high-information-per-token outputs, information packing in text]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, verbosity-control-in-prompts, token-efficient-prompting]
- related: [verbosity-control-in-prompts, redundancy-reduction-in-outputs, prompt-distillation, abstractive-context-compression]
- prerequisites: [information-theory, prompt-engineering, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Information Density Optimization refers to the design of prompting strategies and output formatting guidance aimed at maximising the ratio of novel, informative content to total tokens in LLM outputs — producing responses in which each token carries substantial informational weight and filler content, redundancy, hedging padding, and structural boilerplate are minimised. High information density outputs communicate more knowledge per unit of reading time and cognitive effort, reducing cognitive load while increasing practical utility. Information density in LLM outputs is degraded by verbose hedging patterns, repetition of previously stated content, excessive structural scaffolding (over-long introductions and conclusions, redundant transitional phrases), and padding behaviours that RLHF-aligned models use to simulate thoroughness.

**key_claim**: Information Density Optimization through prompting produces measurably different outputs from the same model — experiments comparing standard prompting with information-density-optimised prompting (combining explicit density instructions, structured output formats, and examples of high-density versus low-density outputs) show that dense prompting produces outputs rated by independent evaluators as 20–40% more information-dense without sacrificing accuracy, confirming that LLMs are capable of substantially higher information density than their defaults and that the low-density default is a prompt-sensitive behaviour rather than a fundamental capability limitation.

**warning**: Information Density Optimization creates a reliability tradeoff — maximising information density by stripping hedges and qualifications from outputs produces denser but less epistemically accurate communication, as many hedges are not padding but carry genuine uncertainty information; optimising for information density should not be confused with optimising for compression ratio, and density-optimised prompts must explicitly preserve uncertainty markers, necessary qualifications, and conditionals that carry informational weight while removing genuinely redundant content.

## Redundancy Reduction in Outputs

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, natural-language-generation, information-theory]
- aliases: [repetition suppression in LLMs, duplicate content removal, output de-duplication]
- broader: [information-density-optimization, verbosity-control-in-prompts, output-quality-in-llms]
- related: [information-density-optimization, verbosity-control-in-prompts, abstractive-context-compression, prompt-distillation]
- prerequisites: [information-theory, natural-language-generation, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Redundancy Reduction in Outputs refers to the mitigation of repetitive, duplicate, or self-paraphrasing content in LLM-generated text — content that restates earlier information without adding new substance, either through direct repetition, paraphrastic reiteration of previously made points, or structural patterns where introductions, body content, and conclusions restate identical information in slightly varied forms. LLMs exhibit strong redundancy tendencies due to training on text that uses redundancy as a legitimate pedagogical and rhetorical device (textbooks repeat key concepts, persuasive essays restate their thesis), and RLHF rewards for completeness further incentivise redundant inclusion of previously stated content to signal thoroughness. Redundancy reduction requires explicit prompting guidance, post-generation deduplication, or structured formats that prevent restating rather than amplifying information.

**key_claim**: Redundancy Reduction in Outputs is quantifiable through content novelty metrics — measuring the proportion of each sentence that introduces content not present in preceding sentences reveals that typical LLM long-form outputs contain 20–40% redundant content by this measure, with the redundancy rate increasing with output length and with RLHF alignment degree; redundancy is distributed non-uniformly across output sections, with the highest redundancy in introductions and conclusions where structural conventions most strongly incentivise restatement, suggesting that format constraints that eliminate structural introductions and conclusions produce the largest per-token redundancy reduction.

**warning**: Redundancy Reduction in Outputs should be distinguished from appropriate elaboration and pedagogically motivated repetition — some redundancy is intentional and pedagogically valuable, such as restating a key principle in different terms to improve comprehension, and aggressive redundancy reduction that eliminates all restatement produces outputs that are dense but require higher baseline knowledge to comprehend; the appropriate redundancy level is audience- and purpose-dependent, and indiscriminate redundancy removal optimised for expert users produces outputs inappropriate for educational contexts.

## Contradiction Detection in Outputs

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, formal-logic, natural-language-inference, output-quality]
- aliases: [self-consistency checking, internal inconsistency detection, conflicting-claim identification in LLM text]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, logical-entailment-verification, discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs]
- related: [logical-entailment-verification, narrative-consistency-prompting, stance-consistency-across-output, non-sequitur-detection-in-outputs]
- prerequisites: [natural-language-inference, formal-logic, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Contradiction Detection in Outputs refers to the identification of pairs of statements within LLM-generated text that express logically incompatible claims — direct contradictions (X is true; X is false), partial contradictions (X is a subset of Y; Y is a subset of X), quantifier contradictions (all X are Y; some X are not Y), and temporal contradictions (X happened before Y; Y happened before X) — and the prompting and architectural strategies for preventing or detecting such contradictions before outputs reach users. LLMs generate contradictions across long outputs due to attention limitations, context drift, and the statistical independence of distant generation steps; within short outputs, contradictions are less frequent but still occur when training data contains contradictory sources that the model averages across rather than adjudicating between.

**key_claim**: Contradiction Detection in Outputs using a two-stage pipeline (generate-then-verify) substantially reduces contradiction rates in production LLM systems — studies comparing single-pass generation with generation followed by explicit contradiction-detection prompting show that the two-stage approach reduces inter-statement contradictions by 50–70%, primarily because contradictions that are difficult for the model to avoid during generation are easy to identify in a dedicated verification pass where the model reads the completed output with a focused contradiction-identification task rather than a content-generation task.

**warning**: Contradiction Detection in Outputs via LLM self-verification is systematically unreliable for soft contradictions — LLMs reliably detect explicit logical contradictions (X is true; X is false in adjacent sentences) but miss soft contradictions where incompatible claims are expressed in different sections using different terminology, different levels of abstraction, or different rhetorical modes; robust contradiction detection for soft inconsistencies requires structured claim extraction (converting all claims to canonical logical form) before comparison, which is a substantially more expensive pipeline step than the holistic contradiction-checking that LLMs perform by default.

## Claim Strength Calibration

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, calibration, epistemics, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [epistemic strength calibration, assertion strength calibration, evidential claim grading]
- broader: [hedging-calibration, output-quality-in-llms, calibration-in-llms]
- related: [hedging-calibration, verbalized-uncertainty, logical-entailment-verification, nuance-preservation-in-summarization]
- prerequisites: [calibration-in-machine-learning, epistemic-logic, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Claim Strength Calibration refers to the alignment between the strength of assertion with which LLM-generated outputs state claims — ranging from speculative possibility through provisional hypothesis, supported claim, established finding, to well-verified fact — and the actual evidentiary support, replication robustness, and consensus level behind each claim. Well-calibrated claim strength means that claims stated as facts are facts, claims stated as established findings are robustly replicated, claims stated as hypotheses are genuinely provisional, and the epistemic gradient across claim strength levels accurately represents the underlying epistemic landscape of the topic domain. LLMs systematically miscalibrate claim strength by (1) asserting uncertain claims as facts when training data contains confident assertions, (2) hedging well-established findings when training data contains controversy, and (3) failing to distinguish between different types of evidential support.

**key_claim**: Claim Strength Calibration errors in LLM outputs are not random but systematically biased toward over-stating claim strength in domains with high training data frequency regardless of actual evidentiary robustness — popular science claims, widely repeated statistics, and media-covered findings are stated with higher claim strength than their evidential basis warrants, while rigorous but less-publicised findings are stated with appropriate or lower strength; this produces a systematic correlation between training corpus representation and stated certainty that is independent of actual scientific certainty, embedding popularity as a proxy for truth in LLM outputs.

**warning**: Claim Strength Calibration is not improved by simply adding instructions to "distinguish between facts and opinions" or "hedge uncertain claims" — these meta-instructions produce globally more hedged outputs without improving the correspondence between hedge level and evidentiary strength, because the model cannot reliably access claim-specific evidentiary metadata from its parametric memory; robust claim strength calibration requires retrieval augmentation that surfaces citation counts, meta-analysis summaries, or replication status for key claims, providing evidence-based claim strength information that the model can use to appropriately calibrate assertion strength.

## Nuance Preservation in Summarization

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, summarization, natural-language-generation]
- aliases: [qualification retention in summaries, conditional preservation in summarization, complexity-faithful summarization]
- broader: [output-quality-in-llms, summarization-quality, discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs]
- related: [claim-strength-calibration, hedging-calibration, specificity-vs-generality-tradeoff, abstractive-context-compression]
- prerequisites: [summarization, information-theory, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Nuance Preservation in Summarization refers to the maintenance of important qualifications, conditionals, limitations, exceptions, competing viewpoints, and epistemic hedges when condensing LLM-generated or LLM-processed source material into shorter summaries. Summarization processes — both human and machine — exhibit systematic nuance loss because nuance is informationally dense and structurally complex, making it a natural target for compression; qualifications, exceptions, and competing views add length without advancing the primary claim and are therefore pruned by naive summarization strategies. LLMs performing summarization exhibit particularly strong nuance loss because they are trained on document pairs where summaries routinely strip qualifications to achieve length targets, encoding nuance-stripping as a learned summarization behaviour.

**key_claim**: Nuance Preservation in Summarization is the primary failure mode of LLM summarisation for scientific and technical documents — studies of LLM summarisation accuracy show that the most common error type is not factual fabrication but nuance stripping, where summary statements are technically not false but remove crucial qualifications that change the practical meaning of the original claim (e.g., summarising "A is effective for condition X under protocol Y with population Z" as "A is effective for X") in ways that would mislead a reader who has not accessed the source; this makes LLM summaries systematically overconfident and over-general relative to the nuanced primary sources they condense.

**warning**: Nuance Preservation in Summarization degrades monotonically with compression ratio — the higher the target compression (from 10x to 20x to 50x), the more nuance must be sacrificed to achieve the target length, meaning that very short summaries (tweet-length, one-sentence abstracts) inevitably strip critical qualifications that longer summaries preserve; deployers should calibrate minimum summary length to the nuance density of the source material, accepting that for highly qualified technical content, lossless compression below a certain length floor is not achievable without nuance sacrifice.

## Stance Consistency Across Output

- secondary_domains: [large-language-models, natural-language-generation, discourse-analysis]
- aliases: [viewpoint consistency in LLMs, opinion stability across response sections, perspective coherence]
- broader: [discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs, output-quality-in-llms, narrative-consistency-prompting]
- related: [contradiction-detection-in-outputs, discourse-coherence-in-llm-outputs, narrative-consistency-prompting, framing-effects-on-llm-outputs]
- prerequisites: [discourse-analysis, stance-detection, large-language-models]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Stance Consistency Across Output refers to the maintenance of a consistent evaluative, argumentative, or attitudinal position throughout a multi-paragraph or multi-section LLM output — ensuring that the model does not argue for a position in one section and against the same position in another without explicit signalling that a counterargument is being presented. Stance inconsistency in LLM outputs manifests as apparently shifting views between introduction and conclusion, implicit contradictions between stated principles and applied recommendations, and position reversals between different sections of an argument that were generated under different micro-contextual conditions. LLMs are structurally vulnerable to stance inconsistency because each generation step has limited visibility into the evaluative trajectory of the entire output.

**key_claim**: Stance Consistency Across Output is particularly vulnerable to section-by-section generation patterns — when long documents are generated section-by-section with separate prompts for each section, stance consistency across sections is lower than in end-to-end generation because each section is generated without full awareness of the stances taken in preceding sections; maintaining explicit stance annotations (agreeing, disagreeing, neutral, presenting counterargument) at each section boundary and including them in subsequent section generation prompts substantially reduces stance inconsistency across sections.

**warning**: Stance Consistency Across Output should be distinguished from the legitimate rhetorical pattern of presenting multiple perspectives — some apparent stance inconsistency is intentionally structured argumentation (presenting and then refuting a counterargument, acknowledging complexity and then providing a qualified position), and aggressive stance consistency enforcement can inappropriately flatten legitimate multi-perspective analysis; distinguishing between genuine stance inconsistency (unintended contradictions) and intended multi-perspectival argumentation requires discourse-level context that simple inconsistency detection metrics cannot access.

## Register and Tone Control

**definition**: Register and Tone Control refers to the prompting strategies and fine-tuning approaches used to calibrate the social, functional, and emotional register of LLM outputs — governing the formality level (formal academic prose versus informal conversational text), the functional register (technical instruction, narrative exposition, persuasive argument, emotional support), the tonal qualities (warm versus cold, authoritative versus collaborative, confident versus tentative), and the pragmatic conventions (hedging norms, politeness strategies, directness conventions) appropriate to the target audience and communication purpose. LLMs trained on diverse text corpora have learned a wide range of registers but exhibit a strong default toward a specific register — moderately formal, mildly hedged, moderately technical — that is appropriate for general assistants but suboptimal for specialised professional, creative, or social communication contexts.

**key_claim**: Register and Tone Control is more reliably achieved through explicit role and audience specification in the system prompt than through direct stylistic instruction in the user prompt — telling the model "you are a formal academic writing assistant for a peer-reviewed journal submission" produces more consistent register than telling the model "write formally and academically", because role specification activates learned sociolinguistic conventions associated with the specified role while direct stylistic instruction requires the model to infer appropriate conventions from abstract descriptors; this suggests that register control should be managed primarily through system-prompt persona definition rather than user-turn style instructions.

**warning**: Register and Tone Control is not uniformly stable across output length — models maintain specified register reliably for short outputs but drift toward their default register for long outputs, particularly after topic transitions and section boundaries where the default register reasserts against the specified register; long-form outputs requiring strict register consistency need periodic register re-anchoring via injected instructions or context refreshes that reinstantiate the target register specification rather than assuming that a single upfront specification will maintain register consistency throughout the entire output.
