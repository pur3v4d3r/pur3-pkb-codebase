# Batch: Epistemology

## A Priori Knowledge

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, philosophy-of-mathematics]
- aliases: [a priori, non-empirical knowledge]
- broader: [theory-of-knowledge]
- related: [analytic-synthetic-distinction, rationalism, empiricism, necessary-truth]
- prerequisites: [theory-of-knowledge]
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Priori Knowledge is knowledge whose justification is independent of sense experience — typically exemplified by mathematical and logical truths and by conceptual analyses — as contrasted with a posteriori knowledge whose justification requires empirical investigation.

**key_claim**: A Priori Knowledge is not equivalent to innate knowledge or to certainty; it is a thesis about the source of justification, which is why mathematical truths discovered late in life through reasoning still count as A Priori, and why fallibilist accounts now treat much A Priori knowledge as defeasible despite its non-empirical warrant.

**warning**: Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" challenges the very analytic-synthetic distinction on which standard accounts of A Priori Knowledge depend, arguing that no statement is immune to revision in light of recalcitrant experience; the contemporary debate has not settled this challenge, so treating A Priori Knowledge as an unproblematic category papers over a live foundational dispute.

## Correspondence Theory of Truth

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [metaphysics, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [correspondence theory, truthmaker theory]
- broader: [theories-of-truth]
- related: [coherence-theory, deflationism, truthmaker-principle, realism]
- prerequisites: [theories-of-truth]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Correspondence Theory of Truth holds that a proposition is true if and only if it corresponds to a fact, state of affairs, or feature of reality — the relation of correspondence is what makes the proposition true and what its truth consists in.

**key_claim**: The Correspondence Theory of Truth is the default realist account of truth and gains support from its alignment with ordinary intuition, but its theoretical content depends entirely on what is meant by "correspondence" and "fact"; cashing out these notions either requires controversial truthmaker ontology or threatens to collapse the theory into a trivial restatement (Tarski-style disquotation).

**warning**: Critics argue that the Correspondence Theory of Truth either says too much (by committing to facts as a category of entity, with all the metaphysical baggage that follows) or too little (by reducing to schemas already accepted by deflationists and coherentists). Proponents of the Correspondence Theory of Truth therefore face a stable dilemma between substantive metaphysics and explanatory triviality.

## Epistemic Autonomy

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [social-epistemology, ethics]
- aliases: [intellectual autonomy, doxastic autonomy]
- broader: [intellectual-virtue]
- related: [testimony, principle-of-charity, epistemic-paternalism, expert-deference]
- prerequisites: [social-epistemology]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Epistemic Autonomy is the capacity and disposition to form beliefs through one's own rational evaluation of evidence rather than by uncritical deference to others, treated by some as a constitutive intellectual virtue and by others as a regulative ideal that must be balanced against epistemic dependence.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Autonomy is in genuine tension with the empirical fact of cognitive division of labor: expertise is real, life is short, and rational reliance on testimony often produces better-calibrated beliefs than personal reasoning; so Epistemic Autonomy cannot be a blanket norm but must be a context-sensitive virtue that specifies when independent evaluation is appropriate.

**warning**: Epistemic Autonomy is frequently invoked to justify rejecting expert consensus ("doing my own research"), but the autonomy literature is clear that genuine autonomy requires the meta-cognitive skills to recognize one's own limits; conflating Epistemic Autonomy with credentialed-expertise rejection produces worse beliefs and corrupts the very ideal it claims to invoke.

## Epistemic Luck

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology, ethics-of-belief]
- aliases: [veritic luck, lucky knowledge]
- broader: [theory-of-knowledge]
- related: [gettier-problem, safety-condition, sensitivity-condition, justified-true-belief]
- prerequisites: [justified-true-belief]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Epistemic Luck is the broad category of cases in which a true belief is held only because of luck, and the central anti-luck program in epistemology aims to specify the kind of luck (typically "veritic" luck, where the belief could too easily have been false) that is incompatible with knowledge.

**key_claim**: Epistemic Luck is the unifying diagnosis of a wide range of post-Gettier counterexamples: the standard "safety" condition states that a known belief could not easily have been false in nearby possible worlds, ruling out veritic Epistemic Luck while preserving the more benign forms of luck (e.g., being born into a culture with reliable testimony) that knowledge tolerates.

**warning**: Anti-luck conditions like safety and sensitivity are themselves contested: each generates its own counterexamples (sensitivity blocks knowledge of necessary truths and rules out closure-under-known-entailment; safety threatens to over-permit lucky inductive beliefs), so claims that Epistemic Luck has been theoretically tamed go beyond what the current state of the literature supports.

## Gettier Problem

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [analytic-epistemology, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [Gettier cases, Gettier counterexamples]
- broader: [theory-of-knowledge]
- related: [justified-true-belief, epistemic-luck, no-false-lemmas, safety-condition]
- prerequisites: [justified-true-belief]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Gettier Problem is the foundational challenge raised by Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper, which presented short cases of justified true belief that nevertheless intuitively fail to constitute knowledge, thereby refuting the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief and launching the contemporary post-JTB literature.

**key_claim**: The Gettier Problem's deep lesson is not merely that JTB is insufficient but that justification can be satisfied via reasoning that passes through false lemmas or coincidental connections; this is why proposed fixes — no-false-lemmas, defeasibility, sensitivity, safety, virtue conditions — all attempt to insulate knowledge from a specific structural form of luck rather than to add a single missing condition.

**warning**: A growing body of experimental epistemology suggests that Gettier intuitions vary with culture, education, and case framing, raising the methodological question of whose intuitions the Gettier Problem is a problem for; the standard interpretation of the Gettier Problem as identifying a universal feature of "knowledge" may overstate the universality of the underlying intuitions.

## Principle of Charity

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-language, argumentation]
- aliases: [principle of charitable interpretation, charitable reading]
- broader: [interpretation-norms]
- related: [steelman, hermeneutics, davidson-radical-interpretation, epistemic-autonomy]
- prerequisites: [argumentation]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: The Principle of Charity is the interpretive norm requiring that, when interpreting another's claims or arguments, one assign the strongest plausible reading consistent with the words and context — minimizing attributed error and maximizing the rationality and coherence of the interpretee's position.

**key_claim**: The Principle of Charity is justified instrumentally on epistemic grounds: charitable interpretation produces better critical engagement because attacking a weakened version of a view (a "strawman") leaves the strongest version intact, whereas refuting the steelmanned version generated by the Principle of Charity is a stronger refutation that also better serves the critic's own learning.

**warning**: The Principle of Charity has limits and can be misapplied when "charitably" reading a position requires inserting premises the author did not hold or ignoring textual evidence of the actual argument; mechanically applied, the Principle of Charity can become a license to substitute one's preferred view for the interpretee's, producing a different failure mode from strawmanning but no less serious.

## Underdetermination Thesis

- domain: epistemology
- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [underdetermination of theory by evidence, Duhem-Quine thesis]
- broader: [philosophy-of-science]
- related: [holism, theory-ladenness-of-observation, scientific-realism, ampliative-inference]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-science]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Underdetermination Thesis is the claim, associated with Pierre Duhem and W. V. O. Quine, that for any body of empirical evidence there are in principle multiple, mutually incompatible theories that are equally consistent with that evidence, so evidence alone cannot rationally compel acceptance of any particular theory.

**key_claim**: The Underdetermination Thesis matters because it forces a choice: either theory selection in science is partly governed by extra-empirical criteria (simplicity, coherence, fruitfulness, conservatism) that themselves require justification, or it is governed by considerations external to rationality narrowly construed — and either horn has serious consequences for scientific realism.

**warning**: A common overreach treats the Underdetermination Thesis as licensing relativism — "any theory is as good as any other" — but the thesis is about in-principle logical possibility; in scientific practice, genuine empirically-equivalent rivals are vanishingly rare, and most cases cited as Underdetermination are in fact resolved by additional evidence, so the thesis grounds far less skepticism than it is sometimes invoked to support.
