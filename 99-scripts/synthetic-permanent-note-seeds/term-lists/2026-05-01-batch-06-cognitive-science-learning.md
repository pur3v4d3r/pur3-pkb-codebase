# Batch: Cognitive Science and Learning Theory

## Expertise

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, professional-skill]
- aliases: [expert performance]
- broader: [knowledge-transfer]
- narrower: [expertise-development, deliberate-practice, adaptive-expertise, tacit-knowledge]
- related: [expertise-development, deliberate-practice, adaptive-expertise, automaticity, schema-construction, long-term-working-memory, cognitive-apprenticeship]
- prerequisites: [domain-specific-knowledge]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Expertise is the consistently superior performance of a domain's representative tasks by a person whose knowledge structures, perceptual chunking, and procedural routines have been shaped by extended experience and deliberate practice — operationalized in the expert-performance approach as the reproducible advantage that experts show under controlled task conditions, not merely the credentialed status of "expert."

**key_claim**: Expertise is constituted in large part by domain-specific representational restructuring — chunking, schema construction, long-term working memory — rather than by superior general intelligence, which is why expert advantage transfers poorly across domains and why estimates of expert ability outside the trained domain regularly converge on the population mean.

**warning**: Expertise is often equated with experience (years on the job), but the relationship between time-in-role and Expertise is moderated by the presence of deliberate-practice conditions — informative feedback, progressively challenging tasks, focused attention on weaknesses — and in their absence years of experience can produce arbitrary durations of unimproved performance.

## Implicit Theories

- secondary_domains: [motivational-psychology, social-cognition]
- aliases: [lay theories, mindsets]
- broader: [mental-model]
- narrower: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence, fixed-mindset, growth-mindset]
- related: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence, growth-mindset, fixed-mindset, attribution-theory, attributional-vocabulary, conception-of-ability]
- prerequisites: [implicit-theories-of-intelligence]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Implicit Theories are a person's tacit, often unarticulated beliefs about the underlying nature of an attribute, capacity, or domain — whether it is fixed and trait-like or malleable and developable — beliefs that operate without explicit endorsement but that systematically organize attention, attribution, effort, and self-regulation in attribute-relevant situations.

**key_claim**: Implicit Theories function as motivational filters: the same situation triggers different appraisals, attributions, and coping responses depending on whether the relevant attribute is held to be fixed or malleable, and the downstream effect on behavior is mediated almost entirely through the appraisal-and-attribution pathway rather than through the implicit theory itself.

**warning**: Implicit Theories research, particularly the fixed-versus-growth-mindset literature, has been the site of well-documented replication concerns and effect-size shrinkage; the cleanest current reading is that Implicit Theories do moderate motivational outcomes but with effect sizes substantially smaller than the popular literature claimed, and that mindset interventions work primarily for narrowly bounded high-risk populations rather than universally.

## Cognitive Development

- secondary_domains: [developmental-psychology, education]
- aliases: [development of cognition]
- broader: [developmental-psychology]
- narrower: [sensorimotor-stage, formal-operational-stage, object-permanence, theory-of-mind]
- related: [developmental-psychology, genetic-epistemology, zone-of-proximal-development, theory-of-mind, executive-function, working-memory]
- prerequisites: [developmental-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Development is the lifespan study of how thinking, reasoning, problem-solving, attention, memory, language, and social cognition emerge, transform, and reorganize from infancy through adulthood — encompassing Piaget's stage theory, Vygotsky's social-developmental account, information-processing approaches, and the contemporary neuroconstructivist synthesis that integrates brain maturation with experience-driven specialization.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Development since Piaget has progressively moved from strict universal-stage models toward domain-specific and gradualist accounts: children show competence earlier than Piaget's tasks suggested when the tasks are simplified, but the timing and content of acquisitions remain regular enough that "free variation across all ages" is decisively ruled out — leaving a moderately structured developmental trajectory as the empirically-supported middle position.

**warning**: Cognitive Development findings are routinely mapped to age-graded educational prescriptions, but the within-age variability across children typically exceeds the between-age mean differences for most cognitive milestones; treating an average age-of-onset as a normative readiness threshold for an individual child systematically misallocates instruction.

## Behavior Change Theory

- secondary_domains: [health-psychology, behavioral-economics]
- aliases: [behavior change frameworks]
- broader: [motivational-psychology]
- narrower: [theory-of-planned-behavior, transtheoretical-model, stages-of-change-model, habit-formation]
- related: [theory-of-planned-behavior, transtheoretical-model, stages-of-change-model, behavior-change-techniques, habit-formation, implementation-intention, motivational-interviewing, nudge-theory]
- prerequisites: [motivational-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Behavior Change Theory is the umbrella term for the family of theoretical frameworks — including the Theory of Planned Behavior, the Transtheoretical Model, Health Belief Model, Social Cognitive Theory, Self-Determination Theory, and the Behavior Change Wheel — that specify the determinants, mechanisms, and intervention targets through which sustained behavior change is hypothesized to occur.

**key_claim**: Behavior Change Theory has converged on the conclusion that no single theoretical model dominates across behaviors and contexts: which mechanisms are most active depends on the behavior's complexity, the population's baseline motivation, and the structural affordances of the change environment, so multi-theory or behavior-specific tailoring outperforms blanket application of any single model.

**warning**: Behavior Change Theory is sometimes used to justify intervention designs that are merely consistent with a theory rather than implementing its mechanisms; the audit question is whether the intervention measurably moves the theory's specified mediators, and most published "theory-based" interventions do not in fact demonstrate movement on the mediators their cited theory specifies.

## Single-Loop Learning

- secondary_domains: [organizational-learning, systems-thinking]
- aliases: [first-order learning]
- broader: [feedback-loops]
- related: [double-loop-learning, feedback-loops, organizational-psychology, mental-model, schema-theory, reflective-thinking]
- prerequisites: [feedback-loops]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Single-Loop Learning, in Argyris and Schon's organizational-learning framework, is the form of learning in which an actor detects an error or gap and corrects it by adjusting actions within the existing governing variables — the goals, values, and frames remain unquestioned and only the action repertoire is updated, contrasted with double-loop learning that revises the governing variables themselves.

**key_claim**: Single-Loop Learning is the modal mode of error correction in individuals and organizations because it operates within the existing identity-and-value structure and therefore does not threaten the actor's commitments — it accomplishes incremental improvement at low psychological and political cost but is structurally incapable of fixing problems whose source lies in the governing variables themselves.

**warning**: Single-Loop Learning is often dismissed as inferior to double-loop learning, but the framework is descriptive rather than evaluative: Single-Loop Learning is the appropriate mode when the governing variables are themselves sound, and forcing double-loop learning where single-loop suffices wastes organizational resources and destabilizes commitments that did not need revision.

## Transformational Creativity

- secondary_domains: [creativity-research, cognitive-science]
- aliases: [transformational-creativity in Boden's typology]
- broader: [creative-problem-solving]
- related: [combinatorial-creativity, divergent-thinking, conceptual-blending, conceptual-change, insight-problem-solving, constraint-relaxation]
- prerequisites: [creative-problem-solving]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Transformational Creativity is the most radical of Margaret Boden's three creativity types — combinatorial, exploratory, and transformational — in which the conceptual space within which a problem or domain is defined is itself altered, allowing solutions or works that could not have been generated under the prior space's constraints, contrasted with mere recombination or with thorough exploration of an unchanged space.

**key_claim**: Transformational Creativity is rare and structurally distinct from combinatorial creativity because it requires not only generating new candidates but also relaxing or replacing the constraints that defined what counted as a candidate — a meta-level operation on the conceptual system, which is why transformational episodes typically reorganize the field they emerge into rather than merely contributing additional items to it.

**warning**: Transformational Creativity is often invoked as an honorific for any sufficiently impressive creative work, but Boden's diagnostic criterion is structural: the work must be impossible under the prior conceptual constraints in a sense that can be specified; without that demonstration, "transformational" reduces to "very good," and Boden's tripartite distinction collapses into a single laudatory label.

## Transfer Adequate Processing

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, instructional-design]
- aliases: [transfer-appropriate processing variant]
- broader: [transfer-of-learning]
- related: [transfer-appropriate-processing, transfer-of-learning, encoding-specificity-principle, levels-of-processing, encoding-variability, near-transfer, far-transfer]
- prerequisites: [transfer-appropriate-processing]
- confidence: medium

**definition**: Transfer Adequate Processing is the principle, within the broader transfer-appropriate-processing family, that retention is maximized when the cognitive operations engaged at study match those required at the criterion task — generalizing the encoding-specificity principle from cue overlap to processing overlap and explaining why depth of processing improves recall when the test rewards conceptual processing but not when it rewards perceptual processing.

**key_claim**: Transfer Adequate Processing reframes the levels-of-processing literature as a study-test-interaction phenomenon rather than a property of encoding alone: there is no universally "deeper" or "better" form of processing because the criterion of better is the match between study and test, which is why instructional design must reason backward from assessment in order to identify which study processing will transfer.

**warning**: Transfer Adequate Processing is sometimes presented as a refutation of levels-of-processing, but the relationship is more nuanced: deeper conceptual processing remains advantageous on average because most useful criterion tasks are themselves conceptual, so the older levels-of-processing finding is recovered as a special case rather than overturned in domains where conceptual transfer is the goal.

## Schema Induction

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [analogical schema induction]
- broader: [schema-construction]
- related: [schema-formation, schema-construction, analogical-reasoning, structure-mapping-theory, worked-example-variability, prototype-theory-of-concepts, generalization]
- prerequisites: [analogical-reasoning]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Schema Induction is the cognitive process by which a learner extracts a generalizable structural pattern from multiple specific instances — typically through analogical comparison across two or more cases — yielding an abstract schema that supports recognition and solution of new instances of the same category, formalized in Gick and Holyoak's classic work on analogical problem solving.

**key_claim**: Schema Induction is reliably triggered by the explicit comparison of two or more analogous cases at training time but only weakly by sequential study of the same cases without comparison, which establishes that the comparison operation rather than the cumulative exposure is the active ingredient — a finding with direct implications for the design of worked-example sequences and case-based instruction.

**warning**: Schema Induction is sometimes conflated with the resulting schema itself, but the operative distinction is between the inductive process (which can fail or succeed) and its product (the schema that may or may not be well-calibrated); a learner can possess a schema acquired by direct instruction without having undergone induction, and the two routes have different transfer properties despite the surface similarity of the end state.

## Schema Formation

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [schema acquisition]
- broader: [schema-construction]
- related: [schema-induction, schema-construction, schema-theory, knowledge-schemas, declarative-schemas, procedural-schemas, knowledge-compilation]
- prerequisites: [schema-construction]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Schema Formation is the broader cognitive process by which schemas — organized knowledge structures that represent regularities of objects, events, and actions in a domain — come into being in a learner's long-term memory, encompassing both inductive routes (extraction from instances) and direct instructional routes (acquisition from explicit explanation followed by exemplification).

**key_claim**: Schema Formation is the central mechanism by which Cognitive Load Theory explains expertise development: as schemas form, the multiple interacting elements of a problem collapse into single chunks for working-memory purposes, freeing capacity for higher-order operations and transforming what was previously element-interactivity-bound into a pattern-recognition-bound task.

**warning**: Schema Formation is sometimes treated as an automatic by-product of practice, but the empirical literature consistently shows that schema-quality depends on the structural alignment of the practice instances; un-curated practice on superficially varied but structurally identical examples can produce surface-level fluency without the deep schema needed for transfer, an outcome instructional design must explicitly engineer against.

## Statistical Learning

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, developmental-psychology]
- aliases: [statistical pattern learning]
- broader: [implicit-learning]
- related: [implicit-learning, language-acquisition, predictive-coding, bayesian-brain, classical-conditioning, prototype-theory-of-concepts]
- prerequisites: [language-acquisition]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Statistical Learning is the cognitive capacity to extract regularities — transitional probabilities, co-occurrence frequencies, distributional patterns — from streams of input without explicit instruction or intent to learn, demonstrated by Saffran and colleagues for infant word segmentation and subsequently shown to operate across visual, auditory, and motor modalities throughout the lifespan.

**key_claim**: Statistical Learning provides the most plausible single mechanism for the rapid acquisition of structured knowledge in early development, particularly language: the kinds of statistical regularities infants are demonstrably sensitive to are sufficient to support segmentation, categorization, and grammatical bootstrapping, which substantially reduces the explanatory burden previously assigned to dedicated nativist machinery.

**warning**: Statistical Learning is sometimes invoked to argue that all of language acquisition reduces to general-purpose statistics, but the developmental literature shows that statistical learning interacts with attention, social cues, and constraints on hypothesis space in ways that go beyond pure distributional analysis; the strong reductionist claim outruns the data and invites the empiricist-nativist debate to be re-litigated as a confused dichotomy.
