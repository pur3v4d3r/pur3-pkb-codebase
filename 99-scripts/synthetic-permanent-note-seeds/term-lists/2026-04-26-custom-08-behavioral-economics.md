# Batch: Behavioral Economics

## Affect Heuristic

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [decision-science, emotion]
- aliases: [affective heuristic, Slovic affect heuristic]
- broader: [heuristics-and-biases]
- related: [availability-heuristic, dual-process-theory, risk-perception, somatic-marker-hypothesis]
- prerequisites: [judgment-under-uncertainty]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Affect Heuristic is a mental shortcut, characterized by Paul Slovic and colleagues, in which people consult an immediate affective evaluation — a "good" or "bad" feeling about an object — and use it as the basis for judgments about that object's risks, benefits, and probabilities.

**key_claim**: The Affect Heuristic produces a robust inverse correlation between perceived risk and perceived benefit for the same object, even though risks and benefits are objectively positively correlated in the world; this anti-correlation is a signature finding because it cannot be generated by any rational model that treats affect as separate from probability assessment.

**warning**: The Affect Heuristic is sometimes treated as automatically irrational, but affect frequently encodes genuine experiential information; the bias appears not in using affect at all but in treating affect as a sufficient summary when its evidentiary basis is thin or when the decision domain is sufficiently novel that affective tags lack diagnostic value.

## Decision Fatigue

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [self-regulation, judgment]
- aliases: [decision depletion, choice fatigue]
- broader: [self-regulation]
- related: [ego-depletion, default-bias, choice-overload, willpower]
- prerequisites: [self-regulation]
- confidence: low

**definition**: Decision Fatigue is the proposed phenomenon that the quality of decisions deteriorates after a long sequence of choices, manifesting as increased reliance on defaults, greater impulsivity, and avoidance of further deliberate choice, and theoretically grounded in a limited-resource model of executive control.

**key_claim**: Decision Fatigue gained prominence through observational findings such as parole judges granting fewer favorable rulings later in sessions, with the interpretation that depleted decision-makers default to the status-quo option (denial), making sequence position a non-trivial determinant of life-altering decisions.

**warning**: Decision Fatigue and the closely related ego-depletion construct have weathered serious replication failures and methodological critiques; the parole-judge result has been challenged on grounds of case-ordering confounds, and large-scale registered replications of ego depletion have produced near-zero effects, so confident clinical or policy recommendations grounded in Decision Fatigue are currently unwarranted.

## Endowment Effect

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [judgment, market-behavior]
- aliases: [divestiture aversion, ownership effect]
- broader: [loss-aversion]
- related: [loss-aversion, status-quo-bias, prospect-theory, willingness-to-accept-pay-gap]
- prerequisites: [prospect-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Endowment Effect is the empirical regularity, identified by Thaler and demonstrated in Kahneman-Knetsch-Thaler mug experiments, that individuals demand substantially more compensation to give up an object they own than they would pay to acquire the identical object, opening a gap between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay.

**key_claim**: The Endowment Effect is most parsimoniously explained by reference-dependent loss aversion: ownership shifts the reference point so that giving up the object is coded as a loss rather than as forgoing a gain, and losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains in prospect-theory utility.

**warning**: The Endowment Effect attenuates or disappears among experienced traders, in repeated market settings, and across some cross-cultural samples; treating it as a universal psychological constant misrepresents the literature and obscures the conditions — novelty, market inexperience, ambiguous valuation — under which the Endowment Effect actually generates the WTA-WTP gap.

## Expected Utility Theory

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [decision-theory, normative-economics]
- aliases: [EUT, von Neumann-Morgenstern utility]
- broader: [decision-theory]
- related: [prospect-theory, allais-paradox, risk-aversion, axioms-of-rationality]
- prerequisites: [probability-theory, preference-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Expected Utility Theory is the normative decision framework axiomatized by von Neumann and Morgenstern in which a rational agent facing risky prospects evaluates each option by summing the utilities of its possible outcomes weighted by their objective probabilities, and chooses the option with the highest expected utility.

**key_claim**: Expected Utility Theory's normative force derives from its representation theorem: any agent whose preferences satisfy completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence behaves as if maximizing expected utility, so violations of EUT are not merely empirical anomalies but indictments of one of these axioms — most often independence, as in the Allais paradox.

**warning**: Treating Expected Utility Theory as a descriptive theory of human choice has been decisively refuted by demonstrations like the Allais and Ellsberg paradoxes and by the emergence of prospect theory; conversely, treating EUT as the unique normative standard ignores live debates about the status of the independence axiom, ambiguity, and state-dependent preferences, where the normative case for EUT is genuinely contested.

## Intertemporal Choice

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [decision-science, self-regulation]
- aliases: [temporal discounting, delay discounting]
- broader: [decision-theory]
- related: [hyperbolic-discounting, present-bias, time-preference, commitment-devices]
- prerequisites: [decision-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Intertemporal Choice is the study of how decision-makers trade off costs and benefits that occur at different points in time, encompassing both the descriptive question of how people actually discount the future and the normative question of how they ought to.

**key_claim**: Intertemporal Choice research consistently finds that discounting is hyperbolic rather than exponential, meaning the ratio of preferences between two delayed rewards depends on whether both are evaluated from a near or distant present — generating predictable preference reversals as the earlier option approaches and yielding the formal signature of present-biased self-control failure.

**warning**: The standard Intertemporal Choice paradigm of "smaller-sooner vs. larger-later" monetary rewards has weak external validity for many real-world decisions; people who choose patiently in laboratory tasks often fail at consumption decisions, and lab discount rates correlate inconsistently with field outcomes, so Intertemporal Choice findings should be generalized to behavior with care.

## Mental Accounting

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [consumer-behavior, judgment]
- aliases: [Thaler mental accounting]
- broader: [behavioral-finance]
- related: [endowment-effect, sunk-cost-fallacy, framing-effect, fungibility]
- prerequisites: [prospect-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Mental Accounting, developed by Richard Thaler, is the cognitive process by which individuals categorize, label, and evaluate financial activities into separate notional accounts (e.g., "vacation money," "rent money," "windfall"), violating the economic principle that money is fungible.

**key_claim**: Mental Accounting predicts systematic deviations from rational consumption — people will simultaneously hold high-interest debt and low-yielding savings, treat a tax refund differently from a wage increase of equal size, and react to identical losses differently depending on which mental account absorbs them — because the accounts impose locally enforced budget constraints.

**warning**: Mental Accounting is not uniformly suboptimal; the same categorical structure that produces inefficiency can also serve as a self-control technology, helping agents protect long-term goals from short-term temptation. Treating Mental Accounting as a pure error reservoir misses that some "irrational" partitioning is precisely what makes household budgeting tractable for cognitively bounded agents.

## Peak End Rule

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [memory, hedonic-psychology]
- aliases: [peak-end heuristic, Kahneman peak-end]
- broader: [retrospective-evaluation]
- related: [duration-neglect, affective-forecasting, hedonic-adaptation, experiencing-vs-remembering-self]
- prerequisites: [hedonic-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Peak End Rule is the empirical finding, established by Kahneman, Fredrickson, and colleagues, that retrospective evaluations of an extended hedonic experience are well predicted by the average of its most intense moment and its final moment, with surprisingly little weight given to the experience's overall duration.

**key_claim**: The Peak End Rule produces the counterintuitive prediction — confirmed in the cold-pressor and colonoscopy studies — that adding a period of mild discomfort to the end of an unpleasant experience can improve its remembered evaluation, because the milder ending lowers the "end" anchor while the peak is unchanged and duration is largely ignored.

**warning**: The Peak End Rule applies to retrospective summaries used in future decisions, not to the moment-by-moment experience itself; designing interventions around the Peak End Rule risks improving memories at the cost of total experienced wellbeing, raising a serious normative question about whether the experiencing self or the remembering self should govern the design of services and treatments.

## Scope Insensitivity

- domain: behavioral-economics
- secondary_domains: [valuation, environmental-economics]
- aliases: [scope neglect, embedding effect]
- broader: [judgment-bias]
- related: [contingent-valuation, identifiable-victim-effect, affect-heuristic, willingness-to-pay]
- prerequisites: [valuation]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Scope Insensitivity is the finding from contingent-valuation studies that stated willingness to pay for a public good is largely insensitive to the magnitude of the good provided — for example, comparable amounts are offered to save 2,000, 20,000, or 200,000 birds — undermining the assumption that elicited valuations track underlying preferences over quantity.

**key_claim**: Scope Insensitivity is best understood as a consequence of valuation-by-prototype: respondents form an affective image of the prototypical scenario (a single oiled bird) and price that image, so quantitative scope information never enters the valuation, which is why Scope Insensitivity persists even when respondents are explicitly given the larger numbers.

**warning**: Scope Insensitivity poses a foundational challenge to using contingent-valuation surveys for cost-benefit analysis of public goods; ignoring the bias and aggregating stated WTP across populations produces estimates that respond to question framing rather than to the policy magnitudes they purport to measure, so policy applications must explicitly address Scope Insensitivity rather than assume it away.
