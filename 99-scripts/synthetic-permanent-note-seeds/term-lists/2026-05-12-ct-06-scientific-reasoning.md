## Falsifiability

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, methodology]
- aliases: [refutability, Popperian falsifiability]
- broader: [demarcation criteria]
- related: [hypothetico deductive method, demarcation problem, pseudo science]

**definition**: Falsifiability is the property of a hypothesis or theory of being open in principle to refutation by some possible observation; a falsifiable claim specifies what would count against it as well as what would count for it.

**key_claim**: Falsifiability marks the asymmetry between confirmation and refutation that Popper placed at the centre of scientific method: a theory cannot be conclusively verified by any finite set of confirmations, but it can be conclusively refuted by a single well-established counterexample.

**warning**: Falsifiability is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for scientific status; many obviously unscientific claims can be made falsifiable by stipulation, and many genuinely scientific theories require auxiliary hypotheses before any specific observation counts as a refutation.

## Hypothetico Deductive Method

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, methodology]
- aliases: [hypothetico-deductive method, H-D method]
- broader: [scientific methodology]
- related: [falsifiability, null hypothesis, inductive confirmation]

**definition**: The Hypothetico Deductive Method is the procedure by which a scientific hypothesis is tested by deducing observational predictions from it (often together with auxiliary assumptions) and comparing those predictions against empirical results.

**key_claim**: The Hypothetico Deductive Method is the standard reconstruction of how testing actually works in science; it captures the essential point that confirmation and disconfirmation operate through entailed consequences rather than through direct comparison of theory to nature.

**warning**: The Hypothetico Deductive Method is famously vulnerable to the Duhem-Quine problem: predictions are derived from the hypothesis only in conjunction with auxiliary assumptions, so failure of a prediction does not unambiguously locate the source of error within the hypothesis under test.

## Demarcation Problem

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, history-of-science]
- aliases: [problem of demarcation]
- broader: [meta-science]
- related: [falsifiability, pseudo science]

**definition**: The Demarcation Problem is the question of how (or whether) to draw a principled distinction between science and non-science, particularly between science and pseudo-science.

**key_claim**: The Demarcation Problem has resisted any single criterion solution; falsifiability, predictive success, methodological rigour, and explanatory virtue have each been proposed and shown to admit counterexamples, suggesting that the boundary may be a cluster rather than a single line.

**warning**: The Demarcation Problem is sometimes declared "solved" by adopting a favoured criterion and treating its counterexamples as outliers; serious engagement with the problem requires acknowledging that any working criterion will excluded some genuine science or admit some genuine pseudo-science at the margins.

## Paradigm Shift

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, history-of-science, sociology-of-science]
- aliases: [Kuhnian paradigm shift, scientific revolution]
- broader: [history of scientific change]
- related: [theoretical virtues, demarcation problem]

**definition**: A Paradigm Shift, in Kuhn's analysis, is the discontinuous transition between successive frameworks of normal science in which the basic concepts, methods, exemplary problems, and standards of explanation are restructured rather than incrementally revised.

**key_claim**: A Paradigm Shift is not the gradual accumulation of new results but a structural reorganisation in which old data is reinterpreted under new categories; this is what makes successive paradigms partially incommensurable rather than simply better-versus-worse on a fixed scale.

**warning**: A Paradigm Shift is widely overused as a marketing term for any modest novelty; the original Kuhnian sense requires deep, framework-level change, and applying it to every new product or method drains the concept of its analytical content.

## Pseudo Science

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, science-communication]
- aliases: [pseudoscience]
- broader: [non-science]
- related: [demarcation problem, falsifiability]

**definition**: Pseudo Science is a body of claims, practices, or doctrines that presents itself as scientific — borrowing the vocabulary, aesthetic, or institutional trappings of science — while failing to satisfy the methodological standards (testability, openness to revision, evidential discipline) that distinguish science from other forms of inquiry.

**key_claim**: Pseudo Science is identified by a combination of features rather than by any single defect; recurring markers include immunity to disconfirming evidence, ad hoc rescues, reliance on anecdote over systematic data, and pattern-matching presentation that performs scientific style without scientific substance.

**warning**: Pseudo Science is not the same as bad science or fringe science; legitimate scientific work can be wrong, methodologically poor, or marginal without being pseudoscientific, and the label should be reserved for cases where the failure is structural rather than incidental.

## Null Hypothesis

- secondary_domains: [statistics, scientific-methodology]
- aliases: [H0, null hypothesis significance testing]
- broader: [hypothesis testing]
- related: [hypothetico deductive method, replication and reproducibility]

**definition**: The Null Hypothesis is the default statistical claim — typically that there is no effect, no difference, or no association — against which observed data are tested in classical significance testing; the test asks how probable the data would be if the null were true.

**key_claim**: The Null Hypothesis serves as a procedural foil rather than a substantive scientific claim; rejecting it does not establish any specific alternative, only that the null is unlikely to have generated the data, which is a much weaker conclusion than non-statisticians often assume.

**warning**: The Null Hypothesis test is widely misinterpreted as giving the probability that the null is true given the data, when it actually gives the probability of the data given the null; this reversal underlies many of the well-documented misuses of p-values across the sciences.

## Inductive Confirmation

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, formal-epistemology]
- aliases: [inductive support, confirmation theory]
- broader: [theories of evidence]
- related: [strong argument, cogent argument, hypothetico deductive method]

**definition**: Inductive Confirmation is the relation by which evidence raises the probability or credibility of a hypothesis without strictly entailing it; the confirmed hypothesis remains defeasible in light of further evidence.

**key_claim**: Inductive Confirmation is gradient and comparative rather than binary; the question is not whether evidence confirms a hypothesis simpliciter but how much, relative to which alternatives, and in light of what background assumptions.

**warning**: Inductive Confirmation faces classical paradoxes (Hempel's ravens, Goodman's grue) that show naive accounts of confirmation generate counterintuitive results; using "confirmed by evidence" as a casual stamp of approval ignores the genuine technical difficulty of formalising what confirmation actually is.

## Operationalization

- secondary_domains: [methodology, social-science, measurement-theory]
- aliases: [operationalisation, operational definition]
- broader: [scientific methodology]
- related: [null hypothesis, replication and reproducibility]

**definition**: Operationalization is the process of translating a theoretical construct (e.g., "depression," "trust," "intelligence") into a concrete, measurable procedure that produces values usable in empirical research.

**key_claim**: Operationalization is what makes a theoretical construct empirically tractable, but the choice of operationalisation is theoretically loaded: different operationalisations of the "same" construct can yield substantively different results, which is why measurement decisions are not pre-theoretical.

**warning**: Operationalization invites the slip from "we measure X with this procedure" to "X just is whatever this procedure measures"; the latter (operationalism in the strict sense) discards the gap between construct and measure that empirical research depends on for revision and improvement.

## Replication And Reproducibility

- secondary_domains: [methodology, meta-science, statistics]
- aliases: [reproducibility, replicability]
- broader: [scientific methodology]
- related: [null hypothesis, operationalization, texas sharpshooter fallacy]

**definition**: Replication And Reproducibility refers to the dual norms that scientific findings should be obtainable again by other researchers — replication producing the same result with new data, reproducibility producing the same result from the original data and methods.

**key_claim**: Replication And Reproducibility provide the structural check on individual studies; a single result, however striking, only earns its place in the body of accepted knowledge through independent successful repetition, because chance and undetected error cannot be ruled out from any single trial.

**warning**: Replication And Reproducibility have themselves come under scrutiny by the replication crisis across multiple sciences; large fractions of high-profile findings have failed to replicate, which means existing literature should be treated as provisional rather than as a stable foundation.

## Theoretical Virtues

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, theory-evaluation]
- aliases: [virtues of theories, super-empirical virtues]
- broader: [theory evaluation]
- related: [paradigm shift, demarcation problem, inductive confirmation]

**definition**: Theoretical Virtues are the non-evidential criteria — simplicity, scope, fruitfulness, internal consistency, integration with other theories, explanatory depth — that scientists use to compare and choose between theories that are roughly equally supported by the available data.

**key_claim**: Theoretical Virtues do real work in scientific practice precisely because evidence typically underdetermines theory choice; multiple theories can fit the same data, and the virtues are how the scientific community discriminates among them when evidence alone cannot.

**warning**: Theoretical Virtues are not universally weighted; different sciences and different historical periods rank simplicity, scope, and fruitfulness differently, so appealing to "the virtues" without specifying the weighting is rhetorically powerful but analytically empty.
