## Belief Bias

- secondary_domains: [reasoning-research, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [conclusion bias]
- broader: [reasoning biases, motivated reasoning]
- related: [myside bias, intellectual integrity]

**definition**: Belief Bias is the tendency to evaluate the logical strength of an argument on the basis of whether one finds its conclusion plausible, rather than on the basis of whether the inference from premises to conclusion is actually valid.

**key_claim**: Belief Bias shows that everyday reasoners do not cleanly separate validity assessment from belief assessment; conclusions that match prior beliefs are accepted as well-argued, while conclusions that conflict are scrutinised for inferential flaws regardless of actual structure.

**warning**: Belief Bias is not eliminated by knowing about it; the effect persists in trained reasoners and intensifies under cognitive load, so the appropriate response is procedural (e.g., evaluating arguments with conclusions hidden) rather than relying on willpower.

## Myside Bias

- secondary_domains: [reasoning-research, political-psychology]
- aliases: [my-side bias, partisan bias in reasoning]
- broader: [reasoning biases, motivated reasoning]
- related: [belief bias, fair mindedness, intellectual empathy]

**definition**: Myside Bias is the tendency to generate, evaluate, and remember evidence in ways that favour one's prior opinions, group commitments, or personal stake in the outcome of an argument.

**key_claim**: Myside Bias is dissociable from general cognitive ability; high-IQ reasoners exhibit it at roughly the same rate as average reasoners, which means that intelligence alone does not protect against it and may actually equip it with more sophisticated rationalisations.

**warning**: Myside Bias is often misidentified as the property of opponents only; the most reliable indicator that one is exhibiting myside bias is feeling certain that the bias is concentrated in the people who disagree with one.

## Naive Realism

- secondary_domains: [social-psychology, philosophy-of-perception]
- aliases: [naïve realism (psychological sense)]
- broader: [reasoning biases, perspective-taking failures]
- related: [intellectual empathy, curse of knowledge, illusion of transparency]

**definition**: Naive Realism (in the psychological sense) is the tendency to believe that one perceives the world objectively, that those who agree share that objective perception, and that those who disagree must be biased, ill-informed, or irrational.

**key_claim**: Naive Realism produces a recursive failure of perspective-taking: disagreement is automatically explained by the other party's deviation from "what is obvious," which forecloses the possibility that one's own view is itself shaped by perspective.

**warning**: Naive Realism (psychological) should not be conflated with naive realism in philosophy of perception, which is a substantive theory about direct perception of mind-independent objects; the psychological term names a bias, not a metaphysical position.

## Curse Of Knowledge

- secondary_domains: [communication, cognitive-psychology, expertise-research]
- aliases: [knowledge curse, expert blindness]
- broader: [reasoning biases, perspective-taking failures]
- related: [illusion of transparency, intellectual empathy]

**definition**: The Curse Of Knowledge is the cognitive bias whereby people who know something find it difficult to imagine, predict, or model the perspective of someone who does not know it.

**key_claim**: The Curse Of Knowledge explains a large class of communication failures — expert opacity, instructional miscalibration, projection of jargon — by locating the source not in malice or laziness but in an automatic and largely unconscious assimilation of others' minds to one's own.

**warning**: The Curse Of Knowledge is not corrected by being told to "remember what it was like not to know"; effective debiasing typically requires structural aids such as testing on naive audiences, since introspective simulation of ignorance is precisely what the bias prevents.

## Illusion Of Transparency

- secondary_domains: [social-psychology, communication]
- aliases: [transparency illusion]
- broader: [reasoning biases, perspective-taking failures]
- related: [curse of knowledge, naive realism]

**definition**: The Illusion Of Transparency is the tendency to overestimate the extent to which one's internal states — emotions, intentions, knowledge — are evident to others through outward behaviour.

**key_claim**: The Illusion Of Transparency is the inverse of the curse of knowledge applied to the self: just as we project our knowledge onto others' minds, we project the salience of our own internal states onto their perception of us, both of which produce systematic miscommunication.

**warning**: The Illusion Of Transparency makes both anxiety and clarity self-defeating; speakers under-explain because they overestimate how clear they are, and the same speakers feel exposed because they overestimate how obvious their inner state is.

## Optimism Bias

- secondary_domains: [decision-making, behavioural-economics, planning]
- aliases: [unrealistic optimism, valence effect]
- broader: [reasoning biases, prediction biases]
- related: [impact bias, neglect of probability, restraint bias]

**definition**: Optimism Bias is the tendency to overestimate the probability of positive future events and to underestimate the probability of negative future events, particularly as they apply to oneself relative to others.

**key_claim**: Optimism Bias is robust across cultures and domains, persisting even when people are given accurate base-rate information; it appears to function as a default setting rather than as a reasoned conclusion that better information could simply correct.

**warning**: Optimism Bias has documented benefits for motivation and resilience, so the corrective is not pessimism but calibration; the goal is to bring probability judgements closer to actual base rates, not to substitute one directional bias for its opposite.

## Third Person Effect

- secondary_domains: [media-studies, social-psychology]
- aliases: [third-person effect, third-person perception]
- broader: [reasoning biases, perspective-taking failures]
- related: [naive realism, myside bias]

**definition**: The Third Person Effect is the tendency for people to believe that media messages — advertising, propaganda, persuasion — affect others more strongly than they affect themselves.

**key_claim**: The Third Person Effect produces a systematically distorted policy intuition: because people see themselves as relatively immune to influence, they support restrictions on media on others' behalf while denying that the same media is shaping their own beliefs.

**warning**: The Third Person Effect is not invariant; it weakens or reverses for content one approves of (a "first-person effect"), so the bias is not about media-influence in general but about influence by content one regards as bad or low-status.

## Restraint Bias

- secondary_domains: [self-control-research, behavioural-economics]
- aliases: [self-control bias]
- broader: [reasoning biases, prediction biases]
- related: [optimism bias, impact bias]

**definition**: Restraint Bias is the tendency to overestimate one's ability to control impulsive urges in the future, leading to under-preparation for situations in which those urges will arise and over-exposure to tempting environments.

**key_claim**: Restraint Bias predicts that the people most confident in their self-control are systematically the most likely to fail at it, because that confidence licences the exposures that subsequently overwhelm whatever actual self-control they possess.

**warning**: Restraint Bias is sometimes dismissed as a minor self-knowledge failure, but it has documented effects on addiction relapse, dietary outcomes, and financial behaviour, where the cost of overestimating future restraint is concrete and recurrent.

## Impact Bias

- secondary_domains: [affective-forecasting, decision-making]
- aliases: [affective forecasting error, durability bias]
- broader: [reasoning biases, prediction biases]
- related: [optimism bias, restraint bias, neglect of probability]

**definition**: Impact Bias is the tendency to overestimate the intensity and duration of one's emotional reactions to future events, both positive and negative; people predict that good outcomes will make them happier for longer and bad outcomes worse for longer than actually occurs.

**key_claim**: Impact Bias arises because affective forecasting under-weights psychological adaptation and contextual dilution; predictions focus on the focal event in isolation, while actual experience embeds it among countless other inputs that moderate its felt impact.

**warning**: Impact Bias does not entail that future events are unimportant or that emotional predictions should be discounted entirely; the bias is specifically about magnitude and duration, and over-correcting toward "nothing will matter as much as I think" is itself a forecasting error.

## Neglect Of Probability

- secondary_domains: [decision-making, behavioural-economics, risk-perception]
- aliases: [probability neglect]
- broader: [reasoning biases, statistical reasoning failures]
- related: [hasty generalization, false cause fallacy, optimism bias]

**definition**: Neglect Of Probability is the tendency, when evaluating risky outcomes, to focus on the salience or affective vividness of an outcome while largely ignoring its actual probability.

**key_claim**: Neglect Of Probability explains why public risk perception is poorly correlated with statistical risk; vivid, emotionally charged outcomes (terrorism, plane crashes, child abductions) command attention disproportionate to their base rates, while abstract high-probability risks recede.

**warning**: Neglect Of Probability is not uniformly irrational; for outcomes of catastrophic, irreversible severity, ignoring probability differences below a threshold can be a defensible decision rule, so the bias label should be applied to cases where probability genuinely should have driven the decision.
