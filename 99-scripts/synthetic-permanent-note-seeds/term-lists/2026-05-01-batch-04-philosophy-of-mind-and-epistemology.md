# Batch: Philosophy of Mind and Epistemology Anchors

## Philosophy of Mind

- secondary_domains: [cognitive-science, metaphysics]
- aliases: [philosophy of psychology]
- broader: [philosophy]
- narrower: [physicalism, eliminative-materialism, functionalism, mind-body-problem]
- related: [consciousness-theories, qualia, intentionality, mental-causation, computational-theory-of-mind, extended-mind-thesis, embodied-cognition]
- prerequisites: [philosophy]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Philosophy of Mind is the philosophical investigation of the nature of mental phenomena and their relationship to the physical world — covering the mind-body problem, the metaphysics of consciousness, intentionality, mental causation, and the ontological status of psychological kinds — and the discipline that frames the conceptual problems empirical cognitive science attempts to operationalize.

**key_claim**: Philosophy of Mind in its modern phase has been organized around a small number of cross-cutting positions on the mind-body problem (dualism, identity theory, functionalism, eliminativism, property dualism, panpsychism), and the empirical sciences of mind inherit substantive philosophical commitments from whichever position frames their explanatory ambitions, even when those commitments are not made explicit.

**warning**: Philosophy of Mind is sometimes dismissed by working scientists as armchair speculation, but every empirical claim about cognition presupposes answers to philosophical questions about what counts as a mental state and what would count as explaining one; the question is not whether to do Philosophy of Mind but whether to do it deliberately or inherit it tacitly.

## Physicalism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, metaphysics]
- aliases: [materialism, ontological physicalism]
- broader: [philosophy-of-mind]
- narrower: [eliminative-materialism, type-identity-theory, token-identity-theory]
- related: [supervenience, functionalism, eliminative-materialism, mental-causation, mind-body-problem, qualia]
- prerequisites: [mind-body-problem]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Physicalism is the metaphysical thesis that everything that exists is either physical or stands in an asymmetric dependence relation (typically supervenience or grounding) on the physical, with the consequence that mental phenomena are either identical to physical phenomena, realized by them, or eliminable in their favor — the dominant default position in contemporary philosophy of mind.

**key_claim**: Physicalism has shifted over the twentieth century from type-identity formulations (each mental kind is identical to a specific physical kind) toward weaker dependence formulations (mental properties supervene on or are realized by physical properties), largely in response to the multiple-realizability argument that mental kinds are realized differently in different physical systems.

**warning**: Physicalism is often equated with the empirical successes of neuroscience, but Physicalism is a metaphysical thesis whose truth or falsity is not settled by neuroscience alone; the canonical objections (the conceivability argument, the knowledge argument, the explanatory-gap argument) target the metaphysical claim and remain live independent of how much we learn about brains.

## Scientific Realism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, epistemology]
- aliases: [realism about science]
- broader: [epistemology]
- related: [philosophy-of-science, underdetermination, theory-ladenness-of-observation, correspondence-theory-of-truth, fallibilism, inference-to-the-best-explanation]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-science]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Scientific Realism is the philosophical thesis that mature, predictively successful scientific theories should be interpreted as approximately true descriptions of an observer-independent reality, including the unobservable entities they posit — contrasted with anti-realist positions (instrumentalism, constructive empiricism) that treat theories as predictive instruments without ontological commitment to their unobservable posits.

**key_claim**: Scientific Realism is most strongly defended by the no-miracles argument — the predictive and technological success of mature science would be inexplicable if its theories were not at least approximately tracking real structure — and most strongly challenged by the pessimistic meta-induction from the discarded ontologies of past successful theories.

**warning**: Scientific Realism is often presented as the default common-sense position, but contemporary versions are highly qualified: structural realism, entity realism, and selective realism each retreat from full-theoretic realism in response to the historical record, and unrestricted Scientific Realism is a position few sophisticated philosophers of science currently hold.

## Supervenience

- secondary_domains: [metaphysics, philosophy-of-mind]
- aliases: [supervenience relation]
- broader: [philosophy-of-mind]
- related: [physicalism, mental-causation, emergence, functionalism, mind-body-problem]
- prerequisites: [physicalism]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Supervenience is the asymmetric dependence relation in which a higher-level set of properties (the supervening properties) is fixed by a lower-level set (the subvening or base properties) such that there can be no difference in the higher-level properties without a corresponding difference in the lower-level base — formalized in weak, strong, and global variants that differ in how the covariation is quantified.

**key_claim**: Supervenience emerged as the philosopher's tool of choice for stating non-reductive forms of physicalism: it captures the intuition that the mental depends on the physical without committing to property identity, but Jaegwon Kim's exclusion-argument literature has shown that supervenience by itself does not secure mental causation against the threat of physical pre-emption.

**warning**: Supervenience is often loosely equated with explanation or reduction, but the relation is purely covariational: A supervenes on B says nothing about why A covaries with B, whether A is reducible to B, or whether A causally influences anything; treating supervenience as an explanatory relation imports content the technical definition does not supply.

## Verificationism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [verification principle, logical positivism's criterion]
- broader: [epistemology]
- related: [philosophy-of-science, scientific-realism, epistemology, fallibilism, pragmatism, falsificationism]
- prerequisites: [epistemology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Verificationism is the family of theses, central to the logical positivism of the Vienna Circle, holding that the meaning of a non-tautological sentence consists in (or is exhausted by) the conditions of its empirical verification — and accordingly that statements that admit no possible empirical verification are literally meaningless rather than merely unsupported.

**key_claim**: Verificationism in its strong form is widely regarded as self-defeating because the verification principle itself does not appear to be empirically verifiable, and Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" further undermined the analytic-synthetic distinction on which the program relied; this combination drove the canonical mid-twentieth-century retreat from logical positivism.

**warning**: Verificationism is often dismissed as historical curiosity, but weak verificationist intuitions remain operative in contemporary philosophy of science (as testability constraints) and in scientific practice itself; assuming the position is dead conceals the fact that "if you cannot say what would count as evidence against it, it is not science" is a Verificationist principle in plain dress.

## Underdetermination

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, epistemology]
- aliases: [underdetermination of theory by data, Quine-Duhem thesis]
- broader: [philosophy-of-science]
- related: [scientific-realism, theory-ladenness-of-observation, fallibilism, inference-to-the-best-explanation, philosophy-of-science]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-science]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Underdetermination is the philosophical thesis, in its strong form associated with Quine and Duhem, that for any finite body of empirical evidence there exist multiple incompatible theories that fit the evidence equally well, with the consequence that data alone never uniquely select a theory and additional non-empirical criteria (simplicity, fertility, conservatism) are always doing some of the selection work.

**key_claim**: Underdetermination has weak and strong readings: the weak reading (any finite data are formally compatible with infinitely many curve-fits) is uncontroversial; the strong reading (all extant scientific theories have serious unconceived rivals that fit the same evidence) is contested but is the version that bears on Scientific Realism, and the dispute over which reading the historical record supports remains live.

**warning**: Underdetermination is often invoked as a one-line refutation of Scientific Realism, but the strong reading requires demonstrating actual unconceived rivals rather than merely asserting their possibility; the weak reading is compatible with realism via inference-to-the-best-explanation, so blanket appeals to Underdetermination without specifying which version is at stake produce equivocation rather than argument.

## Theory-Ladenness of Observation

- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-science, epistemology]
- aliases: [theory-laden observation, theory-ladenness]
- broader: [philosophy-of-science]
- related: [scientific-realism, underdetermination, philosophy-of-science, paradigm, conceptual-change]
- prerequisites: [philosophy-of-science]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Theory-Ladenness of Observation is the thesis, prominent in Hanson, Kuhn, and Feyerabend, that scientific observation is never neutral with respect to theory — what counts as an observation, how it is described, and which features are considered relevant are conditioned by the theoretical framework the observer brings to the perceptual situation.

**key_claim**: Theory-Ladenness of Observation has narrower and broader formulations: the narrow claim (theoretical concepts shape the description and salience of observations) is well supported by perception research; the broad claim (observations cannot adjudicate between rival theories at all) is not, and conflating the two converts a defensible cognitive thesis into an indefensible epistemological one.

**warning**: Theory-Ladenness of Observation is regularly used to license epistemic relativism — "all observation is just theory, so no theory is privileged" — but the inference is invalid: that observation is shaped by theory does not imply that all theory-shapings are equally good, and most contemporary defenders of the thesis explicitly reject the relativist reading.

## Truthmaker Principle

- secondary_domains: [metaphysics, philosophy-of-language]
- aliases: [truthmaker theory]
- broader: [correspondence-theory-of-truth]
- related: [correspondence-theory-of-truth, scientific-realism, ontology-design, philosophy-of-mind, epistemology]
- prerequisites: [correspondence-theory-of-truth]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Truthmaker Principle is the metaphysical thesis that every true proposition requires something in the world — a truthmaker — in virtue of which it is true, with the strength of the principle varying across formulations from "every truth has a truthmaker" (truthmaker maximalism) to restricted versions covering only positive contingent truths.

**key_claim**: The Truthmaker Principle functions as a methodological constraint on ontology: it forces theorists to specify what in their proposed inventory of the world is supposed to make their preferred truths true, exposing positions that affirm truths while denying any worldly basis for them — a form of cheap ontological commitment that the principle is designed to prevent.

**warning**: The Truthmaker Principle is often invoked as if it were neutral common sense, but maximalist versions face well-known difficulties (truths about absences, negative existentials, necessary truths), and the restricted versions that survive these objections do less of the work the principle is typically marshaled to do; using the principle without specifying its scope conceals where the controversial commitments lie.

## Theory of Knowledge

- secondary_domains: [philosophy, philosophy-of-mind]
- aliases: [epistemology proper]
- broader: [epistemology]
- narrower: [foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, virtue-epistemology]
- related: [epistemology, justified-true-belief, gettier-problem, fallibilism, knowledge-by-acquaintance-vs-description, social-epistemology]
- prerequisites: [epistemology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Theory of Knowledge is the central program of epistemology that asks what knowledge is, what its necessary and sufficient conditions are, how it differs from mere true belief, and what makes a belief justified — the program that organized analytic epistemology around the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief and was destabilized by Gettier's 1963 counterexamples.

**key_claim**: The Theory of Knowledge shifted decisively after Gettier from the search for additional clauses on top of justified true belief toward two broader strategies: externalist accounts that locate justification in reliable belief-forming processes rather than internally accessible reasons, and virtue-epistemological accounts that locate it in the cognitive character traits of the knower.

**warning**: The Theory of Knowledge is sometimes dismissed as a domain of esoteric counterexamples, but the central question — what distinguishes a belief that constitutes knowledge from a true belief that does not — has direct downstream consequences for testimony, expert authority, and the design of epistemic institutions; the apparent triviality of the seminar examples masks the practical bite of the underlying question.

## Skepticism

- secondary_domains: [philosophy, epistemology]
- aliases: [philosophical skepticism]
- broader: [epistemology]
- related: [theory-of-knowledge, fallibilism, justified-true-belief, gettier-problem, epistemic-vigilance, principle-of-charity]
- prerequisites: [theory-of-knowledge]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Skepticism is the philosophical position, in its global form, that knowledge of some target domain (the external world, other minds, the past, induction) is impossible or unjustified, and in its local form that some specific class of knowledge claims fails to meet the standards required for genuine knowledge — distinguished from ordinary doubt by its principled rather than merely circumstantial character.

**key_claim**: Skepticism functions in modern epistemology less as a position to be embraced than as a constraint that any positive theory of knowledge must address: the closure principle plus standard skeptical scenarios (the brain in a vat, the evil demon) generate paradoxes that any account of knowledge has to explain away or accept the consequences of, and how a theory handles Skepticism is one of its principal load-bearing tests.

**warning**: Skepticism is frequently confused with fallibilism — the view that knowledge is possible despite the impossibility of certainty — but the two positions are incompatible: fallibilism affirms what Skepticism denies; treating fallibilism as a mild form of Skepticism elides the substantive disagreement and obscures why most contemporary epistemologists are fallibilists rather than skeptics.
