# Batch: Self & Identity

## Self-Concept

- secondary_domains: [social-psychology, developmental-psychology]
- aliases: [self-schema, self-knowledge]
- broader: [self-and-identity]
- related: [academic-identity, self-efficacy, possible-selves, self-determination-theory]
- prerequisites: [educational-psychology]

**definition**: Self-Concept is the cognitive representation of who one is — the organized set of self-relevant beliefs, attributes, identities, and roles — distinguished from self-esteem (the evaluative dimension) and from self-efficacy (the situation-specific competence judgment), and is most usefully modeled as a hierarchically organized network of domain-specific self-schemas.

**key_claim**: Self-Concept derives its predictive power for behavior not from being an undifferentiated global construct but from its domain-specific structure: academic self-concept predicts academic outcomes, athletic self-concept predicts athletic outcomes, and global self-esteem reliably under-predicts both relative to the domain-matched constructs.

**warning**: Self-Concept research is routinely conflated with self-esteem research in popular writing, but the constructs have different antecedents and different consequences; interventions that successfully raise global self-esteem do not reliably alter domain-specific self-concept, and using one literature to support claims drawn from the other reproduces a long-standing methodological confusion.

## Learned Industriousness

- secondary_domains: [behavioral-psychology, motivation]
- aliases: [Eisenberger learned industriousness]
- broader: [motivational-psychology]
- related: [grit, deliberate-practice, effort-based-decision-making, intrinsic-motivation]
- prerequisites: [motivational-psychology]

**definition**: Learned Industriousness is Eisenberger's behavioral-psychology theory that high effort can become secondarily rewarding through a learning history in which effortful behavior has been reliably reinforced — producing a generalized disposition to expend effort across new tasks, anchored not in personality but in conditioned reinforcement history.

**key_claim**: Learned Industriousness reframes "effortful disposition" from a stable trait into a learning history: the willingness to work hard on novel tasks reflects the cumulative reinforcement of effortful behavior across prior tasks, which is why early environments that reliably reward effort produce downstream patterns that look temperamental but are not.

**warning**: Learned Industriousness is sometimes mobilized to recommend that any effortful behavior be reinforced regardless of its quality; the theory's empirical support concerns the reinforcement of effort that is actually demanded by the task, and arbitrary external rewards for unjustified effort produce the overjustification pattern rather than the industriousness pattern.

## Academic Identity

- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, identity-development]
- aliases: [academic self, scholar identity]
- broader: [self-concept]
- related: [self-concept, possible-selves, situated-learning-theory, communities-of-practice]
- prerequisites: [self-concept]

**definition**: Academic Identity is the dimension of self-concept that captures the extent to which a person sees themselves as a learner, scholar, or member of a particular academic discipline — comprising felt belonging, perceived competence, and projected future self — and is among the strongest predictors of long-run educational persistence above and beyond ability and prior achievement.

**key_claim**: Academic Identity is constructed through participation rather than discovered through introspection: the empirical pattern is that students develop disciplinary identities by being treated as members of the discipline (taking on its practices, its language, its quality criteria), which means identity is downstream of legitimate participation rather than a precondition for it.

**warning**: Academic Identity interventions that target only the felt-belonging dimension — without changing the participatory practices that legitimize membership — produce short-lived effects that fade once the intervention is withdrawn; the durable construction of academic identity requires structural access to disciplinary practice, not affirmation alone.
