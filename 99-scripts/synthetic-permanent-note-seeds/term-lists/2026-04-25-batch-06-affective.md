# Batch: Affective & Emotional Learning

## Cognitive Reappraisal

- secondary_domains: [emotion-regulation, clinical-psychology]
- aliases: [reappraisal, cognitive reframing]
- broader: [emotional-regulation]
- related: [academic-anxiety, cognitive-flexibility, attribution-retraining, growth-mindset]
- prerequisites: [emotional-regulation]

**definition**: Cognitive Reappraisal is the antecedent-focused emotion-regulation strategy in Gross's process model in which a person reinterprets the meaning of an emotion-eliciting situation before the full emotional response unfolds, dampening or transforming the emotion at its construal stage rather than at its expression stage.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Reappraisal differs in long-run cost-benefit profile from suppression-based regulation: by changing the meaning of the eliciting event, reappraisal reduces the affective load without the depletion and social costs that expressive suppression incurs, which is why it is the regulatory strategy most consistently associated with well-being outcomes.

**warning**: Cognitive Reappraisal is not universally adaptive; in situations the person can change, reappraisal can substitute for instrumental action and entrench passivity, and the strategy's benefits depend on its being deployed when the situation is genuinely uncontrollable rather than as a default for all aversive events.

## Academic Anxiety

- secondary_domains: [educational-psychology, emotion]
- aliases: [test anxiety, math anxiety]
- broader: [academic-emotions]
- related: [working-memory-capacity, cognitive-load-theory, self-worth-theory, attentional-control-theory]
- prerequisites: [academic-emotions]

**definition**: Academic Anxiety is the family of anxiety responses triggered by evaluative academic situations — including test anxiety, math anxiety, and writing anxiety — and is characterized by intrusive task-irrelevant thoughts, physiological arousal, and the consumption of working-memory resources that would otherwise be available for the academic task.

**key_claim**: Academic Anxiety degrades performance primarily through working-memory hijack rather than through skill deficit: anxious learners often possess the relevant skill but cannot deploy it under evaluative load because the worry stream occupies the very capacity the task requires, which is why interventions that off-load worry (e.g., expressive writing) produce immediate performance gains.

**warning**: Academic Anxiety is often framed as a motivational deficit ("they don't care enough to perform"), which inverts the empirical pattern: high-anxiety learners typically care intensely, and prescribing additional pressure as a remedy reliably worsens both the anxiety and the performance it impairs.

## Curiosity

- secondary_domains: [motivation, cognitive-psychology]
- aliases: [epistemic curiosity, perceptual curiosity]
- broader: [intrinsic-motivation]
- related: [situational-interest, individual-interest, the-information-gap-hypothesis, cognitive-disequilibrium]
- prerequisites: [motivational-psychology]

**definition**: Curiosity is the motivational state that orients attention and effort toward acquiring information for its own sake, most usefully decomposed into Berlyne's perceptual variant (driven by novelty in the immediate environment) and the epistemic variant (driven by awareness of a gap between what one knows and what one would like to know).

**key_claim**: Curiosity is most powerfully evoked by manageable knowledge gaps — Loewenstein's information-gap hypothesis predicts an inverted-U over the size of the gap — which is why instructional design that explicitly surfaces the learner's incomplete prior model outperforms designs that present complete information at the outset.

**warning**: Curiosity is sometimes treated as a stable trait to be either present or absent in a learner; the empirical pattern is that curiosity is highly state-dependent and gap-driven, and the appropriate intervention question is not whether the learner is curious but whether the situation has surfaced a gap they can hope to close.

## Boredom and Learning

- secondary_domains: [academic-emotions, educational-psychology]
- aliases: [academic boredom]
- broader: [academic-emotions]
- related: [mind-wandering, situational-interest, the-control-value-theory-of-achievement-emotions, flow]
- prerequisites: [academic-emotions]

**definition**: Boredom and Learning denotes the body of educational-psychology research on academic boredom — operationalized in Pekrun's control-value theory as the achievement emotion arising when the activity has neither high subjective control nor high subjective value — and on its bidirectional relation with attention, mind-wandering, and learning outcomes.

**key_claim**: Boredom and Learning research overturns the folk view that boredom is a benign byproduct of repetitive tasks: boredom is empirically associated with shallow processing, increased mind-wandering, and lower long-run motivation, and it functions as a signal that the value-control structure of the activity is misaligned for the learner.

**warning**: Boredom and Learning interventions that target only the surface of an activity — adding novelty or entertainment — often fail because they leave the underlying control-value structure intact; the diagnostic question is whether the learner sees the activity as both within their reach and personally meaningful, and surface enrichment without addressing both dimensions has limited effect.

## Emotional Intelligence

- secondary_domains: [personality-psychology, social-cognition]
- aliases: [EI, EQ]
- broader: [individual-differences]
- related: [emotional-regulation, self-awareness, empathy, social-cognition]
- prerequisites: [emotional-regulation]

**definition**: Emotional Intelligence is the cluster of constructs — most clearly articulated in Mayer and Salovey's ability model — that comprise the perception, use, understanding, and regulation of emotion in oneself and others, and that are conceptualized as a set of measurable abilities distinct from general cognitive ability and from personality.

**key_claim**: Emotional Intelligence as an ability construct shows incremental predictive validity over general intelligence and personality for outcomes that depend on emotion-laden judgment (negotiation, leadership, clinical performance), which is the empirical core that survives once the more popular trait and mixed models are bracketed.

**warning**: Emotional Intelligence in popular usage routinely conflates the ability model with mixed-model self-report instruments that overlap heavily with personality; the practical and research literatures behave very differently depending on which instrument is used, and citing one body of evidence as if it supported the other produces persistent confusion about what EI actually predicts.
