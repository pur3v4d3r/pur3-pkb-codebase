# Batch: Cognitive Psychology & Information Processing

## Cognitive Psychology

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [psychology, history-of-cognitive-science]
- aliases: [cognitivism]
- broader: [psychology, cognitive-science]
- narrower: [cognitive-psychology-foundations, information-processing-theory, memory-systems]
- related: [behaviourism, neuroscience, [[cognitive-revolution]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Psychology is the branch of psychology that investigates the internal mental processes — perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making — through experimental and computational methods, treating the mind as an information-processing system whose mechanisms can be inferred from behaviour and brain measurement.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Psychology emerged from the 1950s "cognitive revolution" as a rejection of strict behaviourism, establishing that internal representations and processes are legitimate scientific objects rather than unobservable theoretical fictions, and providing the conceptual scaffold on which contemporary learning science, [[human-computer-interaction]], and cognitive neuroscience are built.

**warning**: Cognitive Psychology has historically privileged controlled laboratory paradigms whose ecological validity is contested; pedagogical claims drawn directly from short laboratory tasks (e.g., list learning) without testing in classroom-relevant durations and contexts have repeatedly failed to scale, which is why translational efforts now demand classroom replications before adoption.

## Cognitive Psychology Foundations

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [psychology, history-of-cognitive-science]
- aliases: [foundations of cognitive psychology]
- broader: [cognitive-psychology]
- related: [information-processing-theory, [[cognitive-revolution]], [[chomskyan-linguistics]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cognitive Psychology Foundations refers to the originating theoretical commitments that distinguished mid-20th-century cognitivism from preceding behaviourism — the postulation of internal representations, the computer metaphor of mind, hypothesis-testing experimentation on unobservable mental processes, and the explanatory primacy of structure over response patterns.

**key_claim**: Cognitive Psychology Foundations rest on four converging influences usually credited together: Chomsky's critique of Skinnerian language learning, Newell and Simon's symbolic-processing models, the Atkinson-Shiffrin memory architecture, and Broadbent's information-flow models of attention; together these established that mental processes were a tractable scientific subject.

**warning**: Cognitive Psychology Foundations are sometimes taught as a single coherent paradigm, but the original "computer metaphor" framing has been substantially revised — by connectionism, embodied cognition, and predictive processing — and uncritically importing the 1960s symbolic-processing assumptions into modern educational design imports their already-known limitations.

## Information Processing Theory

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [psychology, learning-science]
- aliases: [IP theory, information-processing model]
- broader: [cognitive-psychology]
- related: [multi-store-model, working-memory, cognitive-load-theory, [[cognitive-architecture]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Information Processing Theory models human cognition as a sequence of operations on internal representations — encoding, storage, transformation, retrieval, response — analogous to computation, and provides the framework within which capacity limits, processing stages, and serial-versus-parallel debates have been formulated.

**key_claim**: Information Processing Theory's central contribution to education is the explicit identification of *bottlenecks* (sensory register capacity, working-memory span, attentional selection) whose location dictates where instructional design must intervene; [[cognitive-load-theory]] is the most influential pedagogical descendant of this commitment.

**warning**: Information Processing Theory has been criticised for treating cognition as disembodied, sequential, and context-free; its predictions hold best for tasks that resemble its original test cases (digit span, paired-associate learning) and weaken substantially for ecologically rich, temporally extended, socially distributed activities — exactly the tasks education most needs to support.

## Executive Function

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [neuroscience, developmental-psychology]
- aliases: [executive functions, cognitive control]
- broader: [cognitive-control]
- narrower: [working-memory, [[inhibitory-control]], [[cognitive-flexibility]]]
- related: [central-executive, attention-and-cognitive-control, [[prefrontal-cortex]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Executive Function names the family of top-down control processes — typically decomposed into updating (working-memory refresh), inhibition (suppressing prepotent responses), and shifting (switching between tasks or rule-sets) — that coordinate goal-directed behaviour and are anchored neurally in prefrontal-parietal networks.

**key_claim**: Executive Function predicts academic achievement, self-regulation, and life outcomes more robustly than many traditional ability measures because it captures the capacity to maintain and pursue goals against interference, which is the demand structure of nearly every complex learning task.

**warning**: Executive Function is often treated as a unitary "muscle" that can be trained with brief computer games, but the bulk of the evidence shows narrow task-specific gains with weak transfer; the intervention that most reliably *produces* executive-function gains is sustained engagement with cognitively demanding domains (instruments, second languages, sports), not isolated training programmes.

## Attention and Cognitive Control

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [neuroscience, learning-science]
- aliases: [attentional control]
- broader: [cognitive-control, executive-function]
- related: [selective-attention, attention-and-selective-processing, working-memory-capacity, [[interference-resolution]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Attention and Cognitive Control refers to the set of mechanisms by which limited processing resources are deliberately allocated to task-relevant information and protected from interference — encompassing selection, sustained vigilance, switching, and conflict resolution — and is operationalised by paradigms such as Stroop, flanker, and antisaccade tasks.

**key_claim**: Attention and Cognitive Control is the proximate determinant of how much of a learning episode actually reaches durable encoding: a learner whose attention is captured by irrelevant stimuli or whose control fails to sustain focus encodes a fragmented trace regardless of how good the materials are, which is why classroom and digital-environment design materially shape outcomes.

**warning**: Attention and Cognitive Control is often invoked to blame individual learners for distraction, but the controlling environment is at least as causal; phone notifications, multitasking norms, and visually busy interfaces routinely defeat even high-control individuals, and policy interventions (notification suppression, single-window study, blocked time) typically outperform attempts to "train willpower."

## Attention and Selective Processing

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [perception-research, cognitive-control]
- aliases: [attentional selection]
- broader: [attention-and-cognitive-control]
- related: [selective-attention, [[bottleneck-models]], [[load-theory-of-attention]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Attention and Selective Processing names the mechanism by which the cognitive system filters the vast incoming sensory and mental stream so that only a subset receives full perceptual analysis and access to working memory, with the remainder either rejected, processed shallowly, or held in implicit traces.

**key_claim**: Attention and Selective Processing operates at multiple loci — early perceptual, late semantic, and post-categorical — and the locus of selection depends on task load, which is the central insight of Lavie's load theory: under low perceptual load, irrelevant material is processed; under high load, it is filtered automatically.

**warning**: Attention and Selective Processing carries an inherent cost: information selected against is not merely unattended but can be actively suppressed, and that suppression sometimes persists beyond the immediate task — a learner instructed to ignore a distractor can later show impaired access to its content even when it becomes relevant, which complicates "ignore the noise" pedagogical advice.

## Selective Attention

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [perception-research]
- aliases: [focused attention]
- broader: [attention-and-selective-processing]
- related: [attention-and-cognitive-control, [[cocktail-party-effect]], [[dichotic-listening]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Selective Attention is the capacity to focus processing on a chosen stream of stimulation — an auditory channel, a visual location, a feature dimension — while excluding others, and is the construct measured by classic dichotic-listening, visual-search, and flanker paradigms.

**key_claim**: Selective Attention is not all-or-none: unattended channels still receive some processing (the listener's name in the unattended ear is detected reliably), demonstrating that selection sets a *gain* on processing rather than a hard gate, with consequences for how unattended educational stimuli (background video, peer screens) leak into encoding.

**warning**: Selective Attention is asymmetric across modalities and ages; auditory selective attention develops later and remains weaker than visual, and design choices that assume listeners can ignore competing speech (open-plan classrooms, podcast-while-reading) systematically degrade comprehension regardless of the listener's stated preference.

## Mental Model

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [knowledge-representation, learning-science]
- aliases: [mental models]
- broader: [knowledge-representation]
- related: [situation-model, schema-theory, [[analogical-reasoning]], [[causal-reasoning]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Mental Model is an internal representation of a domain's structure — entities, relations, and causal dynamics — that supports simulation, prediction, and inference about situations the model represents, and was systematically theorised by Johnson-Laird (1983) as the substrate of deductive reasoning and discourse comprehension.

**key_claim**: A Mental Model differs from a propositional or schematic representation by being analog and runnable: the learner can mentally manipulate the model to derive consequences not explicitly stated, which is why instruction that builds a working Mental Model produces more flexible transfer than instruction that delivers equivalent propositions in list form.

**warning**: A Mental Model is often *incomplete*, *inconsistent*, or *unstable* in non-experts, and learners typically don't notice these deficiencies until they generate predictions that fail; pedagogy that demands explicit prediction-and-test (rather than only exposition) is what surfaces and corrects faulty Mental Models, which is why expository teaching alone tends to leave naive misconceptions intact.

## Situation Model

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [reading-research, discourse-comprehension]
- aliases: [event model]
- broader: [mental-model]
- related: [working-memory-in-reading, [[reading-comprehension]], [[event-segmentation-theory]]]
- prerequisites: [mental-model]
- confidence: high

**definition**: A Situation Model is the integrated representation a reader or listener constructs of the *state of affairs* described by a text — the entities, locations, times, intentions, and causal connections — distinct from both the surface form (verbatim) and the propositional textbase (paraphrase-equivalent meaning), and is the level at which deep comprehension occurs.

**key_claim**: A Situation Model is what supports inference, integration across paragraphs, and recall after surface details have faded; readers who fail to build a Situation Model can pass paraphrase tests yet fail comprehension probes that require integrating across distant text, which is the pattern characteristic of "shallow but fluent" reading.

**warning**: A Situation Model is built incrementally and depends on working-memory resources to bridge across sentences; texts that exceed the reader's capacity to hold prior context active (long sentences, distant referents, dense terminology) cause Situation Model construction to fail silently — the reader's eyes continue moving but integration stops, producing the illusion of reading without comprehension.

## Fluid Intelligence

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [intelligence-research, individual-differences]
- aliases: [Gf, fluid reasoning]
- broader: [intelligence]
- related: [crystallized-intelligence, working-memory-capacity, executive-function, [[g-factor]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Fluid Intelligence, in Cattell's distinction, is the capacity to reason and solve novel problems independently of acquired knowledge — pattern detection, rule induction, abstract analogy — typically measured by Raven's Progressive Matrices and similar non-verbal tasks, and peaks in early adulthood before slow decline.

**key_claim**: Fluid Intelligence correlates strongly with [[working-memory-capacity]] and [[executive-function]], suggesting these are partially overlapping constructs whose common core is controlled attention and the ability to hold and manipulate novel relations under interference.

**warning**: Fluid Intelligence has resisted training: claims that working-memory training raises Fluid Intelligence have repeatedly failed independent replication, and the more durable strategy for raising performance on fluid-intelligence-loaded tasks is to *convert them to crystallized tasks* by building domain knowledge that turns reasoning problems into pattern-recognition problems for the trained learner.

## Crystallized Intelligence

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [intelligence-research, learning-science]
- aliases: [Gc, crystallized ability]
- broader: [intelligence]
- related: [fluid-intelligence, semantic-memory, expertise-development, [[knowledge-base]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Crystallized Intelligence is the body of acquired knowledge, vocabulary, and culturally transmitted skill that an individual can deploy on familiar tasks, conceived in Cattell's theory as the accumulated deposit of [[fluid-intelligence]] applied across the life span and supported by [[semantic-memory]] and [[procedural-memory]].

**key_claim**: Crystallized Intelligence continues to grow into late adulthood even as [[fluid-intelligence]] declines, which is the empirical basis for the observation that older experts often outperform younger novices on real-world tasks despite slower reasoning on abstract puzzles — knowledge structures compensate for reduced raw processing.

**warning**: Crystallized Intelligence is domain-bounded: high crystallized ability in one field rarely transfers to another, and the popular framing of "wisdom" or "general expertise" overstates the breadth of what cumulates; pedagogy aimed at building Crystallized Intelligence must be explicit about which domain it is building knowledge *in*.

## Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [CTML, Mayer's CTML]
- broader: [learning-theories, cognitive-load-theory]
- related: [dual-coding-theory, redundancy-effect, [[modality-effect]], [[coherence-principle]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-load-theory, dual-coding-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, developed by Richard Mayer, integrates [[dual-coding-theory]], [[cognitive-load-theory]], and active-learning constructivism into an empirically tested framework for designing instruction that uses words and pictures together, formalised as a set of design principles each backed by experimental contrast studies.

**key_claim**: The Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning's principles — coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, multimedia, personalisation, voice — are the most empirically validated set of instructional-design rules in the field, with dozens of independent replications across content domains.

**warning**: The Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning's principles assume novice learners; many of them reverse for experts (the "expertise-reversal effect") because what scaffolds a novice becomes redundant and load-inducing for an expert, which is why generic application of CTML rules to advanced learners can degrade rather than improve learning.

## Redundancy Effect

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [learning-science, instructional-design]
- aliases: [redundancy principle]
- broader: [cognitive-theory-of-multimedia-learning, cognitive-load-theory]
- related: [modality-effect, [[expertise-reversal-effect]], [[split-attention-effect]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-load-theory]
- confidence: high

**definition**: The Redundancy Effect is the empirical finding that adding on-screen text that *duplicates* spoken narration degrades rather than improves multimedia learning, because the learner is forced to mentally reconcile two redundant verbal streams that compete for the same phonological-loop resources.

**key_claim**: The Redundancy Effect is one of the most counter-intuitive findings in instructional design: pedagogically well-meaning practices like simultaneous narration plus full-text captions for non-disabled learners actively reduce learning, which has guided Mayer's coherence-related principles toward eliminating, not adding, redundant verbal channels.

**warning**: The Redundancy Effect does not generalise to all learners or all conditions: for second-language learners, hearing-impaired learners, or learners encountering technical vocabulary for the first time, captions cease to be redundant and become essential; the Redundancy Effect is a default for fluent native learners that must be conditionally suspended in accessibility-sensitive design.

## Distributed Cognition

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [anthropology, hci]
- aliases: [DCog]
- broader: [cognitive-science]
- related: [embodied-cognition, situated-learning-theory, communities-of-practice, [[external-representation]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Distributed Cognition, developed primarily by Edwin Hutchins, treats cognitive processes as constituted across people, artefacts, and environments rather than confined to a single skull — analysing, for example, ship navigation as a computation distributed over instruments, charts, and crew members coordinated through socially defined practices.

**key_claim**: Distributed Cognition reframes "smart performance" as a property of socio-technical systems rather than individuals: the same human becomes a different cognitive agent depending on which artefacts, notations, and collaborators are present, which has direct implications for how PKB design (notes, links, dashboards) constitutes thinking rather than merely recording it.

**warning**: Distributed Cognition is sometimes used to dissolve the individual altogether, but the original framing preserves individual cognition as one component of the larger system; pedagogy that swings to the extreme — "all learning is social" — loses sight of the durable knowledge structures individuals must internalise to participate competently in distributed systems.

## Embodied Cognition

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind, neuroscience]
- aliases: [embodiment, grounded cognition]
- broader: [cognitive-science, 4e-cognition]
- related: [distributed-cognition, [[sensorimotor-grounding]], [[mirror-neurons]]]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Embodied Cognition holds that cognitive processes are deeply shaped by the body's sensorimotor systems and its specific physical capabilities — that concepts are partially constituted by simulations in modality-specific neural substrates, and that thinking recruits the same systems used for perception and action rather than running on amodal symbols.

**key_claim**: Embodied Cognition is supported by behavioural evidence (action-sentence compatibility effects, gesture-aided learning), neuroimaging (motor-cortex activation during action verbs), and computational models, and motivates pedagogy that engages bodily action and gesture as integral to concept formation rather than mere illustration.

**warning**: Embodied Cognition is often overstated as the claim that *all* cognition is bodily; the more defensible position is that some concepts are deeply embodied while others (logical, mathematical, abstract) are at most weakly grounded, and pedagogy that demands embodiment for every concept manufactures unmotivated movement that fails to scaffold learning.

## 4E Cognition

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [philosophy-of-mind]
- aliases: [4e cognition, embodied embedded enactive extended]
- broader: [cognitive-science]
- narrower: [embodied-cognition, [[enactive-cognition]], [[extended-mind]], [[embedded-cognition]]]
- related: [distributed-cognition, ecological-psychology, predictive-processing]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: 4E Cognition is the umbrella term for four post-classical commitments — Embodied (constituted by bodily systems), Embedded (continuously coupled to context), Enactive (emerging through action-environment dynamics), and Extended (legitimately incorporating external tools) — that together challenge the brain-bound, computational picture of mind.

**key_claim**: 4E Cognition reframes the unit of analysis for understanding cognitive performance: rather than asking what is "in the head," analysis should specify the brain-body-environment system whose joint dynamics produce the behaviour, which has direct payoff for designing learning environments rather than only learners.

**warning**: 4E Cognition is a family of partially overlapping and partially conflicting positions, not a single theory; pedagogical proposals that invoke "4E" as if it were unified usually pick favourite elements (embodiment for gesture, extended mind for note-taking) without committing to the deeper enactive critique that they often presuppose.

## Predictive Processing

- domain: cognitive-science
- secondary_domains: [neuroscience, philosophy-of-mind]
- aliases: [predictive coding, free-energy framework]
- broader: [cognitive-science]
- related: [[active-inference], [[bayesian-brain]], 4e-cognition]
- prerequisites: [cognitive-psychology]
- confidence: high

**definition**: Predictive Processing is the framework — most associated with Karl Friston, Andy Clark, and Jakob Hohwy — that models the brain as a hierarchical prediction machine continually generating top-down expectations and adjusting internal models on the basis of prediction error from bottom-up sensory input, unifying perception, action, and learning under a single inferential principle.

**key_claim**: Predictive Processing reframes attention as the precision-weighting of prediction error, learning as model update, and action as the selection of behaviours that minimise expected future prediction error; this gives a single computational story for what previously required separate accounts.

**warning**: Predictive Processing is sometimes presented as if it has won the explanatory contest, but its empirical commitments at the level of specific tasks remain underdetermined, and many of its claimed predictions are also derivable from non-predictive frameworks; importing it into educational theory before its scope and limits are settled risks attaching pedagogical recommendations to an evolving target.

## Ecological Psychology

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [perception-research, philosophy-of-mind]
- aliases: [Gibsonian psychology]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [embodied-cognition, [[affordance]], [[direct-perception]], 4e-cognition]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Ecological Psychology, founded by James J. Gibson, holds that perception is direct pickup of information specifying [[affordance|affordances]] in the organism-environment system rather than inferential construction from impoverished sensory data, and reframes cognitive science around the meaningful structure of the lawful organism-environment relation.

**key_claim**: Ecological Psychology challenges the representation-centric assumption underlying classical [[cognitive-psychology]]: many tasks (catching a ball, navigating a hallway) are better explained by lawful information pickup from optic flow than by internal model construction, which has been a central source of ideas in [[4e-cognition]] and motor learning.

**warning**: Ecological Psychology is often invoked to reject internal representation altogether, but the strongest position is more nuanced — direct perception accounts well for tightly coupled action-perception loops while struggling with off-line, displaced, or symbolic cognition; pedagogy that universalises ecological commitments under-resources what classical cognitive accounts handle better.

## Functional Psychology

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [history-of-psychology]
- aliases: [functionalism]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [pragmatism, [[william-james]], [[john-dewey]], evolutionary-psychology]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Functional Psychology is the late-19th- and early-20th-century American school — associated with William James, John Dewey, James Angell, and Harvey Carr — that defined psychology's subject matter as the *functions* of mind in adapting the organism to its environment, in deliberate contrast to the structuralist project of cataloguing conscious contents.

**key_claim**: Functional Psychology established the lineage that runs from [[pragmatism]] through educational reform (Dewey) into modern [[ecological-psychology]] and applied behavioural science: the question "what is this mental capacity *for*?" — its adaptive function in the organism's life — is its enduring methodological contribution.

**warning**: Functional Psychology's adaptationist framing can drift into Panglossian over-explanation, where every mental quirk is rationalised as serving some adaptive purpose; modern functional reasoning tightly couples function attribution to evidence (developmental, comparative, neural) rather than to plausibility alone.

## Developmental Psychology

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [education, neuroscience]
- aliases: [developmental science]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [genetic-epistemology, [[lifespan-development]], [[piaget]], [[vygotsky]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Developmental Psychology is the scientific study of systematic change in cognition, emotion, and behaviour across the life span — from prenatal development through old age — combining longitudinal, cross-sectional, microgenetic, and increasingly neural methods to explain *how* and *why* psychological capacities change.

**key_claim**: Developmental Psychology's central pedagogical contribution is the recognition that learners are not undersized adults: cognitive capacities, motivational structures, social cognition, and self-regulation undergo non-linear transformations that determine which instructional approaches will work at which age, which is why universal "best practices" routinely fail when applied across developmental windows.

**warning**: Developmental Psychology has historically over-relied on age-graded stage models (especially Piagetian) whose sequence and necessity have been substantially qualified by later research; treating stage descriptions as rigid timetables rather than as approximate trajectories causes pedagogy to underestimate younger learners and overestimate older ones in domain-dependent ways.

## Humanistic Psychology

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [philosophy, education]
- aliases: [humanistic-existential psychology]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [[maslow], [[carl-rogers], self-determination-theory, [[positive-psychology]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Humanistic Psychology is the mid-20th-century "third force" — organised against psychoanalysis and behaviourism by Maslow, Rogers, and others — that centred subjective experience, agency, meaning-making, self-actualisation, and the conditions of psychological flourishing as the proper subject matter of psychology.

**key_claim**: Humanistic Psychology established the conceptual ground from which [[self-determination-theory]] and [[positive-psychology]] later emerged with empirical methodology: the central claim that humans have intrinsic motivational tendencies toward growth, integration, and meaning continues as the kernel of contemporary motivation science.

**warning**: Humanistic Psychology's classical formulations (Maslow's hierarchy, Rogerian core conditions) are widely cited but weakly supported empirically in their original strict form; importing them into pedagogy as established science overstates their evidential standing, while the empirically updated descendants (SDT, Csikszentmihalyi's flow research) carry the productive insights forward on firmer ground.

## Cross-Cultural Psychology

- domain: psychology
- secondary_domains: [anthropology, methodology]
- aliases: [cross-cultural research]
- broader: [psychology]
- related: [[cultural-psychology], [[weird-samples], [[indigenous-psychology]]]
- prerequisites: []
- confidence: high

**definition**: Cross-Cultural Psychology systematically compares psychological processes across cultural groups to determine which aspects of human cognition, emotion, and behaviour are universal versus culturally constructed, and serves as a methodological corrective to claims that generalise from a narrow demographic base.

**key_claim**: Cross-Cultural Psychology's central empirical contribution since Henrich and colleagues' 2010 critique is the demonstration that much of psychology's evidence base derives from "WEIRD" populations (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic) that are systematically unrepresentative of humanity, requiring that any claim of universality survive cross-cultural test.

**warning**: Cross-Cultural Psychology is sometimes used to defend extreme cultural relativism that denies any human universals, but the productive use of the field is the opposite: it identifies which findings *do* survive cross-cultural test (basic memory architecture, infant cognitive milestones, color perception primitives) and which collapse, allowing more accurate calibration of generalisability.
