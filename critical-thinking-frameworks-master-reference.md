# Critical Thinking Frameworks — Master Reference

> [!abstract] Document Purpose
> This reference compiles the most influential conceptual frameworks, definitions, and theoretical models of [[Critical Thinking]] across cognitive science, education, philosophy, and psychology. It is a *companion volume* to the dedicated [[Paul-Elder Framework]] reference and is designed for cross-framework comparison, deliberate practice triangulation, and PKB-integrated study. Each framework is presented with its core architecture, defining commitments, characteristic vocabulary, and practical application notes — so that comparative analysis across models becomes possible.

> [!key-claim] The Strategic Value of Multi-Framework Fluency
> No single framework captures critical thinking exhaustively. Each was developed in response to a specific intellectual problem — operationalizing testing (Watson-Glaser), achieving expert consensus (Delphi), describing cognitive complexity (Bloom), modeling argument structure (Toulmin), accounting for development (Perry, King & Kitchener), explaining dual-system cognition (Kahneman/Stanovich), or grounding thinking in intellectual character (Paul-Elder). Mastery requires holding the **complementary affordances** of these frameworks simultaneously: each illuminates aspects others obscure.

# Section A — Foundational Definitions of Critical Thinking

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> The frameworks in this section attempt to *define* critical thinking — to specify the conceptual core that distinguishes critical from uncritical reasoning. Each definition reflects a distinct theoretical commitment about what is essential to critical thought.

---

## A.1 — Robert Ennis's Streamlined Conception

[Framework:: Ennis-Streamlined-Conception]
[Originator:: Robert H. Ennis]
[Key-Works:: 1962, 1985, 1987, 1996, 2011]
[Type:: Definition + Skill/Disposition Taxonomy]

**Core Definition:** Critical thinking is "**reasonable, reflective thinking focused on deciding what to believe or do.**"

**Significance:** Ennis's definition is one of the most widely cited in the literature. It does three crucial things in a single sentence: (1) it specifies the *quality criterion* — thinking must be reasonable and reflective; (2) it specifies the *cognitive object* — beliefs and actions; (3) it positions critical thinking as a *practical* activity, not merely theoretical contemplation.

**The Streamlined Conception** consists of **12 critical thinking abilities** organized into five clusters, plus **14 dispositions**.

### The Five Ability Clusters

1. **Basic Clarification**
   - Focusing on a question
   - Analyzing arguments
   - Asking and answering clarifying questions

2. **Bases for a Decision**
   - Judging the credibility of a source
   - Observing and judging observation reports

3. **Inference**
   - Deducing and judging deductions
   - Inducing and judging inductions
   - Making and judging value judgments

4. **Advanced Clarification**
   - Defining terms and judging definitions
   - Identifying assumptions

5. **Supposition and Integration**
   - Considering and reasoning from premises with which one disagrees
   - Integrating other abilities and dispositions in making and defending a decision

### The FRISCO Model — Ennis's Practical Decision Heuristic

Ennis distilled his framework into a six-step practical model for evaluating any piece of reasoning:

| Letter | Step | Diagnostic Question |
|---|---|---|
| **F** | **Focus** | What is the main question, conclusion, or issue? |
| **R** | **Reasons** | What are the reasons offered in support? |
| **I** | **Inference** | Is the reasoning from those reasons to the conclusion acceptable? |
| **S** | **Situation** | What is the context — physical, social, intellectual — that frames the argument? |
| **C** | **Clarity** | Are the meanings of key terms clear? |
| **O** | **Overview** | Step back and check the whole — does the reasoning hold up under integrated review? |

> [!example] Application Note
> **FRISCO** is particularly powerful as a *checklist for argument evaluation* in pedagogical settings. Its modesty (six steps, plain language) hides its rigor — applied systematically, it covers most of what more elaborate frameworks accomplish through more complex apparatus.

### Ennis's 14 Dispositions

Ennis grouped dispositions into three clusters: (1) **Care that beliefs be true and decisions justified**, (2) **Represent positions honestly and clearly**, (3) **Care about the dignity and worth of every person**.

Selected core dispositions:
- Seek and offer clear statements of theses or questions
- Seek and offer reasons
- Try to be well-informed
- Use and mention credible sources
- Take into account the total situation
- Keep in mind the original or basic concern
- Look for alternatives
- Be open-minded
- Take a position when the evidence is sufficient
- Seek as much precision as the subject permits
- Be reflective — aware of one's own basic beliefs

## A.2 — The APA Delphi Consensus (Facione, 1990)

[Framework:: Delphi-Consensus]
[Originator:: Peter Facione (panel chair)]
[Method:: Delphi Method — iterative expert consensus]
[Panel-Size:: 46 critical thinking experts]
[Duration:: 2 years (1988-1990)]
[Publication:: APA, 1990 — "The Delphi Report"]

**Origin and Method:** Commissioned by the American Philosophical Association, this study used the [[Delphi Method]] — a structured technique for achieving expert consensus through iterative anonymous rounds of feedback — to produce a definitive characterization of critical thinking that would transcend any single theorist's view.

**The Consensus Definition:**

> "We understand critical thinking to be **purposeful, self-regulatory judgment** which results in **interpretation, analysis, evaluation, and inference**, as well as **explanation** of the evidential, conceptual, methodological, criteriological, or contextual considerations upon which that judgment is based."

**Significance:** Because the Delphi Report represents *consensus* among 46 leading experts across philosophy, psychology, and education, it functions as a kind of canonical reference point. When frameworks converge with the Delphi findings, that convergence is empirical evidence for the construct.

### The Six Core Cognitive Skills

| # | Skill | Sub-Skills |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Interpretation** | Categorization, decoding significance, clarifying meaning |
| **2** | **Analysis** | Examining ideas, identifying arguments, identifying reasons and claims |
| **3** | **Evaluation** | Assessing claims, assessing arguments |
| **4** | **Inference** | Querying evidence, conjecturing alternatives, drawing conclusions |
| **5** | **Explanation** | Stating results, justifying procedures, presenting arguments |
| **6** | **Self-Regulation** | Self-examination, self-correction |

> [!definition] Self-Regulation as the Capstone Skill
> Self-regulation is positioned as *metacognitive* — it operates upon the other five skills, monitoring their application and triggering corrections. Without self-regulation, the other skills can be deployed in service of motivated reasoning. The Delphi panel treats it as the most distinctively *critical* of the critical thinking skills.

### The Ideal Critical Thinker (Delphi Disposition Profile)

The Delphi panel additionally produced a profile of the ideal critical thinker, organized into two clusters:

**Approach to Life and Living in General:**
- Inquisitiveness with regard to a wide range of issues
- Concern to become and remain well-informed
- Alertness to opportunities to use critical thinking
- Trust in the processes of reasoned inquiry
- Self-confidence in one's own ability to reason
- Open-mindedness regarding divergent worldviews
- Flexibility in considering alternatives and opinions
- Understanding of the opinions of other people
- Fair-mindedness in appraising reasoning
- Honesty in facing one's own biases, prejudices, stereotypes, or egocentric tendencies
- Prudence in suspending, making, or altering judgments
- Willingness to reconsider and revise views

**Approach to Specific Issues, Questions, or Problems:**
- Clarity in stating the question or concern
- Orderliness in working with complexity
- Diligence in seeking relevant information
- Reasonableness in selecting and applying criteria
- Care in focusing attention on the concern at hand
- Persistence through difficulties
- Precision to the degree permitted by the subject

> [!key-claim] The Skills-Plus-Dispositions Architecture
> The Delphi Report's most enduring contribution may be the explicit pairing of **skills** (what a critical thinker can *do*) with **dispositions** (what a critical thinker is *inclined* to do). A person can possess the skills without the dispositions to deploy them — and skill without disposition is, in Facione's terms, "unrealized critical thinking."

## A.3 — Harvey Siegel's Reasons Conception

[Framework:: Siegel-Reasons-Conception]
[Originator:: Harvey Siegel]
[Key-Work:: *Educating Reason* (1988)]

**Core Definition:** A critical thinker is "**one who is appropriately moved by reasons.**"

**The Two Components:**

1. **Reason Assessment Component** — The skills required to assess reasons properly: understanding what counts as a good reason, evaluating reasons in particular cases, distinguishing strong from weak reasons.

2. **Critical Spirit Component** — The dispositional commitment to *actually be moved by reasons* — to care about reasons, seek them, demand them, follow them where they lead.

**Significance:** Siegel's definition is philosophically minimalist but normatively strong. By tying critical thinking to **reasons** (not skills, not procedures, not heuristics), he grounds the construct in the broader philosophical tradition of [[Rationality]]. The "appropriately moved by" formulation captures both the cognitive component (correctly identifying good reasons) and the motivational/dispositional component (allowing those reasons to actually shape one's beliefs and actions).

[Distinctive-Commitment:: Critical thinking is *constitutively* connected to rationality, not merely instrumentally useful]

## A.4 — Diane Halpern's Framework

[Framework:: Halpern-Critical-Thinking]
[Originator:: Diane F. Halpern]
[Key-Work:: *Thought and Knowledge* (multiple editions)]
[Associated-Assessment:: Halpern Critical Thinking Assessment (HCTA)]

**Core Definition:** Critical thinking is "**the use of those cognitive skills or strategies that increase the probability of a desirable outcome.**" Critical thinking is "purposeful, reasoned, and goal-directed."

**Distinctive Commitment:** Halpern is explicitly empirical and psychological — she defines critical thinking in terms of *cognitive effectiveness* and emphasizes the importance of **transfer** (the ability to apply critical thinking skills across contexts).

### Halpern's Four-Component Model

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Disposition** | Willingness to engage in effortful thinking — the attitudinal foundation |
| **Skills** | The actual cognitive abilities required for analysis, evaluation, decision-making |
| **Metacognitive Monitoring** | Awareness of one's own thinking — including knowledge of when and how to deploy specific skills |
| **Structural Knowledge for Transfer** | Knowledge organized in ways that promote transfer across novel contexts |

### The HCTA's Five Skill Categories

The Halpern Critical Thinking Assessment operationalizes five clusters:

1. **Verbal Reasoning** — Detecting persuasion techniques, evaluating arguments, recognizing rhetoric
2. **Argument Analysis** — Identifying and assessing argument structure
3. **Thinking as Hypothesis Testing** — Using empirical/scientific reasoning
4. **Likelihood and Uncertainty** — Probabilistic and statistical reasoning
5. **Decision Making and Problem Solving** — Applied reasoning under real-world constraints

> [!example] What Distinguishes Halpern
> Halpern's emphasis on **transfer** addresses a recurring criticism of critical thinking instruction: students learn to perform CT tasks in instructional contexts but fail to deploy those skills in novel domains. Her framework foregrounds the structural knowledge requirements needed for genuine cross-domain transfer.

## A.5 — Sharon Bailin et al.'s Reconceptualization

[Framework:: Bailin-Reconception]
[Originators:: Sharon Bailin, Roland Case, Jerrold Coombs, Leroi Daniels]
[Key-Work:: 1999 series in *Journal of Curriculum Studies*]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Bailin and colleagues argue *against* skill-list conceptions of critical thinking. Their critique: skills cannot exist independently of (a) the knowledge domains in which they operate and (b) the criteria of good thinking specific to those domains.

**Their Reconception — Three Components:**

1. **Background Knowledge** — Substantive knowledge of the domain; one cannot think critically about biology without knowing biology
2. **Critical Concepts** — The conceptual apparatus needed for evaluation (e.g., what counts as evidence, what counts as a fallacy, what counts as a relevant counterexample)
3. **Vocabulary, Strategies, Heuristics** — The tactical knowledge required to engage in actual critical work

**Why It Matters:** Bailin's reconception is the most prominent **anti-generic** position in the field. It opposes the view that critical thinking can be taught as a domain-general skill set, instead insisting that critical thinking is always thinking *about something* and that the something matters constitutively.

[Theoretical-Tension:: Bailin vs. generic CT proponents on domain-specificity]
[Related-Position:: [[John McPeck]]'s subject-specificity argument]

# Section B — Cognitive Process Taxonomies

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> Taxonomies in this section attempt to *classify cognitive processes by complexity or type*. They serve curriculum design, assessment construction, and instructional planning — providing vocabularies for distinguishing low-level from high-level thinking.

---

## B.1 — Bloom's Original Taxonomy (1956)

[Framework:: Blooms-Taxonomy-Original]
[Originator:: Benjamin Bloom (chair) + committee]
[Year:: 1956]
[Domain:: Cognitive Domain (the original taxonomy)]
[Companion-Domains:: Affective, Psychomotor]

**Origin:** Developed by a committee of educators led by Benjamin Bloom to create a standardized vocabulary for educational objectives. Originally one of three planned domains (cognitive, affective, psychomotor) — only cognitive received the canonical treatment that made it famous.

### The Six Original Levels (Lowest → Highest)

| # | Level | Definition | Verb Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Knowledge** | Recall of facts, terms, basic concepts | Define, list, name, recall, state |
| **2** | **Comprehension** | Understanding meaning of information | Describe, explain, summarize, paraphrase, classify |
| **3** | **Application** | Using information in new situations | Apply, demonstrate, solve, use, implement |
| **4** | **Analysis** | Breaking information into component parts | Analyze, compare, contrast, differentiate, examine |
| **5** | **Synthesis** | Combining elements into new whole | Compose, construct, create, design, formulate |
| **6** | **Evaluation** | Judging value of materials/methods | Appraise, critique, defend, judge, justify |

**Significance:** Bloom's original taxonomy structured educational thinking globally for half a century. It became the lingua franca of curriculum design, learning objectives, assessment construction, and pedagogical discussion.

**Key Properties:**
- **Hierarchical:** Higher levels presuppose mastery of lower levels
- **Cumulative:** Each level builds on prior levels
- **Unidimensional:** A single dimension of cognitive complexity

---

## B.2 — Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001)

[Framework:: Blooms-Taxonomy-Revised]
[Revisers:: Lorin Anderson & David Krathwohl]
[Year:: 2001]
[Critical-Change:: Two-dimensional structure]

**Significance:** The revision is not cosmetic. It introduces a **second dimension** (the Knowledge Dimension) crossed with the original Cognitive Process Dimension, producing a 4 × 6 matrix that enables far more precise specification of learning objectives.

### Change 1: Cognitive Process Dimension — Renamed and Reordered

| Original (1956) | Revised (2001) | Change Note |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | **Remember** | Renamed as verb |
| Comprehension | **Understand** | Renamed as verb |
| Application | **Apply** | Renamed as verb |
| Analysis | **Analyze** | Renamed as verb |
| Synthesis | **Evaluate** | **Reordered** — moved below Create |
| Evaluation | **Create** | **Reordered + renamed** — now the highest level |

> [!key-claim] Why Create Was Elevated Above Evaluate
> The reversal reflects the judgment that creative synthesis — building something new from disparate elements — is cognitively more demanding than evaluation of existing material. Evaluation requires applying criteria to extant work; creation requires generating novel structure that doesn't yet exist.

### Change 2: The Knowledge Dimension

| Knowledge Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Factual** | Discrete elements of information | Terminology, specific details |
| **Conceptual** | Interrelationships among elements within a larger structure | Categories, principles, theories, models |
| **Procedural** | How to do something | Algorithms, techniques, methods |
| **Metacognitive** | Knowledge of cognition in general and one's own cognition | Self-knowledge, strategic knowledge |

### The Two-Dimensional Matrix

| Knowledge ↓ \ Process → | Remember | Understand | Apply | Analyze | Evaluate | Create |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Factual** | Recall facts | Explain facts | Apply facts | Compare facts | Judge facts | Generate facts |
| **Conceptual** | Recall concepts | Explain concepts | Apply concepts | Analyze concepts | Evaluate concepts | Create new concepts |
| **Procedural** | Recall procedures | Explain procedures | Execute procedures | Analyze procedures | Critique procedures | Design new procedures |
| **Metacognitive** | Recall strategies | Explain own thinking | Apply self-knowledge | Analyze own thinking | Evaluate own learning | Plan novel strategies |

> [!example] Practical Use
> A learning objective like "Students will understand photosynthesis" is ambiguous on the original taxonomy. On the revised matrix, it can be specified precisely as "Understand × Conceptual" — and contrasted with "Apply × Procedural" (use the photosynthesis equation to solve a problem) or "Create × Conceptual" (design a novel model of photosynthesis-like processes).

## B.3 — Webb's Depth of Knowledge (DOK)

[Framework:: Webbs-Depth-of-Knowledge]
[Originator:: Norman Webb]
[Year:: 1997]
[Primary-Use:: Assessment alignment]

**Core Commitment:** Unlike Bloom's taxonomy (which classifies cognitive *processes* by complexity), Webb's DOK classifies *the depth of thinking required* by a task. The distinction matters because the same Bloom-level verb can describe tasks of vastly different cognitive demand.

### The Four DOK Levels

| Level | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Recall and Reproduction** | Recall facts, definitions, simple procedures; one-step problems |
| **2** | **Skills and Concepts** | Use information, conceptual knowledge; two-step problems; some decisions about how to approach |
| **3** | **Strategic Thinking** | Requires reasoning, planning, using evidence; multiple valid approaches; more abstract |
| **4** | **Extended Thinking** | Requires extended investigation; multi-step over time; complex synthesis; transfer across contexts |

> [!warning] The DOK Insight on Verb-Level Ambiguity
> Webb specifically critiques the assumption that verbs like "analyze" or "evaluate" reliably indicate cognitive demand. *Analyzing* a paragraph for a specific quote (DOK 1) is vastly less demanding than *analyzing* multiple texts to identify an emergent theme (DOK 3). DOK forces explicit attention to **task structure** rather than verb labels.

## B.4 — Marzano's New Taxonomy

[Framework:: Marzano-New-Taxonomy]
[Originator:: Robert Marzano & John Kendall]
[Year:: 2007]
[Distinctive-Feature:: Includes affective/self-system components]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Marzano critiques Bloom for treating cognition as purely intellectual. His New Taxonomy explicitly incorporates the **Self-System** (motivational/affective factors that determine whether a learner engages with a task at all) and the **Metacognitive System** (the executive functions that govern learning).

### The Three Systems

| System | Function | Components |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-System** | Decides whether to engage with task | Importance, efficacy, emotional response, overall motivation |
| **Metacognitive System** | Sets goals and monitors progress | Specifying goals, process monitoring, monitoring clarity, monitoring accuracy |
| **Cognitive System** | Processes information | Retrieval, Comprehension, Analysis, Knowledge Utilization |

### The Six Levels of Processing (Cognitive System Expanded)

| Level | Process | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Retrieval** | Recognizing, recalling, executing |
| **2** | **Comprehension** | Integrating, symbolizing |
| **3** | **Analysis** | Matching, classifying, analyzing errors, generalizing, specifying |
| **4** | **Knowledge Utilization** | Decision making, problem solving, experimenting, investigating |
| **5** | **Metacognition** | Specifying goals, process monitoring, monitoring clarity/accuracy |
| **6** | **Self-System Thinking** | Examining importance, efficacy, emotional response, motivation |

**Significance:** Marzano's framework is uniquely valuable for designing instruction that addresses **why students don't engage** — a question Bloom-style taxonomies cannot ask because they assume engagement.

## B.5 — SOLO Taxonomy (Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes)

[Framework:: SOLO-Taxonomy]
[Originators:: John Biggs & Kevin Collis]
[Year:: 1982]
[Distinctive-Feature:: Classifies learner *responses* rather than cognitive processes]

**Distinctive Commitment:** SOLO classifies the **structural complexity** of a learner's response or understanding — not the cognitive process used to produce it. This shift makes SOLO especially useful for diagnosing what level of understanding a learner has actually achieved.

### The Five SOLO Levels

| Level | Name | Structural Description | Marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Pre-structural** | No understanding; irrelevant or tautological response | Missing the point |
| **2** | **Uni-structural** | One relevant element identified | "It is one thing" |
| **3** | **Multi-structural** | Several relevant elements identified but unconnected | "It is several things" — list-like |
| **4** | **Relational** | Multiple elements connected into a coherent whole | "It is a system" — relationships understood |
| **5** | **Extended Abstract** | Generalizes beyond given context; abstracts to new domains | "It generalizes to other cases" |

> [!example] SOLO in Diagnosis
> A student asked to explain ecosystem dynamics:
> - **Pre-structural:** "Ecosystems are nature." (no relevant content)
> - **Uni-structural:** "Predators eat prey." (one true thing)
> - **Multi-structural:** "There are predators, prey, plants, decomposers, and weather." (list, no connections)
> - **Relational:** "Predators control prey populations, which affects plant abundance, which affects decomposers and the whole nutrient cycle." (system of relationships)
> - **Extended Abstract:** "The principles of ecosystem dynamics — feedback loops, trophic cascades, carrying capacity — apply to economic systems, social networks, and organizational behavior." (transfers abstract structure)

# Section C — Skills-Based Frameworks

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> Skills-based frameworks decompose critical thinking into a discrete set of cognitive abilities that can be taught, practiced, and assessed independently. They serve assessment design and curriculum development.

---

## C.1 — Watson-Glaser Critical Thinking Appraisal

[Framework:: Watson-Glaser]
[Originators:: Goodwin Watson & Edward Glaser]
[Year-Original:: 1925]
[Status:: Most widely used CT assessment, particularly in employment]

**Origin:** One of the oldest standardized critical thinking assessments, developed in 1925 and repeatedly revised. Used extensively in hiring (especially legal/professional roles) and in research.

### The Five Subtests

| Subtest | What It Measures |
|---|---|
| **1. Inference** | Distinguishing between true, probably true, insufficient data, probably false, and false inferences from given data |
| **2. Recognition of Assumptions** | Identifying unstated presumptions underlying a statement |
| **3. Deduction** | Determining whether conclusions necessarily follow from given premises |
| **4. Interpretation** | Weighing evidence and deciding if generalizations or conclusions are warranted |
| **5. Evaluation of Arguments** | Distinguishing strong arguments (relevant + important) from weak ones (irrelevant, trivial, or based on emotion) |

> [!key-claim] The RED Model (Watson-Glaser II)
> The revised Watson-Glaser organizes the five subtests under three macro-skills: **R**ecognize Assumptions, **E**valuate Arguments, **D**raw Conclusions. This **RED Model** is the most compact summary of the framework's commitments.

# Section D — Dispositional Frameworks

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> Dispositional frameworks address what skills-based frameworks cannot: *whether* and *when* a person will actually deploy their critical thinking abilities. Dispositions are the motivational/characterological foundation of critical thought.

---

## D.1 — California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CCTDI)

[Framework:: CCTDI]
[Originator:: Peter Facione & Noreen Facione]
[Year:: 1992]
[Form:: 75-item self-report inventory]
[Theoretical-Basis:: Delphi disposition findings (Section A.2)]

**Purpose:** Operationalizes the Delphi Report's disposition findings into a measurable instrument. Used in research and educational assessment.

### The Seven CCTDI Subscales

| # | Disposition | Core Question |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Truth-seeking** | Does the person actively seek truth, even uncomfortable truth? |
| **2** | **Open-mindedness** | Is the person tolerant of divergent views and willing to consider them? |
| **3** | **Analyticity** | Does the person value reasoned analysis and anticipate consequences? |
| **4** | **Systematicity** | Is the person organized, focused, and diligent in inquiry? |
| **5** | **Self-confidence** | Does the person trust their own reasoned judgment? |
| **6** | **Inquisitiveness** | Is the person intellectually curious across many domains? |
| **7** | **Maturity** | Does the person approach problems with prudent judgment about complexity? |

> [!example] Practical Insight
> Research using the CCTDI consistently shows that **disposition predicts skill deployment better than skill predicts disposition use**. A person with strong skills but weak disposition will reason well only when externally prompted; a person with strong disposition will continue developing skills throughout life because they value the activity.

## D.3 — Costa's Habits of Mind

[Framework:: Costas-Habits-of-Mind]
[Originator:: Arthur Costa & Bena Kallick]
[Year:: 2000]
[Number-of-Habits:: 16]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Costa's framework explicitly extends beyond cognition to include affective and behavioral habits. The "Habits of Mind" are *characteristic behavioral patterns* that intelligent people exhibit when facing problems whose answers are not immediately known.

### The 16 Habits of Mind

| # | Habit | Core Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Persisting | Sticking with tasks; not giving up |
| 2 | Managing Impulsivity | Thinking before acting |
| 3 | Listening with Understanding and Empathy | Devoting energy to understanding others |
| 4 | Thinking Flexibly | Changing perspectives, generating alternatives |
| 5 | Thinking About Thinking ([[Metacognition]]) | Awareness of one's own thought processes |
| 6 | Striving for Accuracy | Setting high standards; reviewing work |
| 7 | Questioning and Posing Problems | Asking generative questions |
| 8 | Applying Past Knowledge to New Situations | Transferring learning |
| 9 | Thinking and Communicating with Clarity and Precision | Precise language, careful expression |
| 10 | Gathering Data Through All Senses | Multi-modal observation |
| 11 | Creating, Imagining, Innovating | Generating novel ideas |
| 12 | Responding with Wonderment and Awe | Cultivating curiosity and appreciation |
| 13 | Taking Responsible Risks | Venturing beyond familiar territory |
| 14 | Finding Humor | Maintaining playfulness amid difficulty |
| 15 | Thinking Interdependently | Working with others productively |
| 16 | Remaining Open to Continuous Learning | Lifelong learning orientation |

> [!key-claim] Why Costa's Habits Endure in Education
> Costa's framework is the most widely adopted dispositional framework in K-12 education because it is *behaviorally observable*. Unlike abstract dispositions, "persisting" or "managing impulsivity" can be seen, named, and reinforced in classroom practice.

# Section E — Argument Analysis Frameworks

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> These frameworks focus narrowly on the *structure of arguments* — providing analytical tools for diagramming, evaluating, and constructing reasoned discourse.

---

## E.1 — Toulmin's Argument Model (1958)

[Framework:: Toulmin-Argument-Model]
[Originator:: Stephen Toulmin]
[Key-Work:: *The Uses of Argument* (1958)]
[Significance:: The dominant framework for analyzing real-world argument structure]

**Origin:** Toulmin developed his model as a *critique* of formal logic's adequacy for representing everyday reasoning. He argued that real arguments have more components — and more nuance — than the syllogistic tradition acknowledged.

### The Six Components of the Toulmin Model

| Component | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | The conclusion being argued for | "Harry is a British subject." |
| **Data (Grounds)** | The facts/evidence supporting the claim | "Harry was born in Bermuda." |
| **Warrant** | The reasoning principle linking data to claim | "Anyone born in Bermuda is a British subject." |
| **Backing** | The support behind the warrant itself | "The British Nationality Acts establish this." |
| **Qualifier** | The degree of certainty | "Presumably" / "Almost certainly" / "Probably" |
| **Rebuttal** | Conditions under which the claim might not hold | "Unless his parents renounced citizenship before his birth." |

### Visual Structure

```
                                  Qualifier
                                     |
   Data ─────────────────────────→ Claim
            (so, presumably)        |
              |                  Rebuttal
              |                  (unless...)
            Warrant
       (since, because)
              |
            Backing
        (on account of)
```

> [!example] Why Warrants Are the Hidden Key
> Most everyday arguments make the **claim** and provide **data** explicitly but leave the **warrant** implicit. The warrant is what *makes the data relevant to the claim* — and it is most often where the disagreement actually lies. Surfacing warrants is one of the highest-leverage moves in argument analysis.

---

## E.2 — Browne & Keeley's 10 Critical Questions

[Framework:: Browne-Keeley-10-Questions]
[Originators:: M. Neil Browne & Stuart Keeley]
[Key-Work:: *Asking the Right Questions* (multiple editions)]
[Approach:: Question-based critical reading]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Critical thinking is a *questioning practice* — and effective questioners deploy a small, well-ordered set of questions repeatedly. The framework operationalizes critical thinking as the disciplined sequential application of ten questions to any argument.

### The 10 Questions

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What are the issue and the conclusion? |
| 2 | What are the reasons? |
| 3 | Which words or phrases are ambiguous? |
| 4 | What are the value conflicts and assumptions? |
| 5 | What are the descriptive assumptions? |
| 6 | Are there any fallacies in the reasoning? |
| 7 | How good is the evidence? |
| 8 | Are there rival causes? |
| 9 | Are the statistics deceptive? |
| 10 | What significant information is omitted? |
| (11) | What reasonable conclusions are possible? *(added in later editions)* |

> [!example] Why This Framework Is Pedagogically Powerful
> Browne & Keeley's framework can be taught in a few hours and applied immediately. Its sequential structure provides scaffolding for beginners while its repeatable application produces deep skill through deliberate practice.

---

## E.3 — Walton's Argumentation Schemes

[Framework:: Walton-Argumentation-Schemes]
[Originator:: Douglas Walton]
[Approach:: Catalog of common reasoning patterns + critical questions for each]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Rather than treating arguments as instances of formal logic, Walton developed a catalog of **argumentation schemes** — recurring patterns of presumptive reasoning that are not deductively valid but are appropriate in specific contexts. Each scheme comes with a set of **critical questions** that probe its weaknesses.

### Selected Major Argumentation Schemes

| Scheme | Pattern | Sample Critical Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Argument from Expert Opinion** | E is an expert in domain D; E asserts A; therefore A | Is E truly an expert? Is A within E's domain? |
| **Argument from Analogy** | C1 is similar to C2; C1 has property P; therefore C2 has P | Are the cases relevantly similar? |
| **Argument from Cause to Effect** | A causes B; A occurred; therefore B will occur | Is the causal link reliable? Are there interfering factors? |
| **Argument from Sign** | A is correlated with B; A is observed; therefore B | Is the correlation reliable? |
| **Argument from Popular Opinion** | Most people accept A; therefore A | Is the opinion well-informed? Is popularity evidence here? |
| **Argument from Consequences** | A leads to consequence C; C is bad; therefore reject A | Are the consequences correctly identified? |

> [!key-claim] Walton's Innovation
> Walton's framework recognizes that **presumptive reasoning** — reasoning that establishes provisional acceptance subject to challenge — is the norm in everyday discourse. Formal validity is the wrong standard for most real arguments; the right standard is *appropriate use of presumption-shifting schemes plus willingness to address critical questions*.

# Section F — Developmental Models

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> Developmental models describe how reasoning *changes over time* — through education, life experience, and intellectual maturation. They identify qualitatively distinct stages or positions rather than continuous skill growth.

---

## F.1 — Perry's Scheme of Intellectual and Ethical Development

[Framework:: Perrys-Scheme]
[Originator:: William G. Perry Jr.]
[Year:: 1970]
[Original-Population:: Harvard undergraduates]
[Stages:: 9 positions in 4 broad phases]

**Origin:** Based on longitudinal interviews with Harvard students, Perry identified a developmental sequence in how learners think about knowledge, authority, and ethical commitments.

### The Four Broad Phases

| Phase | Positions | Core Epistemology |
|---|---|---|
| **Dualism** | 1-2 | Knowledge is right/wrong; authorities have the right answers |
| **Multiplicity** | 3-4 | When authorities disagree, all opinions are equally valid |
| **Relativism** | 5-6 | Knowledge is contextual; some views are better-supported than others |
| **Commitment in Relativism** | 7-9 | Commitment to specific values/views within a relativist framework |

### Detailed Position Map

| Position | Description |
|---|---|
| **1** | Basic Duality — All knowledge is right/wrong; authority knows truth |
| **2** | Multiplicity Pre-legitimate — Disagreement is poor teaching, not real |
| **3** | Multiplicity Subordinate — Some uncertainty exists but is temporary |
| **4** | Multiplicity Coordinate — Disagreement is legitimate; all opinions equal |
| **5** | Contextual Relativism — Knowledge is contextual; evaluation possible |
| **6** | Commitment Foreseen — Recognizes need for personal commitment |
| **7** | Initial Commitment — Makes specific commitments |
| **8** | Orientation in Commitment — Develops style and content of commitments |
| **9** | Developing Commitment — Affirms identity through committed engagement |

> [!example] Implications for Critical Thinking Instruction
> A student at Position 2 cannot productively engage with arguments for multiple positions — they will interpret such instruction as either revealing the "real" right answer or as deficient teaching. Critical thinking instruction must meet learners where they are developmentally; the same lesson lands differently at each position.

---

## F.2 — King & Kitchener's Reflective Judgment Model

[Framework:: Reflective-Judgment-Model]
[Originators:: Patricia King & Karen Kitchener]
[Year:: 1994]
[Stages:: 7 stages in 3 levels]
[Distinctive-Feature:: Focused on reasoning about ill-structured problems]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Where Perry focused on global epistemic development, King and Kitchener focused specifically on how people reason about **ill-structured problems** — problems where the data is insufficient to produce certain answers, where experts disagree, and where judgment is required.

### The Three Levels and Seven Stages

**Pre-Reflective Thinking (Stages 1-3):** Knowledge is assumed to be certain or temporarily uncertain; beliefs need no justification beyond the authority that asserted them.

| Stage | Core Belief |
|---|---|
| **1** | Knowledge is absolutely certain; observation guarantees truth |
| **2** | Knowledge is certain or temporarily uncertain; authorities know the truth |
| **3** | Knowledge is sometimes uncertain; in those areas, opinion is sufficient |

**Quasi-Reflective Thinking (Stages 4-5):** Knowledge is uncertain; reasoning is required but is treated as idiosyncratic — each person's reasoning is valid for them.

| Stage | Core Belief |
|---|---|
| **4** | Knowledge is uncertain because data is always incomplete; we must just choose what to believe |
| **5** | Knowledge is contextual and subjective; conclusions are filtered through individual perspective |

**Reflective Thinking (Stages 6-7):** Knowledge claims must be evaluated and justified. The justification process is itself recognized and developed.

| Stage | Core Belief |
|---|---|
| **6** | Knowledge is constructed through evaluation of evidence and reasoning; some interpretations are better-justified than others |
| **7** | Knowledge is the outcome of inquiry; the most defensible conclusions are those most supported by reasoned argument from evidence |

> [!key-claim] The Empirical Finding
> Decades of research using the Reflective Judgment Interview show that **most undergraduates score in the quasi-reflective range (stages 4-5)** — they recognize uncertainty but lack a model for adjudicating between competing claims under uncertainty. This is the precise developmental gap that critical thinking instruction must address.

---

## F.3 — Kuhn's Levels of Epistemological Understanding

[Framework:: Kuhn-Epistemological-Levels]
[Originator:: Deanna Kuhn]
[Year:: 1991, 1999]
[Stages:: 4 levels]

**Distinctive Commitment:** Kuhn's developmental framework is more compact than Perry's or King & Kitchener's but captures the same essential trajectory. It identifies four qualitatively distinct epistemological orientations.

### The Four Levels

| Level | Name | Core Position | What Counts as Knowledge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Realist** | Knowledge mirrors external reality | Direct correspondence with the world |
| **2** | **Absolutist** | Knowledge is certain; experts have it | Authoritative pronouncement |
| **3** | **Multiplist** | All claims are opinion; certainty is impossible | Personal viewpoint |
| **4** | **Evaluativist** | Claims can be evaluated by reasoned argument | Best-supported view among alternatives |

> [!example] The Critical Transition
> Movement from **Multiplist** to **Evaluativist** is the developmental shift most directly relevant to critical thinking. Multiplists believe everyone has equally valid opinions; Evaluativists believe opinions can be reasoned about, with some being better-supported than others. The skills of argument analysis are precisely the tools that the Evaluativist deploys.

# Section G — Dual-Process Theory & Cognitive Architecture

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> This section addresses the *cognitive architecture* underlying critical thinking — the dual-system model that has become foundational in cognitive psychology and behavioral economics.

---

## G.1 — Dual-Process Theory (System 1 / System 2)

[Framework:: Dual-Process-Theory]
[Major-Theorists:: Daniel Kahneman, Keith Stanovich, Jonathan Evans, Shane Frederick]
[Popular-Articulation:: Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011)]

**Core Architecture:** The human cognitive system contains two distinct modes of processing:

| Dimension | System 1 | System 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | Fast (automatic) | Slow (deliberate) |
| **Effort** | Effortless | Effortful |
| **Control** | Implicit, unconscious | Explicit, conscious |
| **Capacity** | Massive parallel processing | Limited, serial |
| **Operation** | Heuristic, associative | Algorithmic, rule-based |
| **Output** | Intuitions, impressions | Considered judgments |
| **Errors** | Systematic biases | Logical failures from inadequate engagement |
| **Trainability** | Slow; via repeated exposure | Faster; via instruction and practice |

### Implications for Critical Thinking

> [!key-claim] The Dual-Process Insight
> Critical thinking is fundamentally a **System 2 activity** — but System 2 is slow, effortful, and easily fatigued. System 1 generates intuitive responses to almost everything, often before System 2 has the chance to engage. The critical thinking question is therefore not just "how do we reason well?" but "**how do we recognize when System 1's output should be overridden by System 2's analysis?**"

**The Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)** — Shane Frederick's three-item test — operationalizes this. Each item triggers an intuitive (System 1) wrong answer that requires System 2 override to correct. Example:

> *A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?*
>
> System 1's answer: $0.10. Correct answer: $0.05.

### Stanovich's Refinement — The Tripartite Model

Keith Stanovich proposed splitting System 2 into two sub-components:

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| **Autonomous Mind (System 1)** | Fast, automatic, heuristic processing |
| **Algorithmic Mind (System 2a)** | Computational power for explicit reasoning |
| **Reflective Mind (System 2b)** | Dispositional engagement — the *will* to deploy algorithmic capacity |

> [!example] Why Smart People Reason Poorly
> Stanovich's tripartite model explains a persistent finding: high IQ does not guarantee good critical thinking. The Algorithmic Mind provides the *capacity*; the Reflective Mind provides the *deployment*. A person with high IQ but weak reflective disposition will fail at critical thinking tasks despite possessing the cognitive horsepower to succeed.

---

## G.2 — The Cognitive Biases Landscape

[Framework:: Cognitive-Biases]
[Foundational-Work:: Tversky & Kahneman, 1974]

Critical thinking instruction increasingly incorporates explicit study of cognitive biases — the systematic departures from rationality that arise from System 1 heuristics.

### Selected High-Impact Biases for Critical Thinking

| Bias | Description | Counter-strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation Bias** | Seeking/interpreting information to confirm prior beliefs | Actively seek disconfirming evidence |
| **Availability Heuristic** | Judging probability by ease of recall | Use base rates, not memorable cases |
| **Anchoring** | Over-relying on first information encountered | Generate independent estimates first |
| **Representativeness** | Judging by similarity to stereotype, ignoring base rates | Apply Bayesian reasoning explicitly |
| **Hindsight Bias** | Believing past events were predictable in retrospect | Record predictions before outcomes are known |
| **Fundamental Attribution Error** | Over-attributing others' behavior to character vs. context | Consider situational factors equally |
| **Dunning-Kruger Effect** | Incompetent overestimate competence; competent underestimate | Calibrate via external feedback |
| **Motivated Reasoning** | Reasoning toward preferred conclusions | Surface motivations explicitly |

# Section H — Community and Dialogical Models

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> Some frameworks treat critical thinking as fundamentally *social* — as a practice that emerges from and depends upon dialogical community rather than individual cognition.

---

## H.1 — Lipman's Community of Inquiry

[Framework:: Community-of-Inquiry]
[Originator:: Matthew Lipman]
[Year:: 1991, 2003]
[Associated-Program:: Philosophy for Children (P4C)]

**Core Commitment:** Critical thinking is best developed in a **community of inquiry** — a group of people who, through structured dialogue, jointly examine questions, build on each other's contributions, and self-correct as a collective. The community is the unit of thinking, not the individual.

### Lipman's Three Dimensions of Thinking

Lipman uniquely insists that critical thinking is *one of three* essential thinking modes:

| Dimension | Focus | Question Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical Thinking** | Truth, justification, reasoning | "Is this so? On what grounds?" |
| **Creative Thinking** | Possibility, novelty, generation | "What else could this be? What might be?" |
| **Caring Thinking** | Values, empathy, what matters | "Why should this matter? Who is affected?" |

The integrated thinker — what Lipman calls the **multidimensional thinker** — exhibits all three modes in fluid coordination.

### Characteristics of a Community of Inquiry

- Participants build on each other's ideas (cumulative dialogue)
- Disagreement is welcomed as productive
- Reasoning is made explicit and public
- The community self-corrects collectively
- Questions are open-ended and contestable
- The teacher facilitates but does not adjudicate

> [!key-claim] Lipman's Critique of Individualistic CT
> Lipman argues that conceiving of critical thinking as a *property of individuals* misses its essentially dialogical nature. Real critical thinking happens *between* minds — in the give-and-take of reasoned conversation where ideas are offered, challenged, refined, and sometimes abandoned.

---

## H.2 — Brookfield's Critical Thinking

[Framework:: Brookfield-Critical-Thinking]
[Originator:: Stephen Brookfield]
[Key-Work:: *Developing Critical Thinkers* (1987), *Teaching for Critical Thinking* (2012)]
[Distinctive-Focus:: Assumption identification + adult learning]

**Core Commitment:** Critical thinking is fundamentally about **hunting assumptions** — particularly assumptions about power, context, and one's own framing. Brookfield's framework is heavily influenced by [[Critical Theory]] and adult learning theory.

### Brookfield's Four Activities of Critical Thinking

| Activity | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Identifying Assumptions** | Surfacing the taken-for-granted beliefs that frame our thinking |
| **2. Checking Assumptions** | Examining whether those assumptions are accurate and valid in context |
| **3. Seeing Things from Different Viewpoints** | Considering how things look from other people's perspectives |
| **4. Taking Informed Action** | Acting on the basis of well-grounded reasoning |

### Brookfield's Three Types of Assumptions

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Paradigmatic** | Most basic; structuring our entire worldview | "Knowledge is objective and discoverable" |
| **Prescriptive** | About what *should* happen | "Good teachers explain clearly" |
| **Causal** | About cause-effect relationships | "Discussion produces deeper learning than lecture" |

> [!example] Why Paradigmatic Assumptions Are the Hardest
> Paradigmatic assumptions are the most difficult to surface because they constitute the very framework within which we recognize the world. They are typically discovered only when violated — when we encounter someone operating from an entirely different paradigm.

# Section I — Knowledge Hierarchies

> [!abstract] Section Scope
> Knowledge hierarchies organize different *kinds* of cognitive products in relation to each other — distinguishing data from information from knowledge from wisdom.

---

## I.1 — The DIKW Pyramid

[Framework:: DIKW-Pyramid]
[Origin:: Russell Ackoff, 1989; T.S. Eliot's poetic precursor]
[Domains:: Information science, knowledge management]

**Core Architecture:** A four-level hierarchy from raw signals to integrated wisdom.

```
                    ▲
                   ╱ ╲
                  ╱ W ╲           Wisdom
                 ╱─────╲          (Why to act)
                ╱   K   ╲         Knowledge
               ╱─────────╲        (How — context, meaning)
              ╱     I     ╲       Information
             ╱─────────────╲      (What — structured data)
            ╱       D       ╲     Data
           ╱─────────────────╲    (Raw signals)
          ─────────────────────
```

| Level | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Raw, unprocessed facts/signals | "37.5°C" |
| **Information** | Data with context/meaning | "Patient's temperature is 37.5°C" |
| **Knowledge** | Information integrated with understanding | "37.5°C is slightly elevated; suggests possible infection" |
| **Wisdom** | Knowledge applied with judgment about what should be done | "Given patient's history, monitor for 24h before treating" |

> [!key-claim] Where Critical Thinking Lives in DIKW
> Critical thinking is most active at the **transitions** between levels: turning data into information requires interpretation; turning information into knowledge requires integration; turning knowledge into wisdom requires judgment. Each transition involves cognitive moves that can be done well or poorly — and that is where CT skills operate.

---

## I.2 — Bloom's Affective Domain (1964)

[Framework:: Blooms-Affective-Domain]
[Originator:: Krathwohl, Bloom & Masia]
[Year:: 1964]
[Companion-To:: Bloom's Cognitive Taxonomy]

**Significance:** Often forgotten alongside the famous cognitive taxonomy, the Affective Domain addresses how learners develop values, attitudes, and dispositions — directly relevant to critical thinking's dispositional dimension.

### The Five Affective Levels

| Level | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Receiving** | Awareness; willingness to attend to phenomena |
| **2** | **Responding** | Active participation; reacting in some way |
| **3** | **Valuing** | Attaching worth; consistent behavior reflecting value |
| **4** | **Organizing** | Building a value system; reconciling values into a structure |
| **5** | **Characterizing** | The value system becomes a worldview governing behavior |

> [!example] Why This Matters for Critical Thinking
> Movement from "knowing about" intellectual humility (cognitive) to *embodying* it (affective level 5) requires traversing the affective domain. The most sophisticated CT instruction recognizes that disposition development is *affective development* and follows different developmental laws than skill development.

## Synthesis — The Integrated Critical Thinker Profile

> [!key-claim] What These Frameworks Together Describe
> Combining the convergent commitments across all frameworks, the integrated critical thinker is someone who:
>
> 1. **Understands argument structure** (Toulmin, Browne & Keeley)
> 2. **Deploys core analytical skills** (Delphi, Ennis, Halpern, Watson-Glaser)
> 3. **Holds productive dispositions** (Facione, Ennis, Costa, Paul-Elder)
> 4. **Recognizes their own cognitive architecture** (Dual-Process, Stanovich)
> 5. **Has developed past multiplism into evaluativism** (Kuhn, King & Kitchener, Perry)
> 6. **Surfaces and examines assumptions** (Brookfield, Paul-Elder)
> 7. **Engages dialogically with other minds** (Lipman)
> 8. **Maintains substantive knowledge in their domains** (Bailin, McPeck)
> 9. **Operates across cognitive complexity levels** (Bloom, Marzano, SOLO)
> 10. **Integrates skills with values and self-regulation** (Delphi, Costa, Affective Domain)
