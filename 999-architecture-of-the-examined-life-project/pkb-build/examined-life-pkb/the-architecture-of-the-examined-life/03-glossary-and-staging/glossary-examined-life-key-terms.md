## A

### Actively Open-Minded Thinking (AOT)
**Definition:** A measured disposition to consider alternative viewpoints, revise beliefs in light of new evidence, and resist premature closure. Developed by Keith Stanovich as a psychometric construct that predicts rational thinking performance beyond general intelligence.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Fallibilism]], [[#Aporic Receptivity]], [[#Intellectual Humility]], [[#Growth Mindset]]

**Everyday example:** When your teenager presents an argument for why their curfew should be extended, AOT is the difference between immediately dismissing it ("We've already decided") versus genuinely considering whether their reasons might be valid, even if you ultimately maintain the boundary.

---

### Aporia
**Definition:** (Greek: ἀπορία, literally "without a path") The state of intellectual impasse produced by rigorous questioning, where you recognize that your confident belief was unjustified but don't yet have a replacement. Distinguished from simple ignorance by its productive character—aporia activates genuine inquiry rather than terminating it.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Elenchus]], [[#Felt Difficulty]], [[#Socratic Method]], [[#Epistemic Humility]]

**Everyday example:** You've always believed that success requires "working harder than everyone else." A friend gently points out three counter-examples from your own life—times when working smarter, or collaborating better, produced better results than pure effort. For a moment, you can't defend your original belief, but you also don't have a new principle to replace it. That uncomfortable suspension between beliefs is aporia—and it's exactly where learning begins.

---

### Aporic Receptivity
**Definition:** The cultivated disposition to welcome, dwell productively in, and act from states of productive uncertainty rather than reflexively avoiding or suppressing them. The meta-disposition prerequisite to all genuine inquiry.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]] (emergent synthesis)

**Related concepts:** [[#Aporia]], [[#Felt Difficulty]], [[#Intellectual Humility]], [[#Tolerating Ambiguity]]

**Everyday example:** A colleague challenges your approach to a project you've been confident about. Aporic receptivity is the ability to sit with that unsettled feeling without immediately defending yourself—to genuinely examine whether they might be seeing something you've missed, even though it's uncomfortable to question your own judgment.

---

## B

### Bayesian Reasoning
**Definition:** A framework for updating beliefs based on new evidence by calculating how likely the evidence would be under different hypotheses. Named after Thomas Bayes, this approach treats beliefs as probabilities that should be revised as data accumulates.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]], [[report-13-the-predictive-mind|Report 13: The Predictive Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Prediction Error]], [[#Belief Updating]], [[#Active Inference]], [[#Fallibilism]]

**Everyday example:** You hear your teenager come home late. Your initial worry (hypothesis: "they're in trouble") competes with alternative explanations. When you smell pizza and hear laughter, you update your belief—the evidence (pizza, laughter) is more likely under "stopped for food with friends" than "in trouble." You've just done intuitive Bayesian reasoning.

## C

### Cognitive Load
**Definition:** The total mental effort being used in working memory during information processing. Includes intrinsic load (inherent difficulty of material), extraneous load (imposed by poor instruction), and germane load (productive effort toward learning).

**Introduced in:** [[report-04-cognitive-scaffolding|Report 04: Cognitive Scaffolding]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Working Memory]], [[#Zone of Proximal Development]], [[#Scaffolding]], [[#Schema]]

**Everyday example:** When you're learning to drive, steering + watching mirrors + monitoring speed + remembering traffic rules creates cognitive overload. An instructor reduces extraneous load by first practicing in an empty parking lot (simpler environment), allowing you to focus germane load on building the skill itself.

---

### Confirmation Bias
**Definition:** The tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in ways that confirm preexisting beliefs, while giving less consideration to alternative possibilities or contradictory evidence.

**Introduced in:** [[report-05-the-virtuous-reasoner|Report 05: The Virtuous Reasoner]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#System 1]], [[#Motivated Reasoning]], [[#Intellectual Curiosity]], [[#Actively Open-Minded Thinking]]

**Everyday example:** You believe your preferred news source is "more objective" than others. When presented with bias ratings from independent researchers, you scrutinize the methodology when it contradicts your belief, but accept similar methodology uncritically when it supports your view. That asymmetry is confirmation bias at work.

---

### Constructivism
**Definition:** The learning theory that knowledge is actively constructed by learners through interaction with their environment, rather than passively received. Associated with Piaget, Vygotsky, and Dewey, emphasizing that understanding emerges through the reorganization of existing mental structures.

**Introduced in:** [[report-02-the-fallible-knower|Report 02: The Fallible Knower]], [[report-07-the-self-directed-learner|Report 07: The Self-Directed Learner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Schema]], [[#Accommodation]], [[#Assimilation]], [[#Zone of Proximal Development]]

**Everyday example:** A child believes "all dogs are friendly" (existing schema). They encounter a dog that growls and snaps. They can't just add this to their existing belief—they must reconstruct their understanding to something like "most dogs are friendly, but some are scared or protective." That reconstruction is constructivism in action.

---

## D

### Dual Process Theory
**Definition:** The framework proposing two distinct systems of thinking: System 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive, effortless) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical, effortful). Popularized by Daniel Kahneman, though variations exist across cognitive science.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#System 1]], [[#System 2]], [[#Metacognitive Monitoring]], [[#Heuristics and Biases]]

**Everyday example:** You're driving a familiar route (System 1 handles it automatically while you chat). Suddenly a pedestrian steps into the crosswalk—System 2 activates as you consciously decide to brake and check mirrors before maneuvering.

---

## E

### Elenchus
**Definition:** (Greek: ἔλεγχος) The Socratic procedure of cross-examination: eliciting someone's belief, securing agreement to additional premises they find plausible, then demonstrating that those premises contradict the original belief. Aims at exposing internal inconsistency rather than teaching correct answers.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Socratic Method]], [[#Aporia]], [[#Dialectic]], [[#Critical Thinking]]

**Everyday example:** Your friend insists "all generalizations are false." You ask: "Is that statement itself a generalization?" They say yes. "So by your own principle, it's false?" The contradiction forces them to reconsider—that's elenchus.

---

### Embodied Cognition
**Definition:** The theoretical perspective that cognition is inseparable from bodily states and sensorimotor processes. Thinking isn't just "in the head"—it's constituted by the body's interactions with its environment. Part of the "4E" framework: Embodied, Embedded, Enacted, Extended.

**Introduced in:** [[report-09-the-embodied-thinker|Report 09: The Embodied Thinker]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Interoception]], [[#Somatic Markers]], [[#Enacted Cognition]], [[#Extended Mind]]

**Everyday example:** You're writing an email and feeling stuck. You stand up, pace, and suddenly the words come. That's not coincidence—movement changes your cognitive state. Walking literally helps you think differently because your cognition is embodied.

---

### Epistemic Humility
**Definition:** The recognition of the limits of one's knowledge and the fallibility of one's beliefs. Distinguished from general humility by its specifically intellectual focus and from low confidence by its compatibility with strong beliefs held fallibly.

**Introduced in:** [[report-02-the-fallible-knower|Report 02: The Fallible Knower]], [[report-05-the-virtuous-reasoner|Report 05: The Virtuous Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Fallibilism]], [[#Intellectual Humility]], [[#Socratic Wisdom]], [[#Dunning-Kruger Effect]]

**Everyday example:** An experienced doctor says "Based on these symptoms, I'm 85% confident it's X, but we should rule out Y and Z." That calibrated confidence—strong enough to act on, humble enough to remain open to revision—is epistemic humility.

---

### Eudaimonia
**Definition:** (Greek: εὐδαιμονία) Often translated as "flourishing" or "the good life," this Aristotelian concept refers to living in accordance with virtue and realizing one's potential. Distinguished from hedonic pleasure by emphasizing meaning, growth, and excellence over mere satisfaction.

**Introduced in:** [[report-14-the-meaningful-life|Report 14: The Meaningful Life]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Virtue Ethics]], [[#Meaning]], [[#Self-Actualization]], [[#Intrinsic Motivation]]

**Everyday example:** After a day spent helping a friend move, doing creative work, and having a meaningful conversation with your partner, you feel exhausted but deeply satisfied. That's closer to eudaimonia than the pleasure of binge-watching TV—harder earned, but more nourishing to who you want to be.

---

## F

### Fallibilism
**Definition:** The epistemological position that all beliefs, no matter how well-justified, are potentially subject to revision in light of new evidence. Certainty is impossible in empirical matters; the best we can do is hold beliefs tentatively and remain open to correction.

**Introduced in:** [[report-02-the-fallible-knower|Report 02: The Fallible Knower]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Epistemic Humility]], [[#Scientific Method]], [[#Bayesian Reasoning]], [[#Growth Mindset]]

**Everyday example:** You're convinced your friend is angry based on their terse texts. When you see them in person, they're exhausted from a family crisis you didn't know about. Fallibilism is the recognition that your initial interpretation, though reasonable, was provisional—and remaining open to revision means you avoid conflict based on misunderstanding.

---

### Felt Difficulty
**Definition:** (Dewey) The organismic experience of encountering a situation that existing habits and expectations cannot handle smoothly—a lived sense of friction or interruption that triggers reflective thinking. Distinguished from abstract problems by its essentially embodied, affective character.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Aporia]], [[#Prediction Error]], [[#Reflective Thinking]], [[#Inquiry Activation]]

**Everyday example:** You reach for the light switch in your bedroom and the lights don't turn on. For a split second, there's a pause—a tiny friction where your automatic action was interrupted. That moment of "something's not right" before you consciously think "bulb must be out" is felt difficulty.

---

### Flow State
**Definition:** (Csikszentmihalyi) The experience of being completely absorbed in an optimally challenging activity, where skill level matches task difficulty. Characterized by loss of self-consciousness, distorted time perception, and intrinsic motivation.

**Introduced in:** [[report-03-the-motivated-mind|Report 03: The Motivated Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Intrinsic Motivation]], [[#Zone of Proximal Development]], [[#Optimal Challenge]], [[#Self-Determination Theory]]

**Everyday example:** You're playing a musical piece that's just hard enough to demand your full attention but not so hard that you're constantly stumbling. An hour passes like minutes. You're not thinking about performing well or impressing anyone—the activity itself is fully rewarding. That's flow.

---

## G

### Germane Load
**Definition:** (Cognitive Load Theory) The mental effort productively devoted to building schemas and gaining understanding—the "good" kind of cognitive load that produces learning. Distinguished from intrinsic load (inherent difficulty) and extraneous load (poor instructional design).

**Introduced in:** [[report-04-cognitive-scaffolding|Report 04: Cognitive Scaffolding]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Cognitive Load]], [[#Schema]], [[#Intrinsic Load]], [[#Extraneous Load]]

**Everyday example:** When learning to cook a new dish, reading the recipe requires cognitive effort. If the recipe is poorly written (extraneous load) you struggle with confusing instructions. If you're making puff pastry from scratch on day one (intrinsic overload), you're overwhelmed. But when a clear recipe guides you through a challenging-but-doable technique, your effort goes into actually learning to cook (germane load).

---

### Growth Mindset
**Definition:** (Dweck) The belief that abilities and intelligence can be developed through effort, learning, and persistence, as opposed to being fixed traits. Predicts greater resilience, learning orientation, and willingness to take on challenges.

**Introduced in:** [[report-02-the-fallible-knower|Report 02: The Fallible Knower]], [[report-03-the-motivated-mind|Report 03: The Motivated Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Fallibilism]], [[#Intrinsic Motivation]], [[#Self-Efficacy]], [[#Learning Orientation]]

**Everyday example:** Your child struggles with math homework and says "I'm just not a math person." Growth mindset reframes: "You haven't mastered this *yet*. Let's figure out what practice you need." The tiny word "yet" acknowledges current difficulty while keeping the door open to future capability.

---

## H

### Heuristics
**Definition:** Mental shortcuts or rules of thumb that allow quick, efficient decision-making under uncertainty. Adaptive in many contexts but can produce systematic biases when misapplied to situations requiring more deliberate analysis.

**Introduced in:** [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#System 1]], [[#Cognitive Biases]], [[#Availability Heuristic]], [[#Representativeness Heuristic]]

**Everyday example:** You meet someone at a party who reminds you of your funny, kind cousin. You immediately feel warmly toward them (representativeness heuristic: they resemble someone you like). This is adaptive for quick social bonding but can mislead—this person might actually be quite different from your cousin.

---

## I

### Intellectual Curiosity
**Definition:** The virtue of being motivated to acquire knowledge and understanding for their own sake, independent of external rewards. One of the core intellectual virtues that counters confirmation bias by motivating genuine exploration.

**Introduced in:** [[report-05-the-virtuous-reasoner|Report 05: The Virtuous Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Epistemic Curiosity]], [[#Intrinsic Motivation]], [[#Actively Open-Minded Thinking]], [[#Information Gap Theory]]

**Everyday example:** You overhear a conversation using a word you don't know. Intellectual curiosity is what makes you look it up later, not because it's on a test or will impress anyone, but because not knowing bugs you. The learning itself is the reward.

---

### Intellectual Humility
**Definition:** The virtue of being appropriately aware of the limits of one's knowledge and open to revising one's beliefs. Involves accurate self-assessment of when you know versus when you're confident without justification.

**Introduced in:** [[report-05-the-virtuous-reasoner|Report 05: The Virtuous Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Epistemic Humility]], [[#Fallibilism]], [[#Dunning-Kruger Effect]], [[#Metacognitive Accuracy]]

**Everyday example:** In a meeting, someone proposes an approach in an area you know a little about. Intellectual humility means accurately distinguishing "I understand the basics" from "I'm expert enough to evaluate this proposal"—and speaking accordingly rather than overreaching.

---

### Interoception
**Definition:** The perception of internal bodily states—sensing your heartbeat, breathing, hunger, tension, or gut feelings. Now understood as central to emotion construction, decision-making, and self-awareness, not just autonomic monitoring.

**Introduced in:** [[report-09-the-embodied-thinker|Report 09: The Embodied Thinker]], [[report-10-the-feeling-reasoner|Report 10: The Feeling Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Embodied Cognition]], [[#Somatic Markers]], [[#Emotion Construction]], [[#Bodily Awareness]]

**Everyday example:** Before an important conversation, you notice your stomach is tight and your breathing is shallow. That awareness of internal signals is interoception—and it provides the raw data your brain uses to construct the feeling you'll label as "nervousness" or "anticipation."

---

### Intrinsic Motivation
**Definition:** Motivation driven by internal rewards—interest, enjoyment, curiosity, or the satisfaction of mastery itself—rather than external pressures or incentives. Central to Self-Determination Theory and sustained learning.

**Introduced in:** [[report-03-the-motivated-mind|Report 03: The Motivated Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Self-Determination Theory]], [[#Autonomy]], [[#Flow State]], [[#Cognitive Vitality]]

**Everyday example:** A programmer stays up late working on a personal coding project, not because anyone asked or will pay them, but because solving the problem is inherently satisfying. That's intrinsic motivation—the activity itself is the reward.

---

## M

### Metacognition
**Definition:** "Thinking about thinking"—the awareness and regulation of one's own cognitive processes. Includes monitoring (knowing what you know and don't know) and control (directing attention, selecting strategies, self-testing).

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]], [[report-07-the-self-directed-learner|Report 07: The Self-Directed Learner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Metacognitive Monitoring]], [[#Self-Regulated Learning]], [[#Prosoche]], [[#Reflective Awareness]]

**Everyday example:** While reading a complex article, you realize you've been scanning words without comprehending. That awareness ("I'm not actually learning this") is metacognition—and the decision to slow down and re-read is metacognitive control.

---

### Motivated Reasoning
**Definition:** The tendency to unconsciously process information in ways that support preferred conclusions, rather than following evidence wherever it leads. Distinguished from deliberate rationalization by being genuinely non-conscious.

**Introduced in:** [[report-05-the-virtuous-reasoner|Report 05: The Virtuous Reasoner]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Confirmation Bias]], [[#System 1]], [[#Rationalization]], [[#Directional Motivation]]

**Everyday example:** You want a particular candidate to win an election. When negative news about them emerges, you instinctively scrutinize the source, look for alternative explanations, and feel skeptical. When negative news about their opponent emerges, you accept it more readily. You're not lying—you genuinely experience the first source as "less credible." That asymmetry is motivated reasoning.

---

## P

### Phronesis
**Definition:** (Greek: φρόνησις) Practical wisdom—the virtue of making good judgments in concrete situations requiring action under uncertainty. Distinguished from theoretical knowledge by its inherently contextual and action-oriented character.

**Introduced in:** [[report-11-the-practical-reasoner|Report 11: The Practical Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Practical Reasoning]], [[#Virtue Ethics]], [[#Situated Judgment]], [[#Tacit Knowledge]]

**Everyday example:** Your teenager asks to go to a party. The rules don't give you a clear answer—you have to weigh their maturity, the specific situation, precedent, trust, values, and consequences. That situated judgment, balancing multiple considerations without a formula, is phronesis.

---

### Prediction Error
**Definition:** (Predictive Processing) The mismatch between what the brain predicts will happen and what actually happens. The fundamental currency of the predictive brain—prediction errors drive learning, attention, and belief updating.

**Introduced in:** [[report-13-the-predictive-mind|Report 13: The Predictive Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Predictive Processing]], [[#Active Inference]], [[#Bayesian Brain]], [[#Felt Difficulty]]

**Everyday example:** You're about to sit on what you think is a sturdy chair. It wobbles unexpectedly. The jolt you feel is your brain registering a prediction error—your motor system predicted stability, reality delivered instability. That error signal immediately updates your predictions and triggers corrective action.

---

### Predictive Processing
**Definition:** The theoretical framework proposing that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine that generates models of the world and constantly tests them against incoming sensory data. Perception, action, learning, and emotion are all understood as facets of prediction-error minimization.

**Introduced in:** [[report-13-the-predictive-mind|Report 13: The Predictive Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Prediction Error]], [[#Active Inference]], [[#Free Energy Principle]], [[#Bayesian Brain]]

**Everyday example:** You don't passively receive sounds and then decode them into words—your brain *predicts* what you'll hear based on context, then checks incoming data against predictions. That's why you "hear" words that weren't actually spoken when someone mumbles—your predictions filled in the gaps.

---

### Prosoche
**Definition:** (Greek: προσοχή) Stoic term for disciplined attention—the cultivation of vigilant awareness of one's own thoughts, judgments, and reactions. The metacognitive practice of monitoring and regulating mental states to maintain alignment with values.

**Introduced in:** [[report-02-the-fallible-knower|Report 02: The Fallible Knower]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Metacognitive Monitoring]], [[#Mindfulness]], [[#Attentional Control]], [[#Stoicism]]

**Everyday example:** Someone cuts you off in traffic. Before reacting, you notice the surge of anger, recognize it as a judgment ("they're a terrible person!"), and choose not to follow that thought. That pause—that catching yourself—is prosoche in action.

---

## R

### Reflective Thinking
**Definition:** (Dewey) Thinking activated by a felt difficulty and directed toward resolving problematic situations through deliberate inquiry. Distinguished from passive reception of information or automatic habit by its experimental, exploratory character.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Felt Difficulty]], [[#Inquiry]], [[#Metacognition]], [[#Deliberate Thinking]]

**Everyday example:** Your car makes a strange noise. Reflective thinking begins: What kind of noise? When does it happen? What could cause that? Maybe the brakes? Let me test that hypothesis by paying attention next time I brake. That systematic exploration—triggered by the felt difficulty of the strange noise—is Deweyan reflective thinking.

---

## S

### Schema
**Definition:** (Cognitive Psychology) Organized patterns of thought or behavior that help us interpret the world. Mental frameworks built from experience that allow efficient processing of familiar situations but must be updated when reality contradicts them.

**Introduced in:** [[report-02-the-fallible-knower|Report 02: The Fallible Knower]], [[report-04-cognitive-scaffolding|Report 04: Cognitive Scaffolding]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Assimilation]], [[#Accommodation]], [[#Cognitive Load]], [[#Constructivism]]

**Everyday example:** Your schema for "doctor's appointment" includes check-in, wait, exam, prescription/advice. This schema helps you navigate medical visits efficiently. When you encounter a doctor who does home visits, your schema must accommodate the new information—updating your understanding of what "doctor's appointment" can include.

---

### Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
**Definition:** The motivational framework proposing that human flourishing requires satisfying three basic psychological needs: autonomy (experiencing choice and volition), competence (experiencing effectiveness), and relatedness (experiencing connection with others).

**Introduced in:** [[report-03-the-motivated-mind|Report 03: The Motivated Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Intrinsic Motivation]], [[#Autonomy]], [[#Competence]], [[#Relatedness]]

**Everyday example:** A workplace gives employees autonomy to choose projects (autonomy), provides challenging work matched to skills (competence), and fosters collaborative culture (relatedness). SDT predicts this combination produces not just productivity but genuine engagement and wellbeing.

---

### Self-Regulated Learning (SRL)
**Definition:** The process of taking control of one's own learning by setting goals, selecting strategies, monitoring progress, and adjusting approach based on feedback. Emphasizes the learner as active agent rather than passive recipient.

**Introduced in:** [[report-07-the-self-directed-learner|Report 07: The Self-Directed Learner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Metacognition]], [[#Self-Efficacy]], [[#Learning Strategies]], [[#Goal-Setting]]

**Everyday example:** You're learning a language. SRL means you don't just follow a course passively—you assess your weak areas (speaking vs. reading), set specific goals ("hold a 5-minute conversation"), choose strategies (language exchange vs. app), monitor whether they're working, and adjust when they're not.

---

### Somatic Markers
**Definition:** (Damasio) Bodily signals that become associated with particular decisions or situations, providing rapid emotional guidance. The "gut feeling" that steers us toward or away from options before conscious deliberation completes.

**Introduced in:** [[report-09-the-embodied-thinker|Report 09: The Embodied Thinker]], [[report-10-the-feeling-reasoner|Report 10: The Feeling Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Interoception]], [[#Embodied Cognition]], [[#Emotion]], [[#Intuition]]

**Everyday example:** You're considering accepting a job offer. The salary is good, the role fits your resume—but something feels "off." That vague bodily sense of unease is a somatic marker, possibly encoding pattern-matching from past experiences your conscious mind hasn't fully articulated.

---

### Socratic Method
**Definition:** The philosophical practice of eliciting knowledge through questioning rather than direct instruction, typically using the elenchus (cross-examination) to expose contradictions in beliefs and induce aporia (productive puzzlement).

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Elenchus]], [[#Aporia]], [[#Dialectic]], [[#Critical Thinking]]

**Everyday example:** Your child says "stealing is always wrong." You ask: "What if someone steals medicine to save a dying person?" They hesitate. "What if a runaway slave stole food?" Now they're genuinely puzzled—their absolute rule has revealed complexity they hadn't considered. That productive confusion is the Socratic method working.

---

### System 1
**Definition:** (Dual Process Theory) The fast, automatic, intuitive mode of thinking that operates effortlessly and below conscious awareness. Essential for navigating familiar situations efficiently but prone to systematic biases when misapplied.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Dual Process Theory]], [[#System 2]], [[#Heuristics]], [[#Automatic Processing]]

**Everyday example:** Someone says "2 + 2 = ?" and "four" appears in your mind instantly, effortlessly. That's System 1. Similarly, when you recognize a face, catch emotional tone in someone's voice, or complete familiar phrases, System 1 is operating—fast, automatic, and usually accurate enough.

---

### System 2
**Definition:** (Dual Process Theory) The slow, deliberate, analytical mode of thinking that requires conscious effort and attentional resources. Activated when System 1 encounters difficulty, conflict, or situations requiring careful reasoning.

**Introduced in:** [[report-01-the-inquiry-engine|Report 01: The Inquiry Engine]], [[report-06-taming-system-1|Report 06: Taming System 1]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Dual Process Theory]], [[#System 1]], [[#Deliberate Thinking]], [[#Working Memory]]

**Everyday example:** Someone says "17 × 24 = ?" Now you have to actually think—breaking the problem down, holding intermediate results in mind, checking your work. That effortful processing is System 2 in action.

---

## V

### Virtue Epistemology
**Definition:** The branch of epistemology that focuses on the intellectual character traits (intellectual virtues) that make someone a good thinker, rather than just on isolated beliefs or arguments. Treats excellent thinking as a cultivated practice.

**Introduced in:** [[report-05-the-virtuous-reasoner|Report 05: The Virtuous Reasoner]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Intellectual Virtues]], [[#Virtue Ethics]], [[#Character Development]], [[#Epistemic Virtues]]

**Everyday example:** Instead of just asking "Is this belief true?" virtue epistemology asks "Am I being intellectually honest? Curious? Humble? Thorough?" The focus shifts from evaluating products (beliefs) to cultivating the character traits that reliably produce good beliefs.

---

## W

### Working Memory
**Definition:** The cognitive system that temporarily holds and manipulates information during complex tasks. Limited in capacity (roughly 4-7 "chunks") and duration (seconds unless actively rehearsed). The primary bottleneck in human cognitive processing.

**Introduced in:** [[report-04-cognitive-scaffolding|Report 04: Cognitive Scaffolding]], [[report-13-the-predictive-mind|Report 13: The Predictive Mind]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Cognitive Load]], [[#Schema]], [[#Chunking]], [[#Attention]]

**Everyday example:** Someone gives you a phone number verbally: 555-2847. You can hold it in working memory for a few seconds to dial it, but if someone interrupts with "What's the address?" the number vanishes. That fragility and limited capacity is working memory in action—and why we need to chunk information and offload to external tools.

---

## Z

### Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)
**Definition:** (Vygotsky) The sweet spot between what a learner can do independently and what's beyond their current capability—the zone where learning happens most effectively, typically with guidance or collaboration. Too easy produces boredom; too hard produces frustration; ZPD produces growth.

**Introduced in:** [[report-04-cognitive-scaffolding|Report 04: Cognitive Scaffolding]]

**Related concepts:** [[#Scaffolding]], [[#Cognitive Load]], [[#Flow State]], [[#Optimal Challenge]]

**Everyday example:** A child learning to read knows letters and some simple words but can't tackle complex sentences alone. Reading together, with you helping with unfamiliar words while they handle the rest, places them in the ZPD—challenged enough to learn, supported enough to succeed.
