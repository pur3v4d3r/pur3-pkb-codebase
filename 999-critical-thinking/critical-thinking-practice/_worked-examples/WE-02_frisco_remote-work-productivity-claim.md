# WORKED EXAMPLE — FRISCO: "Remote work reduces overall worker productivity"

> [!abstract] How to Use This Worked Example
> This is a **completed reference session** showing the [[FRISCO Model]] applied to a typical pundit-style argument. FRISCO is for **rapid triage** — 5-10 minutes. Notice how each step is short, focused, and produces a defensible verdict without the full machinery of [[Paul-Elder Framework]]. Compare every section to the [[_master-frisco-quick-analysis-v1.0.0|template prompts]].
>
> **Object:** A short op-ed-style argument  ·  **Framework:** FRISCO  ·  **Verdict:** questionable
> **Session duration:** ~8 minutes

---

## 📄 The Argument Under Analysis

> "Remote work reduces overall worker productivity. A recent survey from a major management consultancy found that 65% of managers believe remote employees are less productive than in-office staff. When workers are home, they face constant distractions — children, household chores, social media — that simply don't exist in a structured office environment. Companies that have mandated return-to-office policies, like Amazon and JPMorgan, are clearly responding to this productivity problem. Therefore, the era of remote work should end, and employees should return to the office full-time."

**Source:** fictional composite of common 2024-2025 op-ed argumentation.

---

## F · Focus

> *What is the main question, conclusion, or issue?*

[**Focus**:: Remote work reduces overall worker productivity; therefore companies should end remote work and require full-time office return.]

> The argument has a **two-part structure**: a descriptive claim ("remote work reduces productivity") and a prescriptive conclusion ("therefore mandate return-to-office"). These must be evaluated separately — a true descriptive claim does not automatically license the prescriptive conclusion.

---

## R · Reasons

> *What reasons are offered in support?*

[**Reason-1**:: A survey shows 65% of managers believe remote workers are less productive.]
[**Reason-2**:: Home environments contain distractions (children, chores, social media) that offices lack.]
[**Reason-3**:: Major companies (Amazon, JPMorgan) have mandated return-to-office, which is treated as evidence the productivity problem is real.]

1. Survey: 65% of managers *believe* remote workers are less productive.
2. Home environments are distracting in ways offices are not.
3. Big-company return-to-office mandates are evidence of a real productivity problem.

---

## I · Inference

> *Is the reasoning from those reasons to the conclusion acceptable?*

**Inference type:** `inductive` — the argument generalizes from cited evidence to a population-level claim, then to a policy recommendation.

**Quality assessment:**
- [ ] Premises actually support the conclusion → **NO**
- [ ] No major [[Logical Fallacies|fallacies]] present → **NO**
- [ ] Strength of conclusion is appropriately hedged → **NO**

[**Inference-Verdict**:: The inference chain commits at least four identifiable errors: (1) [[Appeal to Authority]] disguised as data — manager *belief* is not productivity *measurement*; (2) [[Appeal to Popularity]] on corporate decisions, which conflates "what powerful actors do" with "what is true"; (3) [[Cherry Picking]] — Amazon and JPMorgan are named; companies that expanded remote work (e.g., Spotify, Atlassian) are not; (4) [[Is-Ought Fallacy]] — even if productivity dropped, the prescriptive conclusion requires further premises about how to weigh productivity against employee preference, retention, real-estate cost, etc.]

> The reasoning fails the inference test. Each reason commits a specific identifiable fallacy. The conclusion does not follow even if the premises are accepted.

---

## S · Situation

> *What is the context — physical, social, intellectual — that frames this argument?*

[**Context**:: Post-pandemic labor market (2024-2026) with active corporate-vs-employee tension over remote work; commercial real-estate holders have strong financial incentive to promote return-to-office narratives; consultancies that produced the cited surveys are themselves often paid by employers to design return-to-office policies — a conflict-of-interest red flag the argument does not acknowledge.]

> Situation matters: the **source ecology** of arguments like this one includes parties with direct financial interest in the conclusion. This is not ad hominem — it is a legitimate reason to demand higher evidentiary standards from the cited survey.

---

## C · Clarity

> *Are the meanings of key terms clear?*

**Unclear or contested terms:**
- **"Productivity"** → undefined. Are we measuring output per hour, output per employee, lines of code, customer satisfaction, revenue per FTE? Different measures produce different findings; the argument exploits this ambiguity.
- **"Overall"** → unmeasured. Averaged across what population? All industries? All job types? Knowledge work and call-center work have different remote-work effects.
- **"Less productive"** → no baseline. Less productive than *what counterfactual*? Pre-pandemic in-office? Same workers if forced in-office now? Other remote workers at other firms?

> Clarity failure here is **load-bearing for the whole argument**. The term "productivity" is doing all the rhetorical work while being defined nowhere.

---

## O · Overview

> *Step back: does the reasoning hold up under integrated review?*

**Verdict:** `questionable`

[**Final-Verdict**:: The argument is unsound. It substitutes manager beliefs for productivity measurements, commits at least four identifiable fallacies in its inference chain, exploits ambiguity in the term "productivity," ignores conflict-of-interest in its source ecology, and leaps from a contested descriptive claim to a strong prescriptive conclusion without the intermediate premises that move would require. A reasonable revised version of the underlying question would be: "For which job types, under which management practices, does remote work produce which measurable outcomes — and how should those outcomes be weighed against employee preference and retention?" That question is answerable; this argument does not answer it.]

> The verdict is **not** "remote work is good" or "remote work is bad." FRISCO evaluates the *argument*, not the *topic*. The argument fails. The underlying empirical question remains open.

---

## ⚙ Metacognitive Check (mandatory, 60 seconds)

- **What was I *inclined* to conclude before analyzing?**
  > I was inclined to dismiss the argument quickly because I'm sympathetic to remote work. That inclination is itself a [[Confirmation Bias]] risk — I might dismiss it for the wrong reasons (because I disagree with the conclusion) rather than the right reasons (because the inference is fallacious).

- **Did the analysis change my conclusion? Why or why not?**
  > It refined it. My initial reaction was "this is wrong"; the analysis showed *where* and *why* it is wrong, and forced me to separate the bad argument from the open empirical question. I was right that the argument fails, but my reasons for thinking so were initially worse than the reasons the analysis produced.

- **What [[Cognitive Biases|bias]] was I most at risk of in this session?**
  > [[Motivated Reasoning]] — I wanted the argument to fail. I countered this by being explicit in the inference section that the conclusion does not follow even *if* the premises are granted. That move is what distinguishes critique from rebuttal.

# 🔗 Related

## How This Example Demonstrates FRISCO

- **F:** Notice the focus section separated *descriptive* from *prescriptive* claim — a move that paid off in section O.
- **R:** Reasons were listed flatly, without yet evaluating them. Separating the listing from the evaluation prevents premature dismissal.
- **I:** This is where most of the work happens in a bad argument. Naming the specific fallacies (with [[wiki-links]]) makes the critique reusable.
- **S:** Source ecology — the most underused FRISCO move. Always ask: *who benefits if I accept this argument?*
- **C:** When a key term is undefined, the whole argument is suspect until it is defined.
- **O:** The overview is not just a summary — it generates the *better question* that the original argument was failing to ask.
