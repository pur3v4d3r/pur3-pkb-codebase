# PRACTICE PROBLEM (Hard) — Paul-Elder on a university AI-literacy policy proposal

> [!info] How to Use This Practice Problem
> Run the full [[_master-critical-thinking-deliberate-practice-v1.0.0|Paul-Elder template]]. This is a *hard* problem: it is a policy proposal (not a simple claim), spans multiple stakeholders, has empirical and normative components, and rewards strong work in sections 1.5 (Concepts), 1.6 (Assumptions), and 1.8 (Standpoint). Budget **~60 minutes**.

---

## 🎯 The Object of Analysis

> **Policy proposal:** "All universities should require every undergraduate, regardless of major, to complete at least one course in AI literacy (covering how generative AI models work, their limitations, ethical concerns, and effective prompting). Without this requirement, graduates will be unprepared for a labor market in which AI tools are ubiquitous, and the university will be derelict in its educational mission."

**Object type:** policy proposal
**Difficulty:** hard — multi-stakeholder, normative + empirical, contested core concepts ("AI literacy," "educational mission")
**Recommended framework:** [[Paul-Elder Framework]] (full)

## 🧭 Hints

> [!tip]- Hint 1 — Concept clarity
> "AI literacy" is currently used to mean at least four different things: (a) understanding how transformer models work technically; (b) prompt engineering skill; (c) ethical/social awareness of AI impact; (d) being able to identify AI-generated content. A required course can address one or two of these, not all four. The proposal's strength depends on *which* it means.

> [!tip]- Hint 2 — Curriculum displacement
> Every required credit *removes* a credit from somewhere else. A rigorous analysis must answer: which existing requirement is cut? Statistics? A second writing course? An elective slot? The answer changes who pays the cost.

> [!tip]- Hint 3 — Stakeholder standpoints
> At minimum: (1) the engineering student (who may already know this material), (2) the music student (who may benefit most but has the most-displaced curriculum), (3) the adjunct who would teach the course (precarious labor implications), (4) the university administration (cost, brand positioning), (5) employers (do they actually want this, or do they prefer just-in-time training?).

> [!tip]- Hint 4 — The standpoint trap
> If you find yourself agreeing or disagreeing with the proposal *before* doing the analysis, that is the strongest signal that you need to do the [[Steelmanning|steelman]] of the position you do **not** hold first. Write the strongest case for the side you reject, then evaluate.

## ✅ Solution Sketch (expand only after attempting)

> [!example]- Solution Sketch — Key Analytical Moves
>
> **Question reframe:**
> The proposal asks "should universities require X for all majors?" The decomposed questions are:
> 1. (Empirical) Will AI fluency materially affect labor-market outcomes for graduates across all majors?
> 2. (Definitional) What specifically does "AI literacy" mean? Which definition is the requirement enforcing?
> 3. (Comparative) Compared to alternative uses of the same credit, is a required AI-literacy course the best use?
> 4. (Pedagogical) Is a single course an effective delivery mechanism for the chosen definition?
> 5. (Equity) Who bears the cost of the new requirement? Who captures the benefit?
>
> A defensible analysis answers all five. Most pro/con takes answer only #1.
>
> **Information — disconfirming evidence:**
> - Single required general-education courses have **weak transfer** to real-world skill (existing literature on "great books" requirements, ethics-across-the-curriculum programs).
> - Tech literacy half-life is short — a course taken in year 1 may be technically obsolete by graduation.
> - Some labor-market segments (skilled trades, K-12 teaching, much of healthcare) currently show minimal AI displacement; universal application is empirically weaker than discipline-specific application.
> - The strongest comparable historical case — universal computing literacy in the 1990s — produced mixed outcomes; mandatory "computer literacy" courses are now widely considered to have underdelivered.
>
> **Load-bearing assumptions (the fragile ones):**
> - **That AI literacy is sufficiently general-purpose to justify a universal requirement** (alternative: it should be embedded in discipline-specific courses, taught differently for engineers vs. nurses vs. historians).
> - **That a single course is an effective delivery vehicle** (alternative: 4-year integration is more effective; one course is mostly signaling).
> - **That the displaced curriculum is less valuable** (alternative: the course-cut may be a foreign language, a writing course, a statistics course — all with stronger evidence of transfer).
> - **That universities are the right institution to provide this** (alternative: high schools, employers, MOOCs, professional certifications).
> - **That "graduates will be unprepared" is the actual risk** (alternative: graduates self-teach via tools and YouTube faster than universities can update curricula; the proposal solves a problem that markets are already solving).
>
> **Steelmanned opposing view (against the requirement):**
> "Universal requirements are blunt instruments. They impose a fixed cost on every student to address a problem that varies enormously by major. Embedding AI fluency in discipline-specific contexts — a CS course that teaches model internals, a journalism course that teaches verification under AI, a nursing course that teaches AI-assisted diagnosis triage — produces deeper, more transferable learning than a generic course. The proposal solves an administrator's problem (legible, brandable response) rather than a student's problem (actually-useful AI fluency in their actual field)."
>
> **Steelmanned supporting view:**
> "Required general-education courses *do* fail at deep transfer, but their function is establishing common literacy and signaling institutional priority. AI is now consequential enough — in ethics, in epistemics, in labor markets — that a baseline shared literacy across all graduates is itself the value, separate from any specific skill transfer. The historical comparison is not 1990s computer literacy but 1960s expansion of statistics requirements, which is widely judged successful even though many graduates retained little technical statistics."
>
> **Implications:**
> - Adoption: ~3 credits displaced per student (likely from electives or a foreign-language requirement).
> - Cost: large universities ~$2M-$5M/year additional faculty/adjunct cost.
> - Equity: students with less prior tech exposure benefit most; students with prior exposure pay a cost (their displaced credit) for content they already know.
> - Pedagogical risk: course content goes stale rapidly; ongoing curriculum cost is high.
>
> **Revised position:**
> "The strong version of the proposal (universal one-course requirement) is poorly supported by evidence on required-course effectiveness. A defensible weaker version is: (a) embed AI-fluency learning outcomes in **discipline-specific** required courses (analogous to writing-across-the-curriculum), with periodic faculty re-training; (b) offer an optional standalone AI-literacy course for students whose major has the lightest embedding; (c) measure outcomes against discipline-specific metrics, not generic completion. The original framing — 'require for all majors or be derelict in mission' — is a [[False Dichotomy]] that hides the better policy in the middle."
>
> **Expected rigor score:** 4.0-4.5 for a strong attempt; <3.8 if you skipped the stakeholder standpoint section.
