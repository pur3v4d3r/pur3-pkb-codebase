# Motivation, Agency & Self-Regulation — Map of Content

> [!abstract] Domain & Scope
> **Motivation, Agency & Self-Regulation** is the study of *why* people initiate, sustain, and direct their own learning — and what psychological conditions make autonomous, self-directed engagement more or less likely. This MOC organizes ~168 permanent notes spanning Self-Determination Theory (SDT), goal architecture, attribution theory, self-efficacy, metacognition, self-regulated learning (SRL), and self-directed learning (SDL). It is structured as a **hub-and-spoke architecture** centered on the concept of *self-determined agency* — the capacity to pursue goals from an internal locus of causality with full psychological engagement.
>
> **For**: Educators designing autonomy-supportive environments; coaches and practitioners; learners seeking to understand and optimize their own motivational psychology.
> **Companion MOCs**: [[MOC - Cognitive Architecture & Learning Science]], [[MOC - Reasoning, Critical Thinking & Epistemology]]
> **Reading time**: ~28 min full read; the SDT core (§1–§2) is the essential prerequisite for all other sections.

## §1 — The Need Architecture: SDT Foundations

[[self-determination-theory]] (SDT, Deci & Ryan) is the dominant macro-theory of human motivation in educational and psychological research. Its foundational claim is that human beings are *active organismic systems* with an intrinsic developmental tendency toward psychological growth, integration, and well-being — and that this tendency is nurtured or impeded by specific environmental and relational conditions. The [[the-active-organism-hypothesis]] expresses this most directly: SDT begins not from a blank-slate or drive-reduction model of the person but from the premise that the organism is inherently active, inherently inclined toward interest and growth.

The theory's structural core is [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]]: three universal psychological needs — [[autonomy-sdt]], [[competence-sdt]], and [[relatedness-sdt]] — must be satisfied for sustained well-being and autonomous motivation. [[the-three-basic-psychological-needs]] provides the consolidated account; [[basic-psychological-needs]] covers the theoretical grounding.

> [!definition] The Three Basic Psychological Needs
> - **[[autonomy-sdt]]** ([[autonomy-need]]): The need to experience one's behavior as self-authored and congruent with one's values — not merely *independence*, but *volition*. Autonomy is satisfied when choices are genuine, not coerced by external pressure or introjected guilt.
> - **[[competence-sdt]]** ([[competence-need]]): The need to feel effective in one's interactions with the environment — to experience mastery, growth, and the successful exercise of capacities. Competence need satisfaction is not about objective performance; it is about *perceived* effectance.
> - **[[relatedness-sdt]]** ([[relatedness-need]]): The need to feel meaningfully connected to others — to care and be cared for. Relatedness supports motivation indirectly: it creates the interpersonal safety within which autonomy and competence-supportive engagement can occur.

A critical asymmetry structures SDT's dynamics: [[need-frustration]] is not simply the absence of satisfaction — it is an *active pathogenic process* ([[need-frustration-as-an-active-pathogenic-process]]). Need frustration produces specific negative outcomes (controlled motivation, ego-involvement, psychological rigidity) that are not merely the mirror image of need satisfaction's positive outcomes. [[need-thwarting]] specifies the mechanisms by which environments actively block need satisfaction.

The [[the-universality-thesis]] claims that these three needs are pan-cultural and universal — not Western constructs or developmental luxuries. Cross-cultural research broadly supports this claim, though the *expression* and relative salience of each need varies across cultural contexts. [[basic-psychological-need]] and [[need-support]] round out the architecture of need-supportive environments.

## §2 — The Motivation Spectrum: Internalization

One of SDT's most important contributions is its disaggregation of "extrinsic motivation" into a *spectrum* of regulation types rather than a binary intrinsic/extrinsic classification. The [[internalization-continuum]] maps this spectrum from complete heteronomy to genuine autonomy.

[[intrinsic-motivation]] — engaging in an activity for its inherent interest and satisfaction — sits at the autonomous pole. It requires no external maintenance; it is self-sustaining precisely because it is not contingent on external outcomes. [[extrinsic-motivation]] in its most alienated form is [[external-regulation]]: behavior controlled entirely by external reward or punishment. Between these poles: [[identified-regulation]] (endorsing a behavior's value and choosing it as personally important) and [[integrated-regulation]] (full assimilation of a value into the self-concept) represent progressively more autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation. [[controlled-motivation]] vs. [[autonomous-motivation]] is the practically important contrast: both can drive behavior, but autonomous motivation is associated with deeper engagement, persistence, creativity, and well-being.

> [!key-claim] Internalization Is an Active Tendency, Not a Passive Process
> [[internalization]] is not something done *to* a person by a socializing agent. SDT frames it as an active *organismic-integration tendency* ([[organismic-integration-tendency]], [[organismic-integration-theory]]): the organism's inherent propensity to take in ambient social values and transform them into self-regulation. Whether this tendency succeeds depends on whether the social environment satisfies the basic needs — particularly autonomy and relatedness — during the internalization process.

The [[overjustification-effect]] provides a warning that is often misapplied. When [[extrinsic-motivation]] is introduced for a behavior that was already intrinsically motivated, the external reward can *undermine* intrinsic motivation by shifting the perceived locus of causality from internal to external. [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]] is the SDT sub-theory that explains this effect in terms of the informational vs. controlling functions of external events. [[the-replication-challenge-to-the-undermining-effect]] notes that the robustness of the overjustification finding has been contested; [[conditional-regard]] extends the concern to social contingencies that are controlling rather than informative.

[[autonomous-vs-controlled-commitment-integrating-sdt]] addresses the integration challenge: how do practitioners design environments that support internalization without manipulating it — scaffolding the process without substituting for the organism's active contribution?

## §3 — Goal Architecture

Goals are the proximal regulators of motivated behavior. While SDT describes the *quality* of motivation (autonomous vs. controlled), goal theories describe the *structure* of what people pursue and how they pursue it. Two frameworks dominate the vault: Locke & Latham's [[goal-setting-theory]] and the achievement goal frameworks.

[[goal-setting-theory]] establishes four goal properties that predict performance: specificity ([[goal-specificity]]), difficulty ([[goal-difficulty]]), commitment ([[goal-commitment]]), and — most importantly — the interaction between them. The [[high-performance-cycle]] model extends this to show how goal achievement feeds back into self-efficacy and satisfaction, creating motivational momentum. [[goal-intention]] vs. [[implementation-intention]] is a practically critical distinction: forming an intention to achieve a goal ([[goal-intention]]) is significantly less effective than specifying *when, where, and how* the goal-directed behavior will occur ([[implementation-intention]], [[implementation-intentions]]). The [[intention-behavior-gap]] — the well-documented failure to translate formed intentions into actual behavior — is largely bridged by the specificity of implementation intentions.

[[goal-orientation]] describes the motivational lens through which people approach achievement situations. The core distinction is between [[mastery-goal-orientation]] (focused on developing competence, learning, and improvement) and [[performance-goal-orientation]] (focused on demonstrating competence and outperforming others). [[mastery-vs-performance-goal-orientation]] provides the comparative account. The [[the-2-2-achievement-goal-framework]] refines this by adding an approach/avoidance dimension: [[mastery-approach-goal]] (pursue mastery), [[mastery-avoidance-goal]] (avoid losing mastery already gained), [[performance-approach-goal]] (pursue outperformance), [[performance-avoidance-goal]] (avoid looking incompetent). [[achievement-goal-theory]] houses the broader literature.

> [!tension] Mastery Goals vs. Performance Goals: Contextual Complexity
> The simple narrative — mastery goals good, performance goals bad — is empirically incomplete. Performance-approach goals predict higher short-term performance on many tasks and are not consistently associated with negative outcomes. [[mastery-climate-vs-performance-climate]] examines the classroom-level dynamics; [[mastery-climate]] and the TARGET framework ([[target-framework]]) describe how environmental structure shapes which goal orientation is adopted. The design implication: creating mastery-oriented environments reduces the conditions that generate performance-avoidance motivation, which is the most consistently harmful goal orientation.

[[goal-contents-theory]] (a SDT sub-theory) addresses *what* is pursued rather than *how*. Intrinsic goal content (growth, relationships, community) is systematically associated with greater well-being and autonomous motivation than extrinsic goal content (wealth, fame, image). [[task-value]] and [[expectancy-value-theory]] provide complementary accounts of what makes goals worth pursuing in educational contexts.

## §4 — Attribution Theory: The Causal Story

When an outcome occurs — success or failure — the explanation a person constructs *about that outcome* shapes their subsequent motivation more than the outcome itself. [[attribution-theory]] (Weiner) is the systematic account of this causal narrative process.

[[causal-attribution]] is the act of assigning a cause to an event. [[attribution]] covers the broader dispositional and situational factors involved. Weiner identified three dimensions along which attributions are classified: [[locus-of-causality]] (internal vs. external — did the cause reside in me or in the situation?), [[stability-dimension]] (stable vs. unstable — will this cause persist over time?), and [[controllability-dimension]] (controllable vs. uncontrollable — was this cause subject to volitional influence?).

> [!key-claim] The Stability Dimension Governs Expectancy; Locus Governs Affect
> The three dimensions predict different downstream effects. [[the-stability-dimension]] is the primary predictor of *expectancy change* — attributing failure to stable causes (low ability, high task difficulty) predicts future failure, creating a self-fulfilling cycle. [[the-locus-of-causality-dimension]] is the primary predictor of *emotional response* — internal attributions for failure produce shame and guilt; external attributions produce anger. [[the-controllability-dimension]] predicts whether the learner will try different strategies or feel helpless.

[[attribution-dependent-emotion]] maps the matrix of attributional outcomes onto specific emotional states. [[attributional-vocabulary]] notes that the precision of a learner's causal language shapes the quality of their attributional conclusions — imprecise causal language (e.g., "I just can't do math") conflates locus, stability, and controllability in ways that catastrophize.

[[attribution-retraining]] and [[the-two-stage-architecture-of-attribution-retraining]] describe the intervention logic: first, identify the maladaptive attribution pattern; second, replace it with more accurate and controllable attributions while providing mastery experiences that confirm the new causal story. [[the-attributional-bridge]] connects attribution theory to SDT: the [[perceived-locus-of-causality]] in SDT (autonomous vs. controlled regulation) maps closely onto Weiner's locus dimension, but with important differences in what "internal" means across the two theories.

[[implicit-theories-of-intelligence]] (Dweck) extends attribution theory into a more dispositional register. [[entity-theory-of-intelligence]] (intelligence is fixed) is functionally equivalent to making stable-internal attributions for failures — it generates learned helplessness responses and performance-avoidance goal adoption. [[incremental-theory-of-intelligence]] (intelligence is malleable) supports mastery goals and productive attribution patterns. [[growth-mindset]] and [[fixed-mindset]] are the popular-scientific formulations; [[conception-of-ability]] provides the developmental account of how children's theories of ability differentiate from effort over time.

## §5 — Self-Efficacy: Belief as Cognitive Engine

[[self-efficacy]] — defined in [[self-efficacy-theory]] (Bandura) as a person's belief in their capacity to perform a specific behavior or achieve a specific outcome — is arguably the single most robust predictor of motivated behavior in academic contexts. [[perceived-self-efficacy]] is the operational construct; its predictive power over actual behavior exceeds that of objective ability measures in many studies.

Bandura identified four sources of efficacy information: [[enactive-mastery-experience]] (successfully performing the target behavior — the strongest and most durable source), [[vicarious-experience]] (observing a similar-other succeed or fail), [[verbal-persuasion]] (being told one can succeed by a credible source), and [[physiological-and-affective-states]] (interpreting physiological arousal as a signal of capability or incapability). [[efficacy-hierarchy]] orders these sources by influence; [[efficacy-calibration]] addresses the accuracy question: well-calibrated efficacy — neither inflated nor deflated — produces more adaptive behavior than confident overestimation or chronic underestimation.

> [!key-claim] Efficacy Is Domain-Specific and Task-Specific
> [[self-efficacy-theory]] is explicit that efficacy is not a global trait. Efficacy for mathematics predicts mathematics behavior; it does not predict writing behavior. This specificity has design implications: interventions that provide mastery experiences in the *specific* target domain generate reliable efficacy change; generic encouragement or generic confidence-building does not. [[collective-efficacy]] extends the construct to groups and teams — the group's shared belief in their collective capacity is a distinct construct that predicts team performance independently of aggregate individual efficacy.

[[outcome-expectation]] is related to but distinct from self-efficacy. Efficacy asks "Can I perform this behavior?" Outcome expectations ask "If I perform it, what will happen?" Both must be positive for sustained motivated behavior; efficacy without positive outcome expectations produces frustration; outcome expectations without efficacy produce a kind of resigned awareness. [[social-cognitive-theory]] and [[triadic-reciprocal-determinism]] situate self-efficacy within a broader model where behavior, person, and environment are mutually determining.

[[the-centrality-of-efficacy-over-outcome-expectations]] makes the case that self-efficacy is the more proximally controlling variable for predicting behavior — a significant claim with design implications: interventions should prioritize building mastery experiences over altering contingency structures.

## §6 — Self-Regulated Learning: The Three-Phase Cycle

[[self-regulated-learning]] (SRL) is the process by which learners *actively participate in their own learning* — setting goals, selecting strategies, monitoring progress, and adjusting in response to outcomes. The [[cyclical-model-of-self-regulated-learning]] (Zimmerman) provides the canonical architecture: three recursive phases, each with distinct cognitive, motivational, and behavioral components.

**Forethought Phase.** [[forethought-phase]] is where SRL begins — before engagement. [[forethought-as-regulatory-front-loading]] articulates why this phase is load-bearing: learners who invest in planning activate relevant prior knowledge, set specific proximal goals, and select appropriate strategies before beginning, dramatically reducing the processing demands during execution. Task analysis involves assessing the demands of the learning task; self-motivational beliefs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, goal orientation, intrinsic interest) determine whether the learner will engage with full investment.

**Performance Phase.** [[performance-phase]] encompasses strategy deployment and self-monitoring during execution. [[control-phase]] is the moment-to-moment regulatory activity: when monitoring signals a gap between current state and goal, control mechanisms select and implement corrective responses. [[monitoring-control-coupling]] is the functional relationship between these two processes; [[monitoring-control-loop]] describes its dynamic structure. Self-control and self-instruction during task execution distinguish SRL from rote execution.

**Self-Reflection Phase.** [[self-reflection-phase]] is the post-performance reckoning: causal attributions for outcomes, self-evaluation against standards, and self-reactions (satisfaction, dissatisfaction, adaptive inferences). [[reaction-and-reflection-as-cyclic-coupling]] emphasizes that the reflection phase directly feeds the next forethought phase — making the cycle genuinely recursive, not merely sequential.

> [!key-claim] The Forethought Phase Has the Highest Leverage
> [[forethought-as-regulatory-front-loading]] argues — and evidence supports — that high-quality forethought is the strongest predictor of SRL effectiveness. Learners who set specific, proximal goals and select strategies deliberately before beginning outperform those who plunge in, even when the actual time-on-task is equal. The design implication: instructional supports that develop forethought competence (planning templates, strategy prompts, goal-setting structures) have disproportionate returns.

[[pintrich-s-framework-of-self-regulated-learning]] extends Zimmerman's model by specifying four areas of regulation (cognitive, motivational/affective, behavioral, contextual) rather than three, and by organizing them into a 4×4 matrix ([[the-four-areas-of-regulation]], [[the-four-by-four-matrix]]). [[cyclical-model-of-self-regulated-learning]] provides the core cycle; the Pintrich extension provides granularity.

## §7 — Metacognitive Governance

Metacognition is the regulatory architecture *within* self-regulated learning — the mind's capacity to monitor and control its own cognitive processes. While §6 describes the cycle of SRL, §7 describes the mechanism that makes that cycle adaptive rather than automatic.

[[metacognition]] (Flavell) encompasses two primary dimensions: [[metacognitive-knowledge]] (knowledge about cognition in general and about one's own cognitive characteristics) and [[metacognitive-regulation]] (control of cognitive processes). [[flavell-s-metacognitive-taxonomy]] organizes metacognitive knowledge into knowledge about persons, tasks, and strategies. [[metacognitive-experience]] covers the phenomenological layer: the felt sense of knowing ([[feeling-of-knowing]]), ease-of-learning judgments ([[ease-of-learning-judgment]]), and judgment-of-learning evaluations — all of which are inputs into monitoring.

[[metacognitive-monitoring]] is the ongoing assessment of cognitive state: Do I understand this? Am I making progress? Is this strategy working? [[metacognitive-control]] is the response: selecting a different strategy, allocating more time, seeking help. [[monitoring-control-loop]] is the closed-loop dynamic; [[monitoring-control-coupling]] describes how the quality of monitoring constrains the quality of control — you cannot make good strategic adjustments on the basis of inaccurate monitoring.

> [!key-claim] Calibration Is the Metacognitive Variable That Matters Most
> [[calibration]] — the alignment between confidence in one's performance and actual performance — is the single most important metacognitive measure in predicting learning outcomes. [[metacognitive-calibration]] describes the construct; [[calibration-vs-sensitivity-in-metacognitive-judgment]] distinguishes calibration accuracy from response sensitivity. The [[dunning-kruger-effect]] is a well-known calibration failure: novices in a domain systematically overestimate their performance because they lack the metacognitive competence to identify their own errors. The [[fluency-illusion]] is a related failure: smooth processing of material feels like understanding, even when comprehension is shallow.

[[metacognition-as-the-governor-of-strategy-deployment]] is a synthetic claim connecting metacognition to the broader SRL apparatus: strategy selection, application, and abandonment are all metacognitive control decisions. [[metacognitive-sovereignty]] is the developmental endpoint — the capacity to self-govern one's cognitive processes without external metacognitive scaffolding. [[externalized-metacognition]] describes the PKB connection: a well-designed personal knowledge base can serve as an external metacognitive scaffold, making monitoring outputs explicit and control decisions visible.

## §8 — Self-Directed Learning: Autonomy at Scale

[[self-directed-learning]] (SDL) is the macro-level expression of the SRL/metacognition framework: the capacity to take full responsibility for the planning, implementation, and evaluation of one's own learning across extended timescales and real-world contexts. While SRL describes *within-task* regulation, SDL describes *across-task* and *across-life* self-governance.

[[autonomy-as-the-motivational-foundation-of-sdl]] makes the SDT connection explicit: SDL is not merely a skill but a *motivationally sustained practice* requiring the continuous satisfaction of the autonomy need. [[garrison-s-comprehensive-model-of-self-directed-learning]] provides a three-dimensional model (self-management, self-monitoring, motivation) that maps well onto the SRL/SDT integration. [[self-directed-learning-readiness-scale]] assesses the dispositional component — readiness for SDL involves both competence in self-regulation and autonomous motivation to exercise it.

[[heutagogy]] takes SDL to its logical extension: not just self-direction within a structured educational context but learner-determined competency goals, learning pathways, and evaluation criteria. The [[sdl-srl-grain-size-continuum]] maps the progression: SRL operates at the task level, SDL at the course or project level, heutagogy at the life-design level.

> [!tension] Autonomy Support vs. Structure: The Dialectic
> [[autonomy-structure-dialectic]] names one of the most practically important tensions in motivational and educational design. Autonomy support does *not* mean absence of structure — it means providing structure in a way that preserves volition. [[autonomy-supportive-structure]] elaborates: clear expectations, rationale-provision, and choice within boundaries are all compatible with — and often necessary for — genuine autonomy support. [[controlling-teaching-styles]] describes the failure mode: structure delivered in a controlling, pressure-inducing way that thwarts autonomy even when the content is perfectly reasonable.

[[scaffolding-sovereignty-progression]] frames the developmental arc: learners begin needing heavy scaffolding and gradually — as metacognitive capacity and domain competence develop — assume greater self-directedness. [[assessment-for-autonomy]] and [[assessment-as-the-completion-of-the-scaffolding-sovereignty-progression]] describe how assessment can either support or undermine this arc. [[feedback-design-for-autonomy-and-mastery]] provides design principles for feedback that informs without controlling.

## 🌉 Cross-Domain Bridges

> [!related] Companion MOCs
> - [[MOC - Cognitive Architecture & Learning Science]] — The motivational and cognitive domains intersect at multiple load-bearing points. [[cognitive-load-theory]] determines whether the material is within the learner's capacity; self-efficacy determines whether they will attempt it; the SRL cycle monitors whether they are progressing; metacognition regulates strategy adjustment. The most important synthesis note bridging these domains is [[the-metacognitive-scaffolding-principle]].
> - [[MOC - Reasoning, Critical Thinking & Epistemology]] — [[motivated-reasoning]] sits at the intersection: when beliefs are held for emotional or identity-protective reasons rather than epistemic ones, reasoning becomes distorted. The autonomy need, when threatened, can drive defensive information processing. [[intellectual-humility]] and [[epistemic-vigilance]] are epistemic virtues that are also motivationally conditioned.

The PKB cluster bridges both MOCs: [[personal-knowledge-base]], [[the-pkb-as-constitutive-metacognitive-architecture]], and [[externalized-metacognition]] all argue that a well-designed vault is not just a knowledge repository but an *external motivational and metacognitive scaffold* — making progress visible, reducing monitoring load, and supporting the autonomy-satisfying experience of self-directed intellectual work.

## 🌅 Frontier & Open Questions

> [!frontier] Live debates within Motivation, Agency & Self-Regulation
> - **Autonomous vs. controlled commitment**: [[autonomous-vs-controlled-commitment-integrating-sdt]] identifies an underexplored question: is strong, sustained commitment better achieved through autonomous motivation or through controlled motivation with strong habits? The relationship between volitional motivation and habit formation ([[habit-formation]]) is not well specified in SDT.
> - **Need hierarchy**: SDT claims the three needs are co-equal, but [[the-motivational-ecology-thesis]] suggests their relative importance may vary situationally. Cross-cultural evidence on need universality is more nuanced than early SDT proponents acknowledged.
> - **Metacognitive bootstrapping**: [[the-metacognitive-bootstrapping-problem]] — metacognition requires monitoring cognitive processes that are partially opaque — remains unresolved. You need metacognitive skill to develop metacognitive skill, but you need skill to notice that the skill is absent.

> [!frontier] Gaps in this MOC's coverage
> - **Emotional regulation in motivation** — [[emotional-regulation]], [[academic-emotions]] — are structurally important but treated only tangentially here.
> - **Flow states** ([[flow]]) as an extreme case of intrinsic motivation with specific cognitive-load properties are underintegrated.
> - **Social motivational dynamics** — [[communities-of-practice]], [[collective-efficacy]], [[social-cognitive-theory]] in group contexts — are present in the vault but not fully developed in this MOC.
